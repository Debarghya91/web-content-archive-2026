What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain standby letter of credit is. A standby letter of credit is a written undertaking by a bank to pay a stated amount to a named beneficiary if the applicant fails to perform a stated obligation.

Why it matters. If the applicant defaults, the beneficiary can present a demand and the required documents, and the bank must pay.

Chapter objective: Learner explains what a standby letter of credit is, distinguishes it from a commercial letter of credit, and describes issuance, notification, drawing and expiry. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Standby letter of credit
  • Beneficiary presenting a demand and documents on applicant default
  • Contractor abandons
  • Separation: at fault in underlying
  • Expiry date
  • Applicant credit facility or collateral before standby issuance

Key terms

Documentary credit: A letter of credit, a bank undertaking to pay a seller when the seller presents documents that comply with the credit terms.

UCP 600: Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, revision 600, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs documentary credits.

ISP98: International Standby Practices 98, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs standby letters of credit.

Show transcript

A standby letter of credit is a written undertaking by a bank to pay a stated amount to a named beneficiary if the applicant fails to perform a stated obligation. The bank issues it at the request of its customer, who is called the applicant, and it remains in the background while the underlying deal proceeds normally. If the applicant pays on time, delivers the goods, or completes the work as agreed, the standby expires unused and the bank pays nothing. If the applicant defaults, the beneficiary can present a demand and the required documents, and the bank must pay. The word standby captures the essential idea. The bank does not pay in the normal course of business. It stands by, ready to pay only when a defined failure occurs. A construction company in Mumbai may ask its bank, State Bank of India, to issue a standby for 2 million dollars in favour of a project owner in Dubai. While the contractor completes the building on schedule, the standby sits unused. If the contractor abandons the site, the project owner presents a demand, and the bank pays up to the stated limit. A standby is independent from the underlying contract. That independence is a core principle of letter of credit law. The bank deals in documents, not in facts about whether the contractor really abandoned the site or whether the goods really were defective. If the documents presented match the terms of the standby, the bank pays, even if the applicant disputes the claim. This separation gives the beneficiary certainty, because payment does not depend on a court deciding who was at fault in the underlying deal. The amount in a standby is a maximum, not a fixed payment. A standby for 500,000 dollars does not mean the bank will pay 500,000 dollars on every drawing. The beneficiary may draw only the amount it claims is due, supported by the documents the standby requires. A supplier owed 120,000 euros for unpaid invoices may draw 120,000 euros under a 500,000 euro standby, leaving the balance available for later drawings if the standby permits partial drawings. The expiry date and the available balance therefore matter on every drawing. Banks charge a fee for issuing a standby, usually a percentage of the amount per year, plus handling charges for each drawing or amendment. A bank may charge 1.5 percent per year on a 1 million dollar standby, which is 15,000 dollars a year, and it may require cash collateral or a lien on the applicant assets. The fee reflects the risk that the bank will have to pay and then recover the money from its customer. The applicant must therefore have a credit facility or collateral in place before the bank agrees to issue the standby.

A standby letter of credit is a bank payment undertaking that is intended to be used only if the applicant fails to perform its obligation. It is a guarantee in the form of a letter of credit. The issuing bank promises the beneficiary that, if the applicant does not pay, does not perform, or does not deliver as agreed, the bank will pay the beneficiary on presentation of a demand and any documents the standby requires. The standby is a secondary undertaking, it stands behind the underlying obligation, and it is drawn upon only when the underlying obligation is not performed. That is the defining feature, and it is what separates a standby from a commercial letter of credit, which is intended to be used as the normal means of payment.

A standby letter of credit is used in many situations. A contractor gives a standby to a project owner as a performance guarantee, promising that the contractor will complete the work. A tenant gives a standby to a landlord as a security deposit, promising that the tenant will pay the rent. An importer gives a standby to an exporter as a payment guarantee, promising that the importer will pay for the goods. A borrower gives a standby to a lender as a financial guarantee, promising that the borrower will repay the loan. In each case the beneficiary holds the standby as security, and it draws on it only if the applicant defaults. The standby is therefore a risk management tool, not a payment tool, and it is priced as a guarantee rather than as a payment instrument.

The standby has an amount, an expiry date, and a set of drawing conditions. The amount is the maximum the bank will pay, for example USD 1,000,000. The expiry date is the last date a demand can be presented, for example 31 December 2026. The drawing conditions state what the beneficiary must present to draw, normally a written demand stating that the applicant has defaulted, and sometimes a copy of an unpaid invoice, a certificate of default, or a court order. The conditions should be simple and clear, because a complex drawing condition makes the standby hard to use and increases the risk of a discrepancy. A standby that requires a document the beneficiary cannot obtain is a standby that cannot be drawn, and it is worthless as security.

The standby is independent of the underlying contract. The bank pays on presentation of a complying demand, without investigating whether the applicant really defaulted, and without waiting for a court to decide. This is the independence principle, and it is the same principle that applies to a commercial letter of credit. The bank deals in documents, not in goods or in performance. If the demand complies with the terms of the standby, the bank pays. If the applicant believes the demand is wrongful, it must take legal action against the beneficiary separately, and it cannot stop the bank from paying. An applicant who gives a standby must understand this, because it means the beneficiary can draw the standby even if the applicant disputes the default.

The governing rules for a standby are either the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, UCP 600, or the International Standby Practices, ISP98. UCP 600 is the general rule set for letters of credit, and it applies to standby letters of credit when the standby states that it is subject to UCP 600. ISP98 is a rule set written specifically for standby letters of credit, and it is the preferred rule set for standbys in the United States and in many international transactions. The standby should state which rule set applies, and the bank, the applicant, and the beneficiary should all understand the differences. The differences are covered in the last lesson of this chapter, and they matter for the examination of the demand, the expiry, and the transfer.

A standby is normally issued by MT700, the SWIFT message for the issue of a documentary credit, or by MT707 when it amends an existing credit. The MT700 for a standby carries the same fields as for a commercial letter of credit, but the terms are different. Field 40A states the form of documentary credit, and for a standby it states STANDBY. Field 41a states the available with and by, normally the issuing bank, by payment. Field 42C states the draft at, normally not applicable for a standby, because a standby is drawn by demand rather than by draft. Field 46A states the documents required, which for a standby is normally a written demand and any supporting documents. Field 47A states the additional conditions, which for a standby may include the evergreen clause, the reduction clause, or the transfer condition.

An SME should remember that a standby letter of credit is a guarantee that looks like a letter of credit. It is issued by a bank, it has an amount and an expiry, it is drawn by presentation of documents, and it is governed by UCP 600 or ISP98. But its purpose is security, not payment, and it is drawn only on default. A business analyst who works on trade finance should ask, for any standby, who is the applicant, who is the beneficiary, what is the underlying obligation, what is the amount, what is the expiry, what documents are required for a drawing, and which rule set applies. Those seven questions define the standby, and they are the questions this chapter answers in detail.
