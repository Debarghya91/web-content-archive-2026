Why take this lesson

A standby letter of credit is a written undertaking by a bank to pay a stated amount to a named beneficiary if the applicant fails to perform a stated obligation. If the applicant defaults, the beneficiary can present a demand and the required documents, and the bank must pay.

Key terms

Documentary credit: A letter of credit, a bank undertaking to pay a seller when the seller presents documents that comply with the credit terms.

UCP 600: Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, revision 600, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs documentary credits.

ISP98: International Standby Practices 98, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs standby letters of credit.

Exercise

A standby for 500,000 dollars is drawn by a supplier owed 120,000 dollars for unpaid invoices. Work out how much the beneficiary can draw and what remains available.

Use the rule in this lesson that the standby amount is a maximum, not a fixed payment, and that partial drawings are possible if the standby permits them.

  1. What to do. State the amount the beneficiary draws, the amount left available, and what the expiry date and the available balance control on every drawing.

Correct result. The beneficiary draws only the 120,000 dollars it claims is due, supported by the documents the standby requires. The remaining 380,000 dollars stays available for later drawings if the standby permits partial drawings. The expiry date and the available balance matter on every drawing.

Why. A standby amount is a maximum, not a fixed payment. An analyst who treats it as a fixed payment will overstate what the bank owes on any single drawing.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A standby letter of credit is a written undertaking by a bank to pay a stated amount to a named beneficiary if the applicant fails to perform a stated obligation. The bank issues it at the request of its customer, who is called the applicant, and it remains in the background while the underlying deal proceeds normally. If the applicant pays on time, delivers the goods, or completes the work as agreed, the standby expires unused and the bank pays nothing. If the applicant defaults, the beneficiary can present a demand and the required documents, and the bank must pay.

The word standby captures the essential idea. The bank does not pay in the normal course of business. It stands by, ready to pay only when a defined failure occurs. A construction company in Mumbai may ask its bank, State Bank of India, to issue a standby for 2 million dollars in favour of a project owner in Dubai. While the contractor completes the building on schedule, the standby sits unused.

If the contractor abandons the site, the project owner presents a demand, and the bank pays up to the stated limit. A standby is independent from the underlying contract. That independence is a core principle of letter of credit law. The bank deals in documents, not in facts about whether the contractor really abandoned the site or whether the goods really were defective. If the documents presented match the terms of the standby, the bank pays, even if the applicant disputes the claim.

This separation gives the beneficiary certainty, because payment does not depend on a court deciding who was at fault in the underlying deal. The amount in a standby is a maximum, not a fixed payment. A standby for 500,000 dollars does not mean the bank will pay 500,000 dollars on every drawing. The beneficiary may draw only the amount it claims is due, supported by the documents the standby requires. A supplier owed 120,000 euros for unpaid invoices may draw 120,000 euros under a 500,000 euro standby, leaving the balance available for later drawings if the standby permits partial drawings.

The expiry date and the available balance therefore matter on every drawing. Banks charge a fee for issuing a standby, usually a percentage of the amount per year, plus handling charges for each drawing or amendment. A bank may charge 1.5 percent per year on a 1 million dollar standby, which is 15,000 dollars a year, and it may require cash collateral or a lien on the applicant assets. The fee reflects the risk that the bank will have to pay and then recover the money from its customer. The applicant must therefore have a credit facility or collateral in place before the bank agrees to issue the standby.
