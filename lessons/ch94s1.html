Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Correspondent banking is the plumbing of cross-border payments. Bank A in one country holds an account with Bank B in another country, in Bank B's currency. That account is called a nostro account from Bank A's point of view and a vostro account from Bank B's point of view. When a customer of Bank A pays a customer of Bank B, no money physically moves between the countries. Bank A debits its customer's account and debits its nostro account with Bank B, Bank B credits the beneficiary, and the two banks settle the position between themselves. SWIFT carries the messages, but SWIFT moves no money at all. Settlement happens entirely through these accounts, and a bank that has no correspondent in a currency cannot make payments in that currency. A typical payment hops through two or three banks. Take a UK importer paying a supplier in Kenya in US dollars. The UK bank debits the importer in sterling, converts to dollars, and sends an MT103 to its US correspondent. The US correspondent debits the UK bank's dollar nostro and sends the payment to the Kenyan bank's US correspondent. That bank credits the Kenyan bank's dollar nostro and forwards the instruction. The Kenyan bank converts the dollars to shillings and credits the supplier. Four banks, three nostro movements, and each hop adds time, a fee, and a screening check. The customer sees one payment; the network sees four separate book transfers. Smaller banks reach the network through nested accounts. A small bank in a Pacific island may have no direct relationship with a US clearing bank, so it holds its dollar account with a larger regional bank, which in turn holds its dollars with a US bank. The small bank's payments travel through the regional bank's account, and the US bank sees only the regional bank as its customer. This is called nested correspondent banking, and a payable-through account is the formal version of it, where the nested bank's customers can draw directly on the account. The risk is that the bank at the top of the chain cannot see the nested bank's customers, so it cannot screen them. Regulators have pushed banks to map their nested relationships and to demand the customer information behind them. The network has been shrinking for more than a decade. The number of active correspondent banking relationships worldwide has fallen by roughly a third since 2011, and the fall is steepest on corridors with low volumes and high perceived risk. The drivers are the cost of anti-money-laundering compliance, the capital a bank must hold against the risk, and the simple economics of a corridor that generates a few hundred payments a month. Each relationship needs due diligence, ongoing monitoring, sanctions screening and a dedicated nostro account. When the revenue from a corridor no longer covers that cost, the bank exits. The result is concentration: a handful of global banks now carry a large share of cross-border traffic. The cost of that shrinkage falls hardest on remittances and on small countries. A payment from the UK to a small island state may now route through three or four banks instead of two, because the direct correspondent is gone. Each bank in the chain deducts a fee of 15 to 35 dollars and adds a day of processing. A 200 dollar remittance can lose 60 dollars in fees and take four days to arrive, which is 30 percent of the value. That is why the G20 set a target of bringing the average cost of a retail cross-border payment down, and why regulators watch the decline in correspondent relationships as a financial inclusion issue, not just a banking issue. The responses are real but partial. Regional clearing arrangements let banks in a region settle among themselves without a US correspondent. Payment-versus-payment systems remove settlement risk between two currencies. Bilateral links between domestic instant systems, such as the Singapore to India link, bypass the correspondent chain entirely for small payments. Fintech aggregators pool the volume of many small banks into one correspondent relationship. None of these replaces the full network yet, because none of them reaches every country and every currency. For now the correspondent network remains the default, and the skill is in managing it well. For a business analyst, correspondent banking is a set of concrete requirements. Onboarding a new correspondent means a due diligence questionnaire, a risk rating, an account opening in the core banking system, a nostro account with a currency and a limit, cut-off times, a fee schedule, and a relationship management application, the RMA, that authorises SWIFT traffic between the two banks. The acceptance criteria must include a test payment through the new correspondent, tracked end to end, with the fees and the value date verified against the agreement. A corridor must not go live until that test has passed, because a live payment that fails in the middle of the chain is far harder to fix than a test payment.
