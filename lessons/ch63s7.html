What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain ATM cash withdrawals, the SEPA cards framework, clearing and settlement. A cash withdrawal at an ATM follows the same authorisation pattern as a purchase, but the merchant is replaced by the ATM owner.

Why it matters. For a business analyst, the framework matters because it defines the standards a new card product must meet, and for a product owner it matters because it opens the market to acquirers and processors from across Europe.

Chapter objective: Learner explains card types, the three-corner and four-corner models, and the full authorisation, clearing and settlement flow for a EUR 120 online purchase. Learner describes card anatomy, processor roles, fee sharing, ATM withdrawals, and the SEPA Cards Framework.

Key learning points

  • Ledger entries
  • Common standards

Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Exercise

  1. What to do. State what happens at each stage, who pays whom, and when the merchant is actually credited.

Correct result. Authorisation holds the funds and returns a response code. Clearing sends the transaction in a batch file. Settlement moves the net amount between banks, and the acquirer credits the merchant after deducting interchange, scheme fees and its margin.

Why. A card payment is not one event. The customer sees it on Monday, the merchant gets it on Wednesday, and the difference is the clearing and settlement cycle.

A cash withdrawal at an ATM follows the same authorisation pattern as a purchase, but the merchant is replaced by the ATM owner. The cardholder inserts a Meridian Bank card into an ATM owned by Harbour Bank Manchester and asks for EUR 60. The ATM sends an authorisation request through the scheme to Meridian Bank, which checks the card, the PIN and the available balance, and approves the withdrawal. The ATM then dispenses EUR 60 in cash, and Meridian Bank reserves EUR 60 plus any withdrawal fee on the cardholder account. The ATM owner earns a fee for providing the cash, and the cardholder may also pay a fee to the issuer, so the ledger must record the cash, the issuer fee and the ATM fee as separate entries. After authorisation, card transactions are collected into clearing files that the acquirer sends to the scheme, usually at the end of the business day. Clearing is the process of exchanging transaction records, matching them and calculating what each party owes the others. In bilateral clearing, each pair of banks settles directly with each other, so ten banks need many separate settlements. In multilateral clearing, all banks send their files to one central clearer, which nets everything down to one amount per bank, so each bank either pays or receives a single net amount. The EUR 120 purchase and the EUR 60 withdrawal both appear in the clearing files, and the scheme calculates what Meridian Bank owes Harbour Bank after the fees are taken into account. Settlement is the actual movement of money that follows clearing, and it happens between the banks settlement accounts, usually at the central bank. Once the net positions are calculated, Meridian Bank pays its net amount to the scheme settlement account, and the scheme pays Harbour Bank, so the merchant and the ATM owner are funded. Settlement is usually once a day, which is why a merchant who sells goods on Monday may not receive the money until Tuesday. The ledger entries are clear. The cardholder account is debited EUR 120 for the purchase and EUR 60 plus fees for the withdrawal, the merchant account is credited EUR 119.09, and the scheme and acquirer fee accounts receive their shares. If a clearing file is late or a settlement account lacks funds, the scheme applies its rules for delayed settlement and interest. The SEPA Cards Framework is the set of principles that aims to make card payments work the same way across the Single Euro Payments Area, so a card issued in one SEPA country works at any terminal or ATM in another without extra cost or friction. It requires that cards and terminals follow common standards, that a merchant can choose its acquirer in any SEPA country, and that the rules for clearing and settlement are transparent. Interoperability means a cardholder from France can use a card at a terminal in Germany, and a merchant in Spain can use an acquirer in the Netherlands, with the same flow and the same protections. For a business analyst, the framework matters because it defines the standards a new card product must meet, and for a product owner it matters because it opens the market to acquirers and processors from across Europe.
