Key terms

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Mandate: An authorisation given by a debtor to a creditor to collect payments from the debtor account by direct debit.

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Why. A supplier who is paid late by a bounced cheque will not accept a cheque again. The bounce path must be designed as carefully as the success path, because the reversal and the fees land on a customer who did nothing wrong.

A cheque bounces when the paying bank returns it unpaid, and the most common reason is insufficient funds, which means the drawer account does not hold enough money to cover the amount. Other reasons include a signature that does not match the mandate, a cheque that is out of date or post dated, a stop payment instruction from the drawer, a closed or frozen account, and an amount in words that does not match the amount in figures. A cheque can also bounce because the image is unreadable, the magnetic ink line is damaged, or the cheque has already been paid, which the bank detects as a duplicate. Each reason carries a return code, and the code tells the collecting bank and the payee exactly why the payment failed.

When a cheque bounces, the clearing system sends a return message to the collecting bank, and the collecting bank reverses the credit it gave the payee. If the supplier account was credited GBP 4,750 on Tuesday and the cheque is returned on Wednesday, the collecting bank debits GBP 4,750 from the supplier account, so the supplier loses the money it thought it had. Both banks usually charge a fee for a returned cheque, so the drawer may pay a return fee of GBP 10 to GBP 25, and the payee may pay a similar fee, which adds cost to an already failed payment. The supplier is told the cheque bounced, and the business must now pay again, either by a new cheque or by an electronic transfer, and the relationship is damaged.

The drawer can prevent a bounce by keeping enough funds in the account, by checking the signature and the amounts before issuing the cheque, and by telling the payee if a payment will be late. If a cheque does bounce, the drawer should contact the payee immediately, explain the reason, pay the amount by another method and cover any charges the payee suffered. A business that repeatedly issues cheques that bounce will lose its cheque book, because the bank can withdraw the facility, and suppliers will start demanding payment in advance. A product owner should consider whether the app warns the drawer when the balance is close to the cheque amount, because an early warning is cheaper than a bounce, a fee and a lost supplier.

Legal recourse depends on the country, but in most places a bounced cheque is a civil matter first and a criminal matter only when there is intent to defraud. In the United Kingdom, the payee can sue for the debt in the civil courts, and a cheque is strong evidence that the debt exists. In India, a bounced cheque can lead to criminal proceedings under the Negotiable Instruments Act when the drawer fails to pay after a legal notice, which makes the consequences far more serious. In the United States, a bad cheque can lead to civil action and, in some states, criminal charges for repeat offences. A business analyst must know the local law for the market being served, because the return codes, the notice periods and the customer letters all change with the jurisdiction, and the product must support the right process for each country.
