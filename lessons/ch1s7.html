Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Exercise

Read the MT103 message described in this lesson and identify its key parts.

Use the message structure, fields and example in this lesson.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

A business analyst who understands what a payment really is can ask better questions, write clearer requirements, and spot risks earlier. When a stakeholder says the payment failed, you can distinguish whether the instruction was rejected, the clearing failed, the settlement did not complete, or the beneficiary account could not be credited. Each of those failures has a different owner, a different fix, and a different message to the customer. Without this foundation, every failure looks the same, and the team wastes time chasing the wrong cause. This foundation also helps you read payment messages correctly. An MT103, a pacs.008, or a domestic clearing file all encode the same core ideas, which are debtor, creditor, amount, currency, value date, and remittance information. Once you understand the business meaning behind each field, the message structure becomes logical rather than mysterious. You can trace a payment from initiation to settlement, explain why a field is mandatory, and challenge a vendor or a developer who proposes to drop a field that the business actually needs for reconciliation or compliance. Finally, this foundation is the base for everything that follows in Payments Training Hub. The ecosystem chapter builds on the parties introduced here, the lifecycle chapter follows the journey from initiation to settlement, and the messaging chapters show how the same payment is expressed in MT and in ISO 20022. If you are comfortable with payment versus transfer, push versus pull, and payment versus settlement, you already have the mental model that senior payments professionals use every day. Keep returning to these distinctions whenever a new product or a new message type seems confusing. Consider a stakeholder who reports that a EUR 10,000 supplier payment has failed. There are at least four different failures hiding inside that one sentence, and each has a different owner. The payment may have been rejected at validation, for example because the beneficiary account number was wrong, in which case the bank returns a pacs.002 rejection with reason code AC01 and the payments team corrects the account details. It may have passed validation but missed the clearing cut off, in which case it waits for the next cycle and nobody needs to fix anything. It may have cleared but not settled, because the sending bank could not fund its net debit, which is a treasury problem. Or it may have settled but the beneficiary bank could not credit the account because it was closed, in which case the money comes back as a pacs.004 return. The customer message is different in each case, and so is the fix. An analyst turns this into working documents. First, a failure taxonomy in the business requirements document, one row per failure stage, naming the owner, the message or code that proves it, and the expected customer wording. Second, a runbook for the operations team, so the person on the night shift does not have to guess who to call at 2 in the morning. Third, a test pack with one scenario per failure stage, because a system that has only ever been tested on happy path payments will surprise everyone on the first bad day. The same foundation also helps in design reviews. If a developer proposes dropping the remittance information from a payment message to save effort, you can explain exactly which reconciliation process breaks and which customer complaint follows, and the field stays.
