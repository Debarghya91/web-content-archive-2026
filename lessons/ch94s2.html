Transcript and full text

Show transcript

SWIFT gpi, the global payments innovation, is a set of business rules layered on top of ordinary SWIFT messaging. It was launched in 2017 with a simple promise: cross-border payments should be fast, transparent and traceable. Before gpi, a cross-border payment disappeared into the correspondent chain. The customer had no idea where it was, how much had been deducted, or when it would arrive. The bank that received a query had to send an MT199 message and wait for a reply, which could take days. gpi changed the discipline without changing the underlying network: the same correspondent banks move the money, but now they must confirm what they did, and the customer can see it.

The heart of gpi is the UETR, the unique end-to-end transaction reference. It is a 36-character UUID, carried in field 121 of the MT103 and in the pacs.008 message under ISO 20022. The UETR is created by the ordering bank and stays with the payment for its whole life, through every intermediary, every format change and every investigation. Because it is unique, every bank in the chain can report against the same reference, and the tracker can assemble the full picture. A payment without a UETR cannot be tracked, so generating and persisting the UETR is the first requirement of any gpi implementation.

The tracker shows the payment's status at each bank. The statuses are standardised: ACSP means the payment has been accepted for settlement, ACSC means settlement is complete at that bank, ACCC means the beneficiary has been credited, and RJCT means the payment was rejected. Each bank sends a confirmation message, the MT199 or the pacs.002, as the payment passes through. A corporate treasurer in London can open the tracker at 09:00, see that a payment to Singapore was accepted by the intermediary at 09:12 and credited to the beneficiary at 09:47, and know exactly where the money is. The investigation of a missing payment now starts from the tracker, not from a phone call.

The gpi service level agreement is what makes the tracker meaningful. gpi banks commit to confirming the credit to the beneficiary within a defined window, and to making funds available on the same day for the majority of payments. The tracker exposes which bank is slow, because the timestamps are visible to every bank in the chain and to the customer. A bank that consistently confirms late is visible to its correspondents and to its customers, which creates a commercial pressure that no rulebook alone could create. The SLA does not guarantee same-day value in every case, and a payment that hits a sanctions screen or a cut-off will still be delayed, but the delay is now visible and explainable.

Fee transparency is the second big change. Before gpi, a 10,000 dollar payment could arrive as 9,850 dollars with no explanation of who deducted what. Under gpi rules, every bank that deducts a fee must report the amount deducted, and the ordering bank can see the full chain of deductions. Field 71A of the MT103 states who pays the charges: OUR means the ordering customer pays, BEN means the beneficiary pays, and SHA means the charges are shared. The tracker shows the actual deductions against that instruction. A corporate customer can now compare correspondents on the real cost of a payment, not just on the headline fee.

It is important to be clear about what gpi does not do. It does not move money faster by itself, because the money still travels through the same nostro accounts. It does not cover payments that never touch SWIFT, such as a payment between two branches of the same bank or a payment on a domestic instant system. It does not fix the underlying correspondent chain, and it does not remove the need for sanctions screening at each hop. What it does is impose discipline and visibility on a network that previously had neither, and that discipline is what customers actually feel.

For a business analyst, gpi enablement is a defined work package. The payment hub must generate the UETR at initiation and carry it through every message format and every status update. The tracker must be integrated, either through the SWIFT tracker or through a bank's own portal, and the statuses must be mapped to language the customer understands. Exception rules must fire when a confirmation is late, when a payment is rejected, or when the deducted fees exceed the expected amount. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment is sent and every status, from initiation to beneficiary credit, appears in the customer portal with the correct timestamps and the correct fee breakdown.
