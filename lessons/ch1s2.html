Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Cover payment: A payment method where the customer instruction travels one route while the funds travel separately as a bank to bank cover message.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

Show transcript

A transfer is a broader term than a payment. Every payment involves a transfer of value, but not every transfer is a payment. When you move money from your savings account to your own current account, that is a transfer, but it settles no obligation to another person. When a bank moves funds between its own internal accounts for liquidity management, that is also a transfer, not a payment to a customer. The word payment implies a payer, a payee, and an underlying reason for the movement, such as goods received, services delivered, or a debt that is due.

This distinction matters in payment systems because the rules, the messages, and the controls are different. A customer payment such as an MT103 or a pacs.008 carries details about the debtor and creditor, remittance information, and charges, because the receiving party needs to know why the money arrived and how to reconcile it. An internal transfer or a bank to bank cover payment such as an MT202 or a pacs.009 carries different information, because the business purpose is different. If you label a movement incorrectly, downstream systems may apply the wrong screening, the wrong accounting, or the wrong reconciliation logic.

In daily banking work you will also meet the phrase funds transfer, which is often used loosely to mean any electronic movement of money. A business analyst should be precise even when others are loose. Ask whether the movement settles a third party obligation, whether it is between accounts of the same legal owner, and whether it is part of a larger payment chain. The answers decide which message type is correct, which fields are mandatory, and which parties need to be notified. Precision here prevents misrouted payments, failed reconciliations, and audit findings later.

Take a concrete case. A manufacturer in Mumbai owes a German supplier EUR 120,000 for machinery, invoice INV-2025-0447. That is a payment. The manufacturer is the payer, the supplier is the payee, and the EUR 120,000 settles a real obligation. The pacs.008 that carries it names the debtor and creditor, the amount, the value date, and the invoice reference, because the supplier needs all of that to reconcile the receipt against the invoice. Contrast that with the same bank moving EUR 5 million between its own nostro accounts at the end of the day to balance its liquidity. Nobody owes anybody anything, no invoice is settled, and no customer is involved. That movement is a transfer, and it travels as a bank to bank message such as a pacs.009, which carries none of the customer remittance detail because there is no customer to inform.

Getting the label wrong causes real damage. Suppose the EUR 5 million internal movement is sent as a pacs.008 by mistake. The receiving bank treats it as a customer payment, tries to match it against an expected invoice, finds nothing, and raises a reconciliation break that takes a team half a day to clear. Worse, the customer screening rules run against it, and an internal account name that resembles a sanctioned entity can hold the movement for hours. A business analyst prevents this by writing the distinction into the requirements. A short decision table in the requirements states which message type follows from which business purpose, with one row for a customer payment and one row for an internal movement. The internal row states the rule plainly: the movement travels as a pacs.009 with no remittance information, because there is no customer to inform. Writing the rule that plainly is what stops the labelling mistake from reaching production.
