Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Show transcript

The International Bank Account Number, or IBAN, is the account identifier that makes SEPA possible, and every analyst who works on European payments must understand its structure. An IBAN is made of a two letter country code, two check digits, and a Basic Bank Account Number, known as the BBAN, which identifies the bank and the account within that country. The total length varies by country. A German IBAN has twenty two characters, a French IBAN has twenty seven, and a Maltese IBAN has thirty one.

The check digits are calculated with a standard algorithm called MOD 97, which catches most typing errors before the payment is even sent, because a mistyped IBAN will usually fail the check digit validation. The IBAN replaced the need to know a separate bank code and account number, which is how many countries addressed payments before SEPA. A German payment used to need a Bankleitzahl and a Kontonummer; a French payment used to need a code banque, a code guichet and a numero de compte. The IBAN combines all of that into a single string that is the same across the whole area, and it is printed on bank statements, invoices and salary slips across Europe.

A business analyst must design IBAN validation into every payment screen, check the country code, check the length for that country, run the MOD 97 check, and show a clear error if the IBAN is invalid, before the payment is submitted. Catching a bad IBAN at entry is far cheaper than handling a returned payment days later. The Business Identifier Code, or BIC, is the second identifier that appears in European payments, and it identifies the bank rather than the account. A BIC is eight or eleven characters long and is also known as a SWIFT code.

In the early days of SEPA, customers had to provide both the IBAN and the BIC, but the BIC is no longer required for SEPA payments inside the area, because the IBAN alone is enough for the receiving bank to be identified through central directories. The BIC still matters for payments outside SEPA and for some internal routing, so the analyst should keep it in the data model even when the customer does not have to type it. The combination of IBAN and BIC is also a good example of how identifiers evolve.

A system that once needed two identifiers now needs one, because the infrastructure behind it got smarter.
