Key terms

Multilateral netting: Netting across many participants in a clearing system, where each participant settles only its net position against the system.

Direct participant: A bank or institution that holds an account in a payment system and sends and receives payments directly.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

CHIPS is the private-sector large-value payment system for US dollars, owned and operated by The Clearing House. It is the system that most of the world's banks use when they need to move dollars across borders, and it handles around 1.8 trillion dollars on a typical business day. The difference between CHIPS and Fedwire is the difference between a private system and a central bank system. Fedwire settles each payment individually in central bank money. CHIPS settles payments from a prefunded balance, and it uses multilateral netting to let banks send far more in payments than they hold in prefunded funds. How the prefunding works. Each CHIPS participant holds a position in a prefunded balance account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. Before the CHIPS day begins, the participant transfers funds into that account. During the day, when the participant sends a CHIPS payment, its position is debited, and when it receives a payment, its position is credited. The payment is released only when the sending participant has enough in its position to cover it. If it does not, the payment waits in the CHIPS queue. At the end of the day, the remaining positions are returned to the participants. The money in the prefunded account is central bank money, but the payments between participants are settled by moving positions inside CHIPS, not by moving central bank money payment by payment. The queue and the optimisation. CHIPS holds payments that cannot yet be released, and it runs an optimisation that looks for offsetting payments. If Bank A owes Bank B 10 million dollars and Bank B owes Bank A 9 million dollars, the system can release both payments against a net movement of 1 million dollars. This multilateral netting is what makes CHIPS efficient. A participant that funds 500 million dollars in the morning can send several billion dollars of payments during the day, because incoming payments replenish its position continuously. The queue has priorities, and a bank can mark a payment as urgent, but urgency does not create funds. A bank that underfunds in the morning will see its payments sit in the queue while other banks' payments flow. Finality and law. A CHIPS payment, once released, is final under New York law, and the CHIPS rules are governed by Article 4A of the Uniform Commercial Code, the same law that governs Fedwire. The finality matters because CHIPS is used for time-critical payments, and the receiving bank credits its customer on the strength of the CHIPS release. There are around 40 direct CHIPS participants, and thousands of banks reach CHIPS indirectly through a correspondent that is a direct participant. A European bank that sends dollars to the United States almost always sends them through CHIPS, because its US correspondent is a CHIPS participant and the CHIPS route is cheaper and faster than holding a Fedwire position for every payment. What goes wrong. The classic CHIPS failure is a funding shortfall. A participant that expected a large incoming payment that did not arrive finds its position too low to release its own payments, and its queue grows. Other participants waiting for those payments see their own positions fall, and the gridlock spreads. CHIPS has a liquidity-saving mechanism that searches the queue for offsetting payments, and participants can add supplemental funding during the day, but there is a hard deadline. Payments still in the queue at the end of the CHIPS day are not settled. They are returned to the sender, and the sender must try again the next day or use Fedwire instead. A business analyst must write the requirement that the payment hub monitors the CHIPS position in real time and alerts the treasury team when the position falls below a threshold, because by the time the queue is visibly stuck, the damage is already spreading. Worked example. A bank in London needs to pay 12 million dollars to a supplier in New York. The London bank sends an MT103 to its US correspondent, which is a CHIPS participant. The correspondent validates the payment, screens it, and submits it to CHIPS. The correspondent's CHIPS position is 40 million dollars, so the 12 million dollar payment is released immediately. The receiving bank's position is credited, and it credits the supplier's account. The London bank's account with its correspondent is debited by 12 million dollars, and the correspondent's CHIPS position falls to 28 million dollars. Later in the day the correspondent receives 20 million dollars from another CHIPS participant, and its position rises to 48 million dollars. The correspondent funded 40 million dollars in the morning and moved 12 million out and 20 million in, and the netting meant it never needed to hold the full value of every payment it sent.
