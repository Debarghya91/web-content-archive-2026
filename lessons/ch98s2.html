Defining the Problem and the AI Use Case

Every AI feature should start from a business problem, not from the technology. That sounds obvious, but in practice teams often start the other way round. Someone says, we should use AI for this, and then they look for a problem to fit it. A product owner has to reverse that. Start with the pain, the cost, and the outcome you want, and only then ask whether AI is the right tool.

Take a payments operations team that spends three hours every morning sorting payment exceptions. About two hundred payments a day arrive with missing or mismatched references, and each one has to be checked by hand. The problem is clear. Staff time is wasted, payments are delayed, and customers call to chase a payment of, say, 12,500 pounds that has not arrived.

Now ask, what would fix this. A simple rule might be enough. If the reference is missing, route it to a queue with the sender details attached. A report might be enough. AI becomes the right choice when the pattern is too varied for a rule, and too large for a person to read through. For example, if the reference is written in ten different formats, and the correct match depends on the payer name, the amount, and the date together, a model that learns those patterns can help.

But you still need to prove it. Ask three questions before you approve the work. What is the problem in plain words. What does success look like in numbers. And what is the simplest thing that could solve it. If a rule or a report solves it, build that. Use AI only when it clearly does the job better, and when you can measure that difference.
