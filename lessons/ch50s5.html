What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain paying from multiple debit accounts. A corporation may hold an operating account, a tax account, and a payroll account at the same bank, and it may need to pay from more than one of them in a single run.

Why it matters. This matters because the message is the payment instruction. A wrong field, a missing element or a misunderstood format is a failed payment, a return, or a compliance breach.

Chapter objective: Learner explains MT101 use cases, multiple debit accounts, subsidiary payments, funds repatriation and the difference between Sequence A and Sequence B. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Paying from more than one account in a single run
  • Rule: simple to apply
  • Check for each debit account
  • Field 25: IBAN or domestic account number, matching the customer
  • Practical example

Key terms

Execution date: The date on which the bank executes a payment instruction, debiting the debtor account and sending the message onward.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

A corporation may hold an operating account, a tax account, and a payroll account at the same bank, and it may need to pay from more than one of them in a single run. MT101 supports this by allowing field 25, the account number, to appear in Sequence A for the message as a whole, and again in each Sequence B for that transaction. If field 25 appears in a Sequence B, it overrides the Sequence A account for that transaction only. The bank therefore debits the account named in the Sequence B, not the account named in Sequence A, for that payment. The rule is simple to apply in practice. Sequence A field 25 names the default debit account, which is used for any Sequence B that does not name its own account. A Sequence B that names a different account uses that account instead. A company called Northgate Engineering may send an MT101 with Sequence A field 25 naming its operating account GB15 NWBK 6000 1234 5678 90, and then carry three Sequence B blocks, two of which use the operating account by default, and one of which names its tax account GB15 NWBK 6000 1234 5678 91 for a tax payment of 95,000 pounds to HM Revenue and Customs. The bank checks funds separately for each debit account. It will not use the balance in one account to cover a shortfall in another, unless the customer has a sweeping or pooling agreement in place. A payment of 300,000 euros from an account that holds only 200,000 euros will be rejected or queued, even if another account in the same message holds 1 million euros. The treasurer must therefore check each account balance before sending the message, and must fund each account for the payments that will be debited from it on the execution date. Field 25 must be given in a format the bank can read, usually the IBAN or the domestic account number, and it must match an account held by the ordering customer. A message that names an account belonging to another company will be rejected, unless that company has given a mandate to debit its account. A corporation that pays from a subsidiary account must ensure that the subsidiary has authorised the debit, and that the bank holds that mandate on file. A missing mandate is a common reason for a payment from a second account to fail. A practical example shows the benefit. A hotel group with three accounts at Standard Chartered in Singapore sends one MT101 each Monday, with five Sequence B blocks. Two payments for food suppliers, 22,000 Singapore dollars and 18,500 Singapore dollars, are debited from the operating account. Two salary advances, 9,000 Singapore dollars each, are debited from the payroll account. One payment of 40,000 Singapore dollars for licence fees is debited from the tax reserve account. The bank executes all five payments from the three accounts, and the group treasurer reconciles each account from one MT940 statement, with each debit linked to its own transaction reference.

Paying from multiple debit accounts in one MT101 is the feature that makes the message powerful for a group treasury. A corporation holds accounts in several currencies, or several accounts in the same currency for different entities, and it wants to pay from each account in a single instruction. The MT101 supports this by allowing field 25, the account to be debited, to appear in Sequence B as well as in Sequence A. Field 25 in Sequence A names the default debit account for the message. Field 25 in Sequence B, when present, overrides the Sequence A account for that transaction only. The bank debits the account named in each Sequence B, and the corporation gets one message that draws on many accounts.

Consider a concrete example. Atlas Holdings has three accounts at Barclays: a pounds account GB29 BARC 2012 3456 7890 01, a euros account GB29 BARC 2012 3456 7890 02, and a dollars account GB29 BARC 2012 3456 7890 03. On 12 May 2026 Atlas sends one MT101 with three Sequence B blocks. Sequence B 1 pays 45,000 euros to a supplier in Berlin, and field 25 in that Sequence B names the euros account. Sequence B 2 pays 12,000 pounds to a consultant in Manchester, and field 25 names the pounds account. Sequence B 3 pays 30,000 dollars to a supplier in New York, and field 25 names the dollars account. Field 25 in Sequence A names the pounds account as the default, but each Sequence B overrides it with its own account. The bank debits three accounts, one per transaction.

The bank validates each debit account separately. It checks that the account exists, that the sender is authorised to debit it, and that the account has sufficient funds or an approved overdraft for that transaction. If the euros account has only 40,000 euros and the transaction is for 45,000 euros, the bank will reject that transaction, but it will still process the other two, unless the bilateral agreement says the whole message is rejected on any failure. The bank also checks that the currency of the debit account matches the currency of the transaction, or that a conversion is authorised. A euros transaction debited from a pounds account requires a foreign exchange conversion, and the bank will apply its rate and report the converted amount. The corporation should therefore match the debit account currency to the transaction currency wherever possible, to avoid unexpected conversion costs.

The reporting for a multi-account MT101 is per account and per transaction. The bank statement for the euros account will show a debit of 45,000 euros with the field 21 reference of Sequence B 1. The statement for the pounds account will show a debit of 12,000 pounds with the reference of Sequence B 2. The statement for the dollars account will show a debit of 30,000 dollars with the reference of Sequence B 3. The treasurer reconciles each account separately, using the field 21 reference to match the statement entry to the original instruction. A treasury system that cannot reconcile at the level of the individual debit account will struggle, because the three debits appear on three different statements.

A common error is to put field 25 in Sequence B when it is not needed. If all transactions are debited from the same account, field 25 should appear only in Sequence A, and the Sequence B blocks should omit it. Putting the same account in every Sequence B is harmless but adds noise, and it increases the risk of a typo in one block that causes a rejection. The opposite error is to omit field 25 in Sequence B when the transaction should be debited from a different account. The bank will then debit the Sequence A account, which may be the wrong account, and the corporation will discover the error only when it reconciles the statements. An SME who reviews an MT101 interface should check that the system makes the debit account explicit per transaction, and that it defaults to the Sequence A account only when the user has not chosen a different one.

The liquidity check is also per account. The bank checks the available balance in each debit account against the transactions drawn on that account, not against the total of the message. A message with 45,000 euros from the euros account and 12,000 pounds from the pounds account needs 45,000 euros in the euros account and 12,000 pounds in the pounds account. It does not need the equivalent of 57,000 in one currency. The treasurer must therefore ensure that each account is funded before sending the message, and the treasury system should show the balance per account alongside the transactions drawn on it. A payment run that fails because one account is short by a small amount is a common operational failure, and it is avoidable with a pre-send balance check.

For a business analyst, the multi-account requirement must state the override rule clearly. The requirement should say that field 25 in Sequence B, when present, overrides field 25 in Sequence A for that transaction, that the system validates each debit account and its balance before the message is sent, that the system reports the debit account per transaction in the status report, and that the reconciliation matches each statement entry to its transaction by the field 21 reference. A system that only supports a single debit account per message cannot serve a group treasury, and that limitation should be stated plainly in the gap analysis.
