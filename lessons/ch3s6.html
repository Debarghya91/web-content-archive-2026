What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain regulators, compliance and oversight. A regulator is a public authority that oversees payment systems, payment institutions, and banks to ensure safety, stability, fairness, and compliance with the law.

Why it matters. A business analyst must work closely with compliance teams and must understand the basics of anti money laundering, counter terrorist financing, sanctions screening, and fraud prevention.

Chapter objective: Learner maps the payment ecosystem, naming the roles of payer, payee, issuing bank, acquiring bank, clearing house, central bank, and network, and traces how value moves between them. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key learning points

  • Regulator: public authority
  • Compliance, afterthought in payments
  • BA working with compliance: AML, CTF, sanctions and fraud basics
  • Compliance stopping a payment
  • Not a technical defect
  • Stakeholder asking to remove a field or skip a check

Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Debtor: The party that owes money and makes a payment, such as a buyer, an employer or a borrower. In ISO 20022 messages the debtor is the ordering customer, with name, address and account details.

Scheme: A card or payment network that sets the rules, standards and fees for transactions between issuers and acquirers.

Exercise

Decide what the screening or monitoring outcome should be for the scenario in this lesson.

Use the lists, rules and thresholds in this lesson.

  1. What to do. State whether the payment is stopped, held, frozen or released, and what must be recorded for audit.

Correct result. A true match against a sanctions list stops the payment. A false positive is cleared with a reason recorded. An alert that is escalated becomes a case, and a case that meets the threshold becomes a suspicious activity report.

Why. Screening is a control, not a suggestion. A missed true match is a regulatory breach, and an unexplained clearance is an audit finding.

Show transcript

A regulator is a public authority that oversees payment systems, payment institutions, and banks to ensure safety, stability, fairness, and compliance with the law. Central banks often act as payment system overseers, while separate financial conduct authorities supervise the behaviour of banks and PSPs towards customers. Regulators set rules on capital, on safeguarding of customer funds, on anti money laundering, on sanctions screening, on fraud prevention, and on the transparency of charges and exchange rates. They can also impose fines, require remediation, and withdraw licences when rules are broken. Compliance is not an afterthought in payments, it is embedded in every stage of the lifecycle. Before a payment is sent, the debtor agent must screen the parties against sanctions lists and check for fraud indicators. During processing, the payment may be held for further review if it matches a sanctions name, if the amount is unusual, or if the destination is high risk. After processing, the payment data must be retained for audit, and suspicious activity must be reported to the relevant authority. These controls add friction and cost, but they are what keep the payment system trusted and lawful. A payment that bypasses compliance controls is not just a technical failure, it is a regulatory breach. A business analyst must work closely with compliance teams and must understand the basics of anti money laundering, counter terrorist financing, sanctions screening, and fraud prevention. You should know what data compliance needs, which fields are used for screening, and what happens when a payment is held. You should also understand the difference between a regulatory requirement, which is mandatory, and a scheme rule, which is contractual, and a bank policy, which is internal. When a stakeholder asks to remove a field or to speed up a payment by skipping a check, you need to know which authority would be breached and what the consequence would be. See compliance stop a payment. A bank sends USD 25,000 to a supplier, and the beneficiary name matches an entry on a sanctions list. The payment is held automatically before it leaves the bank, and a compliance analyst reviews it. After two days of checking, the analyst confirms that the supplier is a different person with a similar name, a false positive, and the payment is released. The supplier is paid two days late, the bank has spent two days of analyst time, and no rule was broken, but the delay is the price of the control. Now take the opposite failure. A payment is sent without screening because a new payment channel was launched before the screening feed was connected. That is not a technical defect, it is a regulatory breach, and it can bring a fine, a remediation order, and in serious cases a restriction on the bank's licence. The difference between the two cases is the difference between a control working and a control missing. A business analyst works with compliance as a design partner, not as a reviewer at the end. The requirements should state which fields are used for screening, name, address, and BIC at a minimum, what happens when a payment is held, who reviews it, and within what time. They should state the difference between a regulatory requirement, which is mandatory and cannot be negotiated, a scheme rule, which is contractual, and a bank policy, which is internal. When a stakeholder asks to remove a field or to skip a check to make a payment faster, the analyst should be able to name the authority that would be breached and the consequence, because that answer ends the discussion faster than any technical argument.
