Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Bank Operation Code: A code in field 23B of an MT103 that tells the receiving bank how to handle the transfer, such as CRED for a normal credit transfer, SPAY for a payment, or CRTS for a cheque related transfer.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

The MT102 is the SWIFT message that carries many customer credit transfers inside a single message. Where an MT103 instructs one payment to one beneficiary, an MT102 instructs a batch of payments to many beneficiaries, all sent from one bank to one receiving bank. The receiving bank splits the batch apart and credits each beneficiary separately, while the two banks settle only once, for the total of the batch. The MT102 is therefore a bulk instruction, and its value lies in replacing many messages and many settlements with one of each.

A bank uses an MT102 when it holds many small payments that share the same destination bank, the same currency, and the same value date. Payroll runs, pension payments, dividend distributions, and batches of remittances collected during the day are the classic cases, because each payment is too small to justify its own message and settlement. The bank collects the instructions, groups them, and sends one MT102, often as an evening batch after the working day. Use of the message is subject to an agreement between the sending and receiving banks, which settles the practical details such as the maximum batch size and the treatment of failed items, so a bank cannot simply start sending MT102 messages to a correspondent that has not agreed to receive them.

The structure of an MT102 has three sequences, each with a distinct job. Sequence A holds the batch level detail shared by every transaction in the message: field 20 gives the file reference and field 23 gives the bank operation code. Sequence B repeats once for every individual transaction, and each repeat carries its own related reference in field 21, its own amount in field 32B, the ordering customer in field 50, the beneficiary in field 59, remittance information in field 70, and the charges detail in field 71A. Sequence C carries the settlement detail for the batch as a whole, with the value date, currency, and total amount in field 32A, and field 19 can carry the sum of the individual amounts as a control total. The first check the receiving bank makes is that the total in field 32A reconciles with the sum of the amounts in field 32B, allowing for the charges treatment agreed between the banks.

The receiving bank treats the MT102 as one settlement and many payments. It validates the message as a whole, confirms that the totals reconcile, and then splits the batch into its individual transactions. Each transaction is checked and posted as if it were a separate customer payment, with its own beneficiary credit, its own remittance advice, and its own reconciliation record. The interbank settlement, by contrast, happens once, for the single total in field 32A, through the account the two banks hold with each other. Compared with sending the same payments as separate MT103 messages, the MT102 saves message charges, settlement entries, and reconciliation effort, at the cost of flexibility, because every transaction in the batch must share the same value date, currency, and receiving bank.

A failed transaction inside an MT102 does not normally stop the rest of the batch. The receiving bank processes the sound transactions and isolates the failed one, which may have a closed or incorrect beneficiary account, a name that does not match the account, or a compliance hold. The bank holds the amount for that transaction, tells the sending bank what went wrong, and returns the funds for that transaction alone, so the sending bank can correct the details and send the payment again as a fresh instruction. A format error in the common part of the message is different, because it can hold up the whole batch until it is repaired, which is why the sending bank validates the batch before release. There is also a straight through processing variant, MT102 STP, with stricter rules that reward clean data with fully automated handling.

A payroll example shows the economics at work. A bank groups 200 salary payments for employees whose accounts sit with the same receiving bank into one MT102, with a single settlement of EUR 540,000 and 200 separate beneficiary credits underneath it. One employee account has closed, so 199 salaries are credited on the value date and one amount is returned for correction, without disturbing the rest. New cross border flows now use ISO 20022, where a single pacs.008 can carry many transactions, so the MT102 is met mainly in domestic and legacy traffic and in records of past payments. An analyst who meets one should still be able to read it: find the batch total in Sequence C, count the transactions in Sequence B, and reconcile the two before investigating any single payment inside it.
