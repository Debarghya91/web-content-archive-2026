Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. State what happens at each stage, who pays whom, and when the merchant is actually credited.

Correct result. Authorisation holds the funds and returns a response code. Clearing sends the transaction in a batch file. Settlement moves the net amount between banks, and the acquirer credits the merchant after deducting interchange, scheme fees and its margin.

Why. A card payment is not one event. The customer sees it on Monday, the merchant gets it on Wednesday, and the difference is the clearing and settlement cycle.

Show transcript

A payment instrument is the tool or mechanism used to make a payment. Cash is the oldest instrument, and it settles immediately when notes and coins change hands, but it leaves no electronic trail and is hard to use at distance. A cheque is a written instruction from an account holder to a bank to pay a named person a stated amount, and it was once the dominant business instrument, though it is now slow and declining in most markets. A payment card, whether debit or credit, lets a payer instruct a payment at a point of sale or online, with the card scheme and the acquiring and issuing banks handling the clearing and settlement behind the scenes.

Electronic transfers and direct debits are the workhorses of modern business payments. A credit transfer is an instruction from the payer to push money to the payee, and it is used for salaries, supplier payments, and most cross border customer payments. A direct debit is the opposite, an instruction that lets the payee pull money from the payer account with prior authorisation, and it is used for utility bills, subscriptions, and loan repayments. E-money, mobile wallets, and instant payment systems are newer instruments built on the same ideas, but with faster settlement and richer data. Each instrument carries a different speed, cost, risk, and information profile.

A business analyst must know which instrument fits which use case. A high value, time critical supplier payment needs a real time or same day credit transfer, not a cheque. A recurring small bill fits a direct debit, not a manual transfer each month. A card payment fits a retail purchase, not a million pound interbank settlement. Choosing the wrong instrument creates delay, extra cost, or failed reconciliation. When you document a payment product, always name the instrument, its clearing path, its settlement timing, and its failure modes, because those details decide the customer experience and the operational risk.

Take a worked example. A customer buys a laptop for EUR 1,200 and pays by card at the shop, because a card fits a single retail purchase and settles quickly. The same week, a manufacturer pays a supplier EUR 40,000 by credit transfer, because a high value supplier payment needs a same day push with full remittance detail. And a household pays a EUR 60 monthly subscription by direct debit, because a small recurring bill fits a pull under a mandate. Three payments, three instruments, and each choice follows from the amount, the timing, and who initiates the movement. If the manufacturer had paid by cheque instead, the supplier would have waited days for clearing.

Worked example with real numbers: a corporate treasurer must pay three obligations on the same day, a EUR 250,000 supplier invoice, a EUR 1,800 monthly software subscription, and a EUR 45 customer refund. The subscription is paid by SEPA Direct Debit under a mandate, because a recurring small payment fits a pull, and the refund is paid by card reversal, because the original payment was by card and the scheme rules require the refund to follow the same route. If the treasurer had paid the supplier by direct debit, the supplier would have had to pull the money, which it cannot do without a mandate, and if the subscription had been paid by manual transfer, someone would have to remember it every month.

Failure and recovery: a credit transfer fails when the IBAN is wrong, and the return comes as a pacs.004 with reason code AC04 for a closed account or BE01 for an incorrect account number, and the payer corrects the IBAN and resends. A direct debit fails when the mandate is missing or the debtor disputes it, and the refund window under the SEPA Direct Debit scheme is 8 weeks for an authorised collection and 13 months for an unauthorised one, so the creditor must hold the mandate evidence. A card payment fails at authorisation, with an ISO 8583 decline, or at clearing, when the merchant does not capture, and the recovery is a new authorisation, not a pacs.004. Each instrument has its own failure message, and a generic failure playbook will miss it.

For a business analyst, product owner or subject matter expert, the instrument choice must be documented as a comparison, with one row per instrument and columns for money type, initiator, timing, cost, data, finality and failure. The comparison is the evidence that the choice was deliberate, and it is the first artefact a reviewer asks for. The ISO 20022 catalogue, the card scheme rules and the SEPA rulebooks are the sources for the message and timing columns, and they must be cited with version and effective date. A requirement that says payment, without naming the instrument, is incomplete, because the instrument decides the message, the clearing, the settlement and the reconciliation.
