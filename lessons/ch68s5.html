Key terms

Irrevocable: A payment or instruction that cannot be cancelled or reversed once it has been made, except by agreement of all parties or by a court order.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

T-account: A ledger representation of an account shaped like the letter T, with debits on the left and credits on the right.

Acceptance means the bank has checked the instruction and agreed to process it, and it happens before any money moves. The bank has validated the format, the account, the funds and the compliance checks, and it has told the customer the payment is accepted, usually through a status report. Acceptance does not move money, does not change any balance, and does not guarantee settlement, because a payment can still fail at the clearing system or at the correspondent. In the worked example, Meridian Bank accepts the USD 120,000 instruction from the London corporate at twelve minutes past nine in the morning, sends an acceptance report, and reserves USD 120,000 on the corporate account so the funds cannot be spent elsewhere. Testing acceptance means sending a valid instruction and confirming the acceptance report, and sending an invalid instruction and confirming a rejection instead.

A ledger entry records the payment in the bank books, and it is a debit and a credit that must always balance. When Meridian Bank accepts the USD 120,000 payment, it posts a debit of USD 120,000 to the corporate customer account and a credit of USD 120,000 to its internal settlement account, so the corporate balance falls by USD 120,000. The ledger entry is the record of the obligation, and it exists even before settlement, which is why the customer sees the money leave the account while the supplier has not yet been paid. If the payment later fails, the bank posts a reversing entry, a credit of USD 120,000 back to the corporate account and a debit of USD 120,000 to the settlement account. Testing ledger posting means checking every payment creates equal debits and credits, and checking a failed payment creates a reversing entry.

Settlement is the final movement of funds between banks, and it is the moment the payment becomes irrevocable. For the worked example, settlement happens when Meridian Bank pays USD 120,000 across its correspondent account at Harbour Bank, and Harbour Bank confirms the funds are received and available. Settlement can happen hours after acceptance and after ledger posting, because it depends on clearing cut offs, correspondent availability and the time zone of the receiving bank. The Tokyo supplier bank receives the funds only at settlement, and until that moment the payment can still be stopped, returned or reversed by the sending bank. Testing settlement means confirming the settlement account moves by exactly USD 120,000, confirming the confirmation arrives, and confirming the payment state changes to settled only when the confirmation is received.

Crediting the customer account is the last step, when the receiving bank posts the funds to the beneficiary account, and it happens only after settlement. In the worked example, the Tokyo supplier bank receives USD 120,000 at settlement, checks the beneficiary details, and credits USD 120,000 to the supplier account, less any charges the supplier agreed to bear. The supplier sees the money only at this point, even though the London corporate saw its account debited hours earlier, and that gap is normal in cross border payments. A T-account view makes the whole chain clear, with the corporate account debited USD 120,000, the Meridian settlement account credited then debited USD 120,000, the Harbour correspondent account credited then debited USD 120,000, and the supplier account credited USD 120,000. Testing the chain means tracing one payment through all four accounts and confirming each debit has a matching credit.
