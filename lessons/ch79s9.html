Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a $10,000 payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full amount. Under SHA the sender pays the sending charges and the beneficiary pays the receiving charges. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives the amount less all fees.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

Show transcript

Finding a payment among millions, by reference, amount, date, or party, in under a second, needs an index. Scanning every record for every query would take minutes. The data structure that makes search fast is the inverted index, and its building blocks are posting lists. Scaling the index across machines needs sharding. These three ideas, inverted indexes, posting lists, and sharding, are the internals of every search system, from the payment engine operations console to the web search engine, and understanding them helps an analyst specify, test, and troubleshoot search.

An inverted index reverses the natural order. The natural order maps each record to its terms: this payment has these references, these names, these amounts. The inverted index maps each term to the records containing it: this reference appears in these payments, this name appears in those payments. A search for a term looks up the index and gets the record identifiers directly, without scanning. The index is built once, when records are added or updated, and queried many times. The build cost is paid upfront, and the query cost is small. That trade, expensive build, cheap query, is what makes search fast.

The lists in the index are posting lists. For each term, the posting list is the sorted list of record identifiers, the postings, that contain the term. A posting may carry extra data: the position of the term in the record, or how often it appears, which supports phrase search and ranking. Query processing combines posting lists: an AND query intersects the lists, keeping only the identifiers in all of them; an OR query unions them. Skip lists let the intersection jump ahead instead of stepping through every entry, and compression keeps the lists small on disk and in memory. The efficiency of search is the efficiency of posting list operations.

Sharding splits the index across machines. In document based sharding, each shard holds the index for a subset of records: shard one holds January payments, shard two holds February, or the split is by hash of the record identifier. A query fans out to all shards, each shard returns its matching results, and a coordinator merges them, sorts them, and returns the top results. Sharding scales capacity, because each shard holds a fraction of the index, and throughput, because shards answer in parallel. Replication, holding a copy of each shard on another machine, provides availability: if a shard machine fails, its replica answers.

Payment search is mostly structured, and that shapes the design. Operations search by reference, amount, date range, status, and party: exact matches and range queries, where the index must support the field, the range, and the combination. Free text search over remittance information and narrative needs ranking: which results match best, using measures like term frequency and inverse document frequency, and filters and facets to narrow the results. The design must serve both: the structured query that must return the exact payment, and the text query that must return the most relevant payments. A search that returns the wrong payment, or no payment, when the payment exists, is an operations failure.

For a business analyst, search requirements cover what is indexed, how fresh the index is, and how fast queries must return. The indexed fields must match the queries operations actually run: a field that is searched but not indexed is a slow query. Freshness, the delay between a payment arriving and appearing in search, must be specified: real time for operations, near real time for reporting. And the latency target, the query time at a defined load, must be tested. Search is the operations console: a slow search means slow exception handling, slow investigations, and slow customer answers. Index design is a requirements decision, not an implementation detail.
