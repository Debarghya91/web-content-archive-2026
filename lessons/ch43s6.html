Correct result. A true match against a sanctions list stops the payment. A false positive is cleared with a reason recorded. An alert that is escalated becomes a case, and a case that meets the threshold becomes a suspicious activity report.

Why. Screening is a control, not a suggestion. A missed true match is a regulatory breach, and an unexplained clearance is an audit finding.

Fraud controls start before a payment is sent, with checks that look for risk in the instruction itself. A payment of 300,000 pounds to a newly added beneficiary, a payment to a high risk country, or a payment that breaks the normal pattern for that customer should trigger additional scrutiny. The system may hold the payment for review, ask for step up authentication, or require dual approval from a second user. A corporate customer that normally pays suppliers in the UK and suddenly sends 200,000 euros to an account in a different country should expect a challenge, because that change in behaviour is a classic sign of business email compromise.

Transaction monitoring continues after the payment is sent, by comparing each transaction with the customer profile and with known fraud patterns. A monitoring system might flag a customer who receives a large credit and then sends the full amount onward within an hour, a pattern known as money mule activity. It might flag a series of payments just below a reporting threshold, or a payment to an account that has been linked to previous fraud. When a rule fires, an alert goes to a fraud analyst, who reviews the account history, the beneficiary, and the payment message before deciding whether to block, release or investigate further.

Sanctions screening is a related control that must run on every payment, without exception. Before a pacs.008 leaves the bank, the beneficiary name, the remitter name and the payment details are checked against sanctions lists. A payment to a sanctioned person or entity must be blocked, and a payment with a possible match must be held for manual review. A bank that allows a payment to bypass screening, because of a system error or a manual override, risks a regulatory fine and reputational damage. Screening must also be tested regularly, with test names that should match and test names that should not match, to prove that the system still works.

When fraud is confirmed, incident response must start immediately, and the first hour matters most. The bank should stop further payments from the affected account, freeze the beneficiary account if possible, and start a recall for any payment that has already left, such as a 150,000 dollar payment sent to a fraudulent account. The bank must notify the customer, the receiving bank, and where required, the regulator and law enforcement. It must preserve logs, message copies and access records as evidence, and it must not delete or overwrite data that an investigation will need.

After an incident, the bank must learn from it and fix the control that failed. If the fraud succeeded because a password was stolen and MFA was not required, MFA must be enforced. If it succeeded because a beneficiary change was not verified, a cooling off period or a callback check must be added. A post incident review should record what happened, what worked, what failed, and what will change, with an owner and a date for each action. A bank that treats fraud as a one off event, and changes nothing, will face the same fraud again, often from the same attacker.
