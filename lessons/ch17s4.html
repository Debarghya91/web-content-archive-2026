Key terms

Multilateral netting: Netting across many participants in a clearing system, where each participant settles only its net position against the system.

Bilateral netting: Netting between two banks, where their mutual obligations are offset and only the net amount is settled.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. List the payments in the cycle, calculate the net position for each bank, and state what settles.

Correct result. Clearing validates, sorts, matches and nets the instructions, then produces a net position per bank. Only the net position settles, which needs far less liquidity than settling every payment individually.

Why. Netting is what makes high volume payments possible. Without it, every payment would need full liquidity at the moment it is sent.

Multilateral netting extends the same idea across many participants at once. Instead of calculating a separate net position for every pair of banks, the system calculates one single net position for each bank against all other participants combined. A bank adds up everything it owes to every other bank and subtracts everything it is owed by every other bank. The result is a single number, either a net debit or a net credit, that represents its position for the whole clearing cycle. Banks with a net debit pay that amount, and banks with a net credit receive it, usually through a central settlement account. A clearing house is the central entity that performs this calculation and manages the process. It receives payment files from all participants, validates them, sorts them by receiving bank, calculates the multilateral net positions, and tells each participant what it owes or is owed. The clearing house does not itself hold the settlement funds in most designs. It instructs the central bank or the settlement agent to move funds between participants based on the net positions it has calculated. This central coordination is what allows dozens of banks to settle a full day of payments with one payment each. Multilateral netting delivers a much larger liquidity saving than bilateral netting, because a payment a bank owes to one counterparty can be offset by a payment it is owed by a completely different counterparty. A bank that is a net payer to Bank B but a net receiver from Bank C may end up with a small overall net position, or even a net credit, even though its gross flows in each direction are large. This is why national retail payment systems and automated clearing houses almost always use multilateral netting. It is the most efficient way to handle high volumes of low value payments. Follow multilateral netting step by step. Each bank submits its payment files to the clearing house before the cut off. The clearing house validates the files, sorts the instructions by receiving bank, and checks for duplicates and errors. It then adds up, for each bank, everything that bank owes to every other bank, and everything it is owed by every other bank. The difference is a single net position per bank, either a net debit or a net credit. The clearing house tells each bank its net position and sends settlement instructions to the central bank. Banks with a net debit fund their settlement accounts, banks with a net credit receive funds, and the whole cycle settles with one payment per bank. The clearing house is the central coordinator, and its rules govern the whole process. It sets the file formats, the cut off times, the validation rules, and the error handling. It calculates the net positions and issues the settlement instructions. In most designs, the clearing house does not hold the settlement funds itself and does not guarantee settlement. That is different from a central counterparty, which steps into the middle of the trade, becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, and guarantees settlement even if a participant fails. A clearing house coordinates, while a central counterparty guarantees, and an analyst should not confuse the two. That is why national retail payment systems and automated clearing houses almost always use multilateral netting. The limits of multilateral netting are about concentration and dependence. Every participant depends on the clearing house to calculate correctly and on time, and on every other participant to pay its net debit. If one bank fails to fund its net debit, settlement can be delayed while the positions are recalculated without that bank, and the other banks may have to fund a larger amount than they expected. That is why clearing houses set membership criteria, require collateral or prefunding in some designs, and run default procedures that every participant agrees to in advance. An analyst working on a clearing system must understand those default procedures, because they decide what happens on the worst day, not on a normal day.
