Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Choosing a CSM is a commercial and operational decision that a bank revisits every few years, and it is usually made by a committee that includes payments operations, treasury, technology, and procurement. The decision is not about which CSM is best in the abstract. It is about which CSM fits the bank's volumes, its customer promises, its liquidity position, and its risk appetite. The first criterion is reach. The CSM must be able to deliver payments to every creditor bank the bank's customers pay. That sounds obvious, but reach is not binary. A CSM may reach a creditor bank directly, or indirectly through an interoperability link, and the indirect route may be slower or more expensive. The bank must check the reachability table for its top corridors, not just for the SEPA area as a whole. A bank whose customers pay heavily into France needs a CSM that reaches French banks quickly and reliably. The second criterion is cost, and the comparison must be done on total cost, not on the headline price. A CSM charges a membership fee, a per-transaction fee that usually falls with volume, a file fee, and charges for R-transactions such as returns and rejects. A bank sending 2 million SEPA credit transfers a month at 0.8 cents each pays 16,000 euro a month in transaction fees, plus a membership fee of perhaps 3,000 euro a month, plus file fees. A competing CSM might charge 1.0 cent per transaction but no membership fee, which is cheaper at low volume and more expensive at high volume. The crossover point must be calculated, and the calculation must include the cost of the settlement account, the liquidity cost, and the internal cost of the connection. The third criterion is cut-off times and cycles. A bank promises its customers a cut-off: payments received before 15:00 will reach the creditor the same day. To keep that promise the bank needs a CSM with a cycle after 15:00, and it needs time between its own customer cut-off and the CSM cut-off to validate, screen, and format the payments. A CSM with five cycles a day gives the bank more chances to meet the promise than a CSM with two. For direct debits the cycle pattern matters differently: the bank needs a CSM whose cycles align with the due dates in the SDD rulebook. The fourth criterion is liquidity. A net settlement CSM needs the bank to fund a net debit once or twice a day, which is manageable. A gross settlement CSM such as TIPS needs prefunded liquidity available 24 hours a day, including weekends, which ties up cash and needs a treasury process. A small bank without a central bank account needs a CSM that offers settlement through a settlement bank, and the terms of that arrangement matter as much as the CSM's own price. The fifth criterion is resilience. The bank must ask what happens when the CSM is down. Does it have a second CSM to fall back to, and does the payment hub support that fallback without duplicating payments? What is the CSM's own recovery time, and has it been tested? Under the Digital Operational Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, the CSM is a third-party ICT provider, and the bank must keep a register of it, assess the risk, hold contractual audit and exit rights, and maintain an exit plan that proves the bank can move its clearing to another provider. A CSM that will not agree to those contract terms cannot be used, however cheap it is. The sixth criterion is services beyond clearing. Some CSMs offer mandate management for direct debits, fraud screening, sanctions screening, or investigation support. A bank that buys those services from the CSM avoids building them itself, but it also becomes dependent on the CSM for them, and the exit plan must cover them too. The selection process itself follows a fixed pattern. The bank writes a request for proposal with its volumes, its corridors, its cut-off requirements, and its resilience requirements. It scores the responses, runs a proof of concept or a pilot with the leading candidate, and then runs a parallel period where the new CSM processes a share of the volume alongside the old one. The migration is done instrument by instrument, not all at once, and the old CSM is kept available as a fallback for at least one full cycle after the migration. A worked comparison shows the method. A bank sends 1.5 million SCT a month and 400,000 SDD a month. CSM X charges 0.7 cents per SCT and 1.2 cents per SDD, with a membership fee of 2,500 euro a month. CSM Y charges 0.9 cents per SCT and 1.0 cent per SDD, with no membership fee. CSM X costs 10,500 plus 4,800 plus 2,500, which is 17,800 euro a month. CSM Y costs 13,500 plus 4,000, which is 17,500 euro a month. The difference is 300 euro, which is noise. The decision will be made on cut-offs and resilience, not on price, and that is the normal outcome: at realistic volumes, the CSMs cost roughly the same, and the operational criteria decide. For a business analyst, the CSM selection is a requirements and testing exercise. The requirements must state the volumes, the corridors, the cut-offs, the settlement arrangements, and the resilience expectations. The acceptance criteria must include a test where the new CSM processes a full day of volume, a test where the primary CSM fails and the fallback takes over, and a test where the monthly invoice is reconciled against the bank's own count of submitted transactions.
