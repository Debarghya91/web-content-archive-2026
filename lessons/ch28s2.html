Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Purpose code: A code that states the commercial reason for a payment, such as supplier payment, salary or goods. Purpose codes are taken from an ISO external code list and support regulatory reporting, screening...

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Business process modelling turns a spoken description of how payments work into a diagram that everyone can read, challenge and agree on. The analyst always models the As-Is process first, which is how the payment flows today, including every manual step, every workaround and every delay. Only after the As-Is model is signed off does the analyst draw the To-Be model, which shows how the process should work after the project. Skipping the As-Is step is a common mistake, because a team that does not understand its current process cannot design a better one.

BPMN, which stands for Business Process Model and Notation, is the standard language for these diagrams. A rounded rectangle represents a task, a diamond represents a decision or gateway, a circle represents an event such as the start or end of a process, and arrows show the sequence of flow. For payments work, the analyst needs only a small subset of BPMN symbols, but must use them consistently so that a reader in operations, IT or audit interprets the diagram the same way. Every diagram carries a title, a version number and the name of the process owner.

Swimlane diagrams are the most useful format for payments, because a single payment crosses many teams. Each lane belongs to one team or system, such as the branch, the payment operations team, the sanctions screening system, the payment hub and the nostro agent bank. The payment moves from lane to lane as it is captured, validated, screened, released and settled. When the analyst lays the flow out this way, the handoffs become visible, and handoffs are exactly where payments get delayed, duplicated or lost.

Consider an As-Is cross-border payment of 125,000 US dollars from a corporate customer at a bank in Mumbai to a supplier in Germany. The branch captures the instruction on paper, a clerk keys it into the payment system, operations checks the balance and the purpose code, the message goes to sanctions screening, and if it clears, the payment hub sends an MT103 to the nostro bank. The whole journey takes six hours and involves three manual re-keying steps, each of which can introduce an error such as a wrong account number or a missing field 70 remittance line.

The To-Be model for the same payment removes the paper and the re-keying. The corporate customer submits the instruction through the online banking portal, the portal validates the IBAN and the purpose code before submission, the payment hub enriches the message automatically, sanctions screening runs in under two minutes, and the MT103 is released straight through. The target end-to-end time drops from six hours to under thirty minutes, and the only manual touch is an exception queue for payments that fail validation or hit a sanctions alert.

A good process model is validated with the people who do the work, not just with managers. The analyst walks the operations team through the As-Is diagram step by step and asks whether each task, decision and handoff matches reality. Invariably the team corrects details, such as a second approval that only applies above 1,000,000 rupees, or a manual check that happens every Friday. These corrections are added to the diagram before sign-off, because a model that does not match reality will produce requirements that do not match reality either.
