Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Field 52a identifies the ordering institution, the financial institution that ordered the payment on behalf of the ordering customer, when that institution is different from the sender of the MT103. For example, a regional bank in Lyon called Banque Rhone Alpes may hold no account with Banco Santander in Madrid, so it asks BNP Paribas in Paris to send the MT103 on its behalf. BNP Paribas is the sender, but Banque Rhone Alpes is the ordering institution, and field 52a must name it so the receiver and the beneficiary bank know where the instruction truly began.

Field 52a has three common formats. Option A, written as 52A, carries the BIC of the ordering institution, such as BRALFRPP for the Lyon bank. Option D, written as 52D, carries the full name and address when no BIC exists or when the BIC is not enough to identify the institution. Option F is rarely used in MT103 practice. The choice depends on whether the ordering institution has a BIC and whether the receiver needs a postal address for screening.

When a BIC is available, 52A is preferred because it is precise, machine readable and easy to screen. Field 52a appears whenever the sender is acting for another bank, and it must not appear when the sender itself took the instruction from the customer. If BNP Paribas receives an instruction directly from its own customer Atelier Lumiere SARL and sends the MT103 itself, BNP Paribas is both the ordering institution and the sender, so field 52a is omitted. Adding it in that case would be wrong, because it would suggest a second bank was involved.

Operations teams check this rule first when they validate a payment, because a wrong 52a can misdirect a compliance query. A concrete example makes the rule clear. A customer of Credit Agricole in Marseille instructs a payment of 85,000 euros to a supplier in Milan. Credit Agricole has no direct account with the Milan bank, so it sends the instruction to its correspondent Deutsche Bank in Frankfurt, which sends the MT103 to Milan. Deutsche Bank is the sender, Credit Agricole is the ordering institution in field 52A, and the Milan bank screens both names.

If Deutsche Bank omitted field 52a, the Milan bank would think Deutsche Bank itself had ordered the payment, and its sanctions screening would miss the true originator. Field 52a also protects the ordering institution in the chain. When a later bank in the serial chain forwards the payment, it keeps field 52a unchanged so the origin remains visible to every bank that handles the funds. That continuity matters for anti money laundering checks, for investigations, and for returns, because a return must go back to the institution that ordered the payment, not merely to the last sender.

A payment that loses its 52a in the middle of a chain becomes harder to trace, harder to return, and harder to explain to a regulator who asks where the money came from.
