What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain UK payment fraud and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. APP reimbursement is now a cost of doing business, and the controls that prevent it are a commercial priority.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the UK payment systems in depth, distinguishes BACS, CHAPS, Faster Payments and Open Banking payments, applies Confirmation of Payee outcomes, explains the Bank of England RTGS renewal, and applies the APP scam reimbursement rules to realistic UK payment scenarios with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Authorised push payment scam
  • Invoice scams: intercepted email, changed bank details, fraudster paid
  • Rules application to consumers
  • Exceptions: narrow
  • Receiving bank acting quickly on the recall, freezing funds
  • Recall process covered: message to the receiving bank, freezing funds

Key terms

Recall: A request by the sending bank to the receiving bank to return funds for a payment already sent, usually because of a duplicate, a technical error or fraud.

An authorised push payment scam, an APP scam, is a fraud in which the victim is tricked into authorising a payment to an account controlled by the fraudster. The victim believes they are paying a genuine payee, a solicitor, a builder, a supplier, or their bank, and they authorise the payment themselves. The bank's systems work correctly. The payment is genuine, the authentication is genuine, and the money moves. That is what makes APP fraud different from unauthorised fraud, where the fraudster takes the money without the victim's knowledge. In an APP scam the victim is the one who presses send, and the fraud is in the deception that led them to press it. The common scam types are worth knowing by name, because each one needs a different control. Impersonation scams are the largest category: the fraudster pretends to be the police, the bank, or a government department, and tells the victim their money is at risk and must be moved to a safe account. Invoice scams target businesses: the fraudster intercepts an email chain, changes the bank details on an invoice, and the business pays the fraudster instead of the supplier. Investment scams promise high returns and ask the victim to transfer money to a trading account that does not exist. Purchase scams advertise goods that do not exist, take the payment, and disappear. Romance scams build a relationship over months and then ask for money for an emergency. The reimbursement rules changed on 7 October 2024, when the Payment Systems Regulator made reimbursement mandatory for APP scams sent by Faster Payments or CHAPS. The sending payment service provider and the receiving payment service provider share the cost equally, 50 percent each. The maximum reimbursement is 85,000 pounds per claim. The reimbursement must be made within five business days of the claim, unless the sending provider needs more time to investigate, in which case it must tell the customer and can take up to 35 business days. The rules apply to consumers, micro-enterprises and small charities. The change is significant because before October 2024 reimbursement was voluntary under the Contingent Reimbursement Model code, and many victims received nothing. There are exceptions, and a payments SME must know them. The consumer standard of caution requires the victim to have taken reasonable care, and a victim who ignored a clear warning, such as a Confirmation of Payee no match warning or a fraud warning from their bank, may receive a reduced reimbursement or none. Gross negligence is the threshold: the victim must have been grossly negligent, not merely careless, for the provider to refuse. First-party fraud, where the customer makes a false claim, is excluded. Payments made to an account the customer controls, and payments made for an unlawful purpose, are excluded. Claims must be made within 13 months of the last payment in the scam. The exceptions are narrow, and the burden of proving them sits with the provider, not the customer. Confirmation of Payee is the main preventive control, and its role in reimbursement is direct. If the payer received a no match warning and proceeded anyway, the provider can argue the payer did not meet the consumer standard of caution. If the payer received no warning because the payee's bank does not support CoP, the provider cannot use that argument. The CoP log is therefore evidence in the reimbursement decision, and the bank must be able to produce it. Other controls include the fraud warning the bank shows before a first payment to a new payee, the cooling-off period some banks apply to a new payee, and the transaction monitoring that flags a payment that does not match the customer's normal pattern. What goes wrong in APP fraud handling is usually delay or poor evidence. A victim who reports the scam late gives the fraudster time to move the money onward, often within minutes, through mule accounts and onwards to crypto exchanges or overseas accounts. The receiving bank must act on the recall request quickly, and must freeze the funds if they are still in the account. A sending bank that takes 35 business days to investigate must keep the customer informed, and must not use the time to avoid paying. The most difficult cases are those where the victim was warned repeatedly and proceeded, because the provider must then decide whether the victim was grossly negligent, which is a judgement call that the Financial Ombudsman Service will review if the customer complains. For a business analyst, APP fraud requirements are about the warning, the claim, and the evidence. The acceptance criteria must state the warning the customer sees before a first payment to a new payee, the CoP outcome that is shown, and the claim journey the customer follows after a scam. A test must prove that a claim is paid within five business days when the evidence is clear, and that the 50-50 split between the sending and receiving providers is calculated correctly. The requirements must cover the recall process, including the message sent to the receiving bank and the freezing of funds. The design must log every warning shown to the customer, every CoP result, and every step of the claim, because the log is the evidence the Ombudsman will ask for. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: Payment Systems Regulator policy statement on APP scam reimbursement, PS24/11; the Contingent Reimbursement Model code; Financial Ombudsman Service decisions on APP fraud; UK Finance fraud data and typology reports.
