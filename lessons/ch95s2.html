Show transcript

Four lists dominate sanctions screening, and a bank must screen against all of them. The United States OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list contains more than 10,000 entries and is enforced with strict liability, which means a bank can be penalised even if it did not intend to breach. The European Union consolidated list implements the Common Foreign and Security Policy decisions and is enforced by member states. The United Nations Security Council lists cover the Al-Qaida and ISIL regimes, the Taliban, and country-specific regimes, and every member state must implement them. The United Kingdom consolidated list is maintained by the Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation and implements the UK autonomous regime alongside the UN lists. Each list is structured differently: the OFAC list carries aliases, dates of birth, nationalities, and identity document numbers; the EU list carries similar fields in a different format; and the bank screening engine must normalise all four into a single format before it can match against them. The OFAC 50 Percent Rule is the detail that catches firms out, and it needs a worked example to understand. Under the rule, an entity is blocked if blocked persons own 50 percent or more of it in aggregate, even if no single blocked person owns 50 percent. Take HoldCo, owned 30 percent by blocked person X and 25 percent by blocked person Y. Neither owns 50 percent alone, but together they own 55 percent, so HoldCo is blocked. Now take the indirect case. Blocked person X owns 50 percent of Company A. Company A owns 50 percent of Company B. X therefore indirectly owns 25 percent of Company B. If another blocked person owns 30 percent of Company B directly, the aggregate is 55 percent and Company B is blocked. The bank must map ownership chains through holding companies, and must recalculate when ownership changes. A corporate customer that was clean at onboarding can become blocked when a sanctioned person buys a stake, and the bank must catch that through ongoing monitoring, not at the next periodic review. Screening the debtor, the payer, is the first check in the payment flow. The engine screens the ordering customer name, the account number, and the address. In a SWIFT MT103 the ordering customer is field 50a. In a pacs.008 the debtor is the Dbtr block, with the name, the postal address, and the account. A partial match on the debtor name generates an alert, and the analyst checks the secondary identifiers: the date of birth, the nationality, and the identity document. The debtor check matters because the payer is the bank own customer, and the bank is expected to know them. A debtor hit that is a true match means the bank own customer is sanctioned, which is a serious finding and triggers a wider review of the relationship. Screening the creditor, the beneficiary, is the check that most often blocks a payment. In an MT103 the beneficiary is field 59a. In a pacs.008 the creditor is the Cdtr block. The creditor is the person who will receive the money, so a true match means the bank would be sending funds to a sanctioned person, which is the core breach the regime exists to prevent. The creditor check must also cover the creditor account: an account that was previously flagged, an account in a high-risk jurisdiction, or an account whose name does not match the beneficiary name. A mismatch between the beneficiary name and the account name is itself a red flag, and it must be investigated before the payment is released. Screening the intermediary banks is the check that is most often forgotten and most often causes a problem. In an MT103 the intermediary institution is field 56a and the account with institution is field 57a. In a pacs.008 the intermediaries appear in the intermediary agent blocks. Every bank in the chain must be screened, because a payment can be blocked if an intermediary is sanctioned, even when the payer and the payee are clean. The correspondent bank that clears the payment also screens it, and if the correspondent finds a hit that the sending bank missed, the payment is blocked at the correspondent and the sending bank is asked to explain. A bank that does not screen intermediaries will learn about its gap from its correspondent, which is the worst way to learn. Screening the payment message fields is the deepest check, because the fields carry free text. In an MT103, field 70 carries the remittance information and field 72 carries sender to receiver information. In a pacs.008, the remittance information and the purpose codes carry the same content. The engine screens these fields for sanctioned ports, sanctioned vessels, sanctioned goods descriptions, and the names of sanctioned individuals. A payment whose remittance field says freight to a sanctioned port is a sanctions problem even when the payer and the payee are clean. The tension is with data minimisation: the more free text the payment carries, the more the screening engine must read, and the more personal data travels to every bank in the chain. The practical control is to limit free-text fields at initiation and to warn the customer that the reference will be visible to other banks. For a business analyst, the sanctions screening work is a field-by-field requirements exercise. The engine must screen the debtor, the creditor, every intermediary, and the message fields, against all four lists, with a documented threshold per field. The 50 Percent Rule calculation must be automated, with ownership data from the customer record and from the beneficial ownership register. The audit trail must record which fields were screened, which lists were used, the list version, and the score for each match. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a debtor hit is caught, a test where a creditor hit blocks the payment, a test where an intermediary hit is caught, a test where a sanctioned port in field 70 generates an alert, and a test where the 50 Percent Rule calculation is correct for an aggregated ownership case. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
