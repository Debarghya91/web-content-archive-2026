Key terms

Multilateral netting: Netting across many participants in a clearing system, where each participant settles only its net position against the system.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

CLS, originally known as Continuous Linked Settlement, is a global system that provides payment versus payment settlement for foreign exchange trades in eighteen major currencies. It is operated by CLS Bank, which is based in New York and regulated by the Federal Reserve, with oversight from the central banks of the currencies it settles. Member banks submit their foreign exchange instructions to CLS, fund their net positions in each currency, and CLS settles both legs of each trade simultaneously on its own books. If a bank does not fund its position, its trades do not settle, and no other bank loses its principal.

CLS works on a multilateral netting basis within each currency. For each settlement date and each currency, CLS calculates each member's net pay in or pay out, and members fund only the net amount. This keeps the liquidity requirement manageable, even though the gross value of trades settled through CLS runs into trillions of dollars each day. Settlement takes place during a defined window, typically in the early morning European time, when the payment systems for the relevant currencies are all open. This window is carefully chosen to overlap the operating hours of the central banks involved.

The impact of CLS has been profound. Before CLS, banks faced Herstatt risk on every foreign exchange trade, and they managed it with bilateral limits that constrained trading. After CLS, principal risk on covered trades was effectively eliminated, which allowed the foreign exchange market to grow far larger and more liquid. CLS does not cover every currency or every type of trade, and it does not eliminate replacement cost risk or liquidity risk, but it is the single most important piece of infrastructure for managing foreign exchange settlement risk. Any business analyst working on cross border payments should understand how CLS fits into the overall flow.

Take a EUR/USD trade of EUR 10 million against USD 10.8 million, agreed on Monday for settlement on Wednesday. On Wednesday morning, both banks submit their instructions to CLS. CLS calculates each member's net pay-in for each currency. The euro seller must pay in EUR 10 million and the dollar seller must pay in USD 10.8 million, but because both banks have many trades that day, their net pay-ins are much smaller, perhaps EUR 2 million and USD 3 million. The banks fund those net amounts into CLS between 07:00 and 09:00 Central European Time, CLS checks that both sides are funded, and then settles both legs simultaneously on its own books, so neither bank is exposed to the other for even a second.

If a member fails to pay in, CLS holds the trades and the other members are protected. The short member has until a defined deadline to fund, and if it still cannot, CLS uses its in/out swaps to exchange the currencies it needs, so that the funded members still settle on time. The failing member faces a liquidity shortfall, a penalty, and a report to its regulator, but the other members lose nothing on principal. The risk that remains is liquidity risk: a bank that expected to receive dollars at 09:00 and does not receive them until the afternoon must fund its own payments in the meantime, which is why CLS members keep intraday credit lines ready on settlement days.

A BA working on foreign exchange settlement should know the CLS timetable by heart, because it drives the nostro funding schedule. Instructions must be submitted by the CLS cut-off, pay-ins must be funded before the settlement window, and any trade that misses the cut-off settles outside CLS, which means it carries principal risk again. Requirements cover the submission format, the pay-in schedule, the reconciliation of CLS statements against the bank's own records, and the exception process when a trade is held. The BA should also know which currencies and which trade types CLS covers, because a trade in a currency CLS does not settle, or a trade type it does not cover, needs a different risk control, usually a bilateral limit.
