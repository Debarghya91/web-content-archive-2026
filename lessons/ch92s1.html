Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Irrevocable: A payment or instruction that cannot be cancelled or reversed once it has been made, except by agreement of all parties or by a court order.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Fedwire is the Federal Reserve's real-time gross settlement system, and it is the backbone of large-value dollar payments in the United States. It is owned and operated by the Federal Reserve Banks, and it settles each payment individually, in real time, in central bank money. That last phrase matters more than any other fact about Fedwire. Central bank money is money held in an account at the Federal Reserve, and a payment settled in central bank money carries no credit risk between the sending and receiving banks, because the Federal Reserve itself moves the funds. There is no netting, no end-of-day batch, and no waiting for another payment to arrive. When a Fedwire payment is sent, the sending bank's master account at the Federal Reserve is debited and the receiving bank's master account is credited, and at that moment the payment is final and irrevocable. The scale is hard to overstate. Fedwire processes roughly 900,000 payments on a typical business day, with a total value of around 4 trillion dollars. The average payment is therefore several million dollars, and that tells you what Fedwire is for. It is not a consumer system. It is the system a corporate treasurer uses to move 45 million dollars to a supplier, the system a bank uses to settle a securities trade, and the system the US Treasury uses to move government funds. A bank that wants to send a Fedwire payment must hold a master account at its regional Federal Reserve Bank, and it must be able to send and receive Fedwire messages through the Fedwire network. Fedwire operates for about 22 hours each business day, opening at 9.00 pm Eastern Time on the evening before the business day and closing at 7.00 pm Eastern Time. That long window exists because dollar payments do not keep office hours. A bank in Asia that needs to settle a dollar obligation can send a Fedwire payment in the early morning in its own time zone, and a bank in California can still send one in the late afternoon. The long window also creates the liquidity problem that defines Fedwire operations, because a bank can send payments all day but its incoming payments arrive unevenly, and the gap between the two is funded by daylight overdraft. A daylight overdraft is an intraday loan from the Federal Reserve. When a bank sends a Fedwire payment before it has received enough incoming payments to cover it, its master account goes negative during the day, and the Federal Reserve allows that negative balance up to a limit called the net debit cap. The cap is set from the bank's capital, and the bank's board must approve it. The Federal Reserve charges a fee on daylight overdrafts, currently 50 basis points annualised on the average overdraft, with a small weekly waiver. A bank that runs a large average overdraft pays real money, so the treasury team manages the daylight position actively, holding payments until incoming funds arrive, or borrowing in the federal funds market, or pledging collateral to the Federal Reserve to secure a higher cap. A bank that exceeds its cap can have its payments rejected, and a rejection in the middle of a time-critical settlement chain causes real damage. The message format matters because it is where most Fedwire failures begin. Fedwire historically used its own proprietary message format, and in March 2025 it completed a migration to ISO 20022, so Fedwire payments now travel as pacs.008 and pacs.009 messages, the same family of messages used by SEPA and by CHIPS. The migration forced every Fedwire participant to rebuild its payment engine, its screening, and its reconciliation, because the new messages carry structured addresses, richer remittance data, and stricter validation. A payment with a malformed message, a missing mandatory field, or a beneficiary account that does not exist is rejected by the Federal Reserve, and the sending bank must fix it and resend. There is no partial credit and no second chance inside the same message. The most important operational fact about Fedwire is finality. Once the Federal Reserve has debited the sender and credited the receiver, the payment cannot be recalled through the system. If the sending bank made an error, if it sent 4.5 million dollars instead of 450,000 dollars, or if it sent the payment to the wrong bank, the only remedy is to ask the receiving bank to send the money back as a new payment, and the receiving bank is under no obligation to do so immediately. That is why the pre-send controls on a Fedwire payment are heavier than on any other US payment type. The payment is validated against the Fedwire directory, the beneficiary bank's routing number is checked, the amount is checked against the customer's limit and against the bank's own daylight position, and a second person approves it. A business analyst writing requirements for a Fedwire flow must treat the approval step as mandatory, not optional, and must include a test where a payment above the approval limit cannot be released by one person alone. Worked example. A corporate treasurer needs to pay 45 million dollars to a supplier at 2.00 pm Eastern Time. The bank's payment hub validates the supplier's routing number against the Fedwire directory, screens the payment against the sanctions lists, and checks the customer's available balance and the bank's daylight position. The payment is approved by two people, and at 2.04 pm the hub sends the pacs.009 to the Federal Reserve. The Federal Reserve debits the sending bank's master account by 45 million dollars and credits the receiving bank's master account by the same amount. At 2.05 pm the receiving bank credits the supplier's account. The whole movement took one minute, and the ledger entries are simple. The sending bank's master account goes down by 45 million, the receiving bank's master account goes up by 45 million, and the supplier's account goes up by 45 million. If the sending bank had not had enough in its master account, the payment would have drawn a daylight overdraft, and the treasury team would have seen the overdraft on its monitor within seconds. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: Federal Reserve Financial Services, Fedwire Funds Service operating circular; Federal Reserve Policy on Payment System Risk; ISO 20022 pacs.009 usage guidance for Fedwire.
