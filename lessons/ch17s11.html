Correct result. Clearing validates, sorts, matches and nets the instructions, then produces a net position per bank. Only the net position settles, which needs far less liquidity than settling every payment individually.

Why. Netting is what makes high volume payments possible. Without it, every payment would need full liquidity at the moment it is sent.

Show transcript

Netting and clearing systems let participants exchange payments through the day and settle the net amounts later. The efficiency of netting creates exposure: between the moment a payment is accepted and the moment the net settles, each participant is exposed to the others. Limits cap that exposure. Two kinds of limit work together: bilateral limits, which cap the exposure between two participants, and multilateral limits, which cap a participant total exposure to all others combined. Understanding both, and how they interact, is essential to understanding how netting systems stay safe.

A bilateral limit caps the net debit position between two participants. Participant A sets a limit on its net exposure to participant B: the maximum net amount A is willing to owe B at any point during the day. Every payment A sends to B increases the net debit; every payment B sends to A reduces it. When a new payment would take the net debit beyond the limit, the payment does not process: it queues, waiting for an incoming payment to reduce the position, or it is rejected, depending on the system rules. The bilateral limit is a credit decision expressed as a number: how much daylight A allows between itself and B.

A multilateral limit caps a participant total net debit to the system as a whole. It is the maximum net amount the participant is willing to owe all other participants combined. The multilateral limit is usually set by the system, or agreed between the participant and the system, reflecting the participant capital, liquidity, and risk appetite. A participant may be well within every bilateral limit and still breach its multilateral limit, if it owes a little to many counterparties. The multilateral limit is the backstop: it bounds the total exposure however it is distributed.

A payment must pass both limits. The system checks the bilateral limit with the receiving participant and the multilateral limit overall, and the payment proceeds only if both checks pass. The interaction creates queueing behaviour: a payment that fails the bilateral check waits for an incoming payment from that counterparty; a payment that fails the multilateral check waits for any incoming payment. Operators monitor both, because a participant approaching its multilateral limit is approaching a system wide constraint, while a participant approaching a bilateral limit has a counterparty specific problem. The two limits together shape the flow of payments through the day.

Setting limits is a risk decision. Bilateral limits reflect counterparty credit quality: a strong counterparty gets a higher limit, a weak one a lower limit, and limits are reviewed as credit assessments change. Multilateral limits reflect the participant own capacity: how much intraday exposure it can fund, and how much risk the system will allow it to run. Limits are set before the operating day, reviewed regularly, and changed only through a controlled process: an intraday limit increase in stress needs authorisation, because it increases the exposure the system carries. In systems like EURO1, limits are a core risk control, alongside loss sharing arrangements.

Operations lives with limits all day. Limit utilisation is monitored in real time: warnings fire as limits approach, and the participant acts, by funding, by delaying non urgent payments, or by requesting a limit change. Limit breaches queue payments, and queued payments create gridlock risk, so limit management and queue management are the same operational problem seen from two sides. The trade off is fundamental: tight limits are safer but queue more payments; loose limits flow freely but carry more exposure. The limit framework is where a netting system risk appetite becomes numbers, and the numbers become the day operational reality.
