Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Show transcript

The Single Euro Payments Area covers 36 countries: the 27 members of the European Union, plus the United Kingdom, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Switzerland, Monaco, San Marino, Andorra and Vatican City. A payment inside this area, in euro, between two accounts held at participating banks, is a SEPA payment, and it must be treated the same as a domestic payment. The area includes countries that do not use the euro, such as the United Kingdom, Norway and Sweden, and a bank in those countries can participate in the euro schemes even though its domestic currency is different, which is why a Swedish bank can send a SCT payment in euro. Participation is per scheme and it is a formal act. A payment service provider joins a scheme by signing an adherence agreement with the European Payments Council, and the EPC records the adherence in the register of participants. The register is public, and it lists every participant by BIC, the schemes it has joined, and the clearing systems it uses. A bank can join SCT and not SCT Inst, or join SDD Core and not SDD B2B, and each choice is recorded separately. The register is the document a bank checks when it onboards a new counterparty, and it is the document a clearing system checks when it routes a payment. Reachability is the practical consequence of participation. A bank is reachable for a scheme when it can receive payments under that scheme, either directly as a participant, or indirectly through another participant that acts on its behalf. Direct reachability means the bank has its own connection to a clearing system and settles in its own name. Indirect reachability means the bank is carried by a sponsor bank or an instructing party, and the sponsor settles on its behalf. A small bank in a small SEPA country is often reachable indirectly, and that is normal, but the sponsor must be named in the arrangement, and the sponsor must hold the liquidity and manage the exceptions for the bank it carries. When a bank is not reachable, the payment fails, and the failure must be handled cleanly. A SCT payment sent to a BIC that is not in the SCT register is rejected before settlement, and the originator bank must return it to the payer with a clear reason, not a generic error. An SCT Inst payment sent to a bank that is reachable for SCT but not for SCT Inst is rejected for the instant scheme, and the originator bank should offer the payer the regular SCT as an alternative. The customer experience depends on the reason being specific: a message that says the payee bank does not support instant payments is useful, a message that says the payment failed is not, and a message that says nothing is a complaint waiting to happen. Take a worked example. A small bank in Malta joins SCT and SDD Core but decides not to join SCT Inst, because it has no 24-hour operations team. A customer of a German bank tries to send an instant payment of 4,300 euro to an account at the Maltese bank on a Saturday. The German bank checks reachability, finds the Maltese bank is not reachable for SCT Inst, and rejects the payment before it leaves, telling the customer the payee bank does not support instant payments and offering a regular SCT instead. The customer accepts the regular payment, which settles on Monday. The following year the Maltese bank joins SCT Inst through an instructing party, the register is updated, and the same payment the next Saturday settles in eight seconds. The register, not a phone call, is what made the difference. Joining and leaving a scheme are both managed processes. To join, a bank signs the adherence agreement, completes testing with its clearing system, proves it can handle the scheme messages and the exception procedures, and is added to the register on the effective date. Testing includes the happy path, the rejects, the returns, and the timeout handling for instant payments. To leave, a bank gives notice, settles its outstanding payments, and is removed from the register on the agreed date, and from that date other banks must stop sending it payments under that scheme. A bank that leaves without notice, or that is terminated by the EPC for breaching the rules, creates a reachability problem for every bank that sends it payments, which is why the register is updated immediately and clearing systems check it daily. For a business analyst, participation and reachability work is a data and process discipline. Every requirement should state how the register is checked, how often it is refreshed, what happens when a counterparty is not reachable, and what message the customer sees. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment to a non-participant is rejected with a specific reason, a test where a payment to a bank reachable only for SCT is offered as a regular payment when the instant payment fails, a test where the register is refreshed and a newly joined bank becomes reachable, and a test where a bank that has left a scheme is no longer sent payments under it.
