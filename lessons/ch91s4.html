Key terms

Open banking: A system where banks share customer account data and payment services with licensed third parties through secure APIs, with customer consent.

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Refund: A repayment of funds to a customer after a payment fails, is cancelled or is disputed. In card payments a refund returns money to the cardholder account.

Confirmation of Payee, usually called CoP, is the UK name checking service for electronic payments. Before a payer sends a Faster Payment or a CHAPS payment, their bank asks the payee's bank whether the name the payer entered matches the name on the account. The payee's bank answers with one of three outcomes: match, close match, or no match. The service was built to fight authorised push payment fraud, where a fraudster tricks a victim into sending money to an account the victim believes belongs to someone else. If the name does not match, the payer is warned before the money moves.

The check works through a central service operated by Pay.UK, using the Open Banking standards for the API calls. When the payer enters the sort code, account number and payee name, the payer's bank sends a CoP request to the payee's bank, which compares the name against the account holder name it holds. The comparison is not a simple string match. It tolerates common variations, such as a missing middle name, an initial instead of a full first name, or a trading name instead of a legal name. The payee's bank returns the outcome, and may return the correct name in a close match case, so the payer can see what the account is actually called.

A match means the name the payer entered agrees with the name on the account, and the payment can proceed with confidence. A close match means the name is similar but not exact, for example the payer entered a nickname or the account is in a trading name. The payer is shown the actual name on the account and asked to confirm. A no match means the name does not agree with the account, and the payer is warned that the payment may be going to the wrong person, or to a fraudster, and is asked whether to proceed anyway. The payer can still proceed after a no match, and the bank will send the payment, but the warning is recorded, and it matters later if the payer claims a refund.

CoP also checks the account type. The payer's bank can ask whether the account is a personal account or a business account, and warn the payer if the type does not match what they expected. A payer who thinks they are paying a business but is actually paying a personal account has a reason to stop and check. The account type check catches a common fraud pattern, where a fraudster gives a personal account number and claims it belongs to a company. CoP does not check whether the account is genuine, whether the account holder is who they claim to be, or whether the account is controlled by a fraudster. It checks only the name.

Coverage is the practical limitation. CoP is mandatory for the largest UK banks and payment service providers, and most major banks are connected. Smaller providers have joined over time, but a payment to a provider that does not support CoP returns no check at all, and the payer gets no warning. A payment to an account held overseas is not covered, because CoP is a UK domestic service. A payment made by BACS Direct Debit is not checked, because the Direct Debit Guarantee already protects the payer. The coverage gap is where fraud migrates, so a bank that relies only on CoP will miss fraud that routes around it.

What goes wrong with CoP is usually a data problem, not a system problem. A joint account can produce a close match when the payer enters only one holder's name. A business that trades under a different name from its registered name produces a close match, and the payer must be shown the registered name to decide. A name with a special character, a hyphen, or a non-Latin script can fail the match even when it is correct. A payee who has recently changed their name, after marriage for example, may not match until the bank updates its records. Each of these is a customer experience problem as well as a fraud problem, because a payer who gets a no match on a legitimate payment may abandon it.

For a business analyst, CoP requirements are about the three outcomes and the audit trail. The acceptance criteria must state what the customer sees for a match, a close match and a no match, and what happens when the payee's bank does not respond. A test must prove that a no match produces a warning and that the customer's decision to proceed is logged with a timestamp. The requirements must cover the account type check and the handling of a provider that does not support CoP. The design must log every CoP request and response, because the log is the evidence the bank needs if the payer later claims they were not warned. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: Pay.UK Confirmation of Payee rules and standards; the Payment Systems Regulator directions on Confirmation of Payee; Open Banking CoP API specifications.
