What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain intraday processing, memo postings, value dating and back-value dating and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Memo versus ledger is the reason a customer sees two different balances, and the analyst must explain it.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how a bank's ledgers record every payment, distinguishes customer accounts, internal accounts, nostro and vostro ledgers, suspense and transit accounts, writes the exact general ledger postings for outgoing and incoming payments, and explains intraday processing, value dating, subledger feeds, reconciliation and error correction, with correct terminology.

  • Batch and several jobs in sequence
  • Cheque paid in today
  • Four-eyes approval
  • Requirements stating cut-off per type, value date rule, back-value approval

Key terms

Value dating: The practice of assigning a value date to a payment, which is the date funds become available and interest starts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

A bank's ledger works in two speeds, and confusing them causes most of the arguments between customers and operations teams. During the day, the core banking system processes payments intraday, which means it updates the customer's available balance immediately but does not yet write the hard ledger posting. These intraday updates are called memo postings. A memo posting is a note that says this money is committed, but it is not yet a journal entry. When a customer sends a Faster Payment of 500 pounds, their available balance falls by 500 pounds at once, but the ledger entry that records the debit and the credit is written during the end-of-day batch. The customer sees the payment immediately, and the ledger catches up overnight. The distinction between available balance, ledger balance and cleared balance is the practical consequence. The ledger balance is what the books say the customer holds. The available balance is the ledger balance minus holds and memo debits, plus memo credits, and it is the number the customer sees in their app and the number the bank uses to decide whether a payment can be made. The cleared balance is the ledger balance minus items that have not yet settled, such as a cheque that has been paid in but not yet cleared. A customer can have a ledger balance of 10,000 pounds, an available balance of 9,000 pounds because of a 1,000 pound card hold, and a cleared balance of 8,000 pounds because of a 2,000 pound cheque still clearing. A payment that exceeds the available balance is rejected, even though the ledger balance would have covered it. The end-of-day batch is the moment the day's memo postings become hard postings. The batch runs after the last cut-off, usually in the evening, and it does several jobs in sequence. It posts all the day's payments to the general ledger, it calculates and posts interest, it charges fees, it runs the trial balance, and it generates statements. Only when the trial balance matches can the day be closed and the next business day opened. If the batch fails, the next day cannot start, which is why an end-of-day failure is treated as a major incident and why banks rehearse it. The batch window is also when regulatory reports are generated, because they need a complete and balanced set of postings. Value dating decides from which day interest is calculated, and it is not always the same as the posting date. A payment made today with a value date of today accrues interest from today. A payment warehoused for a future date has a future value date, and the customer does not earn or pay interest on it until that date. A cheque paid in today may have a value date two days later, when the funds clear. The value date matters most for corporate customers, who manage their cash by value date and will complain loudly if a payment that should have been value-dated today is value-dated tomorrow, because a day of interest on 10 million pounds is real money. Back-value dating means posting a transaction with a value date in the past, and it is one of the most tightly controlled operations in a bank. It is used when a payment was delayed by the bank's own error and the customer should not lose the interest, or when a correction must restore the position the customer would have been in if the original entry had been correct. Back-value dating changes the interest calculation for the whole period back to the value date, so the system must recalculate interest and post an adjustment. It requires four-eyes approval, it is restricted to a small number of authorised users, and every back-valued entry is reported to finance, because back-value dating can be used to hide a loss by moving it into a closed period. A back-valued entry that moves value across a month end or a year end needs additional approval, because it changes a period that has already been reported. Cut-off times decide which value date a payment gets. In the UK, the CHAPS cut-off for customer payments is around 17:20, and the Bank of England's RTGS system closes at 18:00. A payment instructed at 17:30 misses the cut-off, is processed the next business day, and gets the next business day's value date. Faster Payments has no cut-off because it runs around the clock, but a payment sent on a Saturday is value-dated Saturday, and the ledger still posts it in the weekend batch. For a business analyst, the requirements must state the cut-off for each payment type, the value date rule, who can approve a back-valued entry, and what the interest adjustment looks like. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment is back-valued by three days and the interest adjustment is calculated and posted correctly. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
