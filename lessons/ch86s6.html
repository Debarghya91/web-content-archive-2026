What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain RBI directions and Indian payment regulation and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Indian payment regulation is a separate framework from European rules, and a global bank must run both at once.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the regulatory framework that binds payments in Europe, the UK, the US and India, distinguishes PSD2 from PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation, and applies DORA, the Instant Payments Regulation, sanctions regimes, AML rules, RBI directions and FFIEC guidance to realistic payment scenarios with correct terminology.

Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Tokenisation: Replacing a sensitive value, such as a card number, with a random token that has no meaning outside the system that created it.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

The Reserve Bank of India regulates Indian payments under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2007, which gives it the power to authorise payment system operators, to set the rules they must follow, and to inspect them. Every payment system in India, from the national financial switch to a prepaid payment instrument issuer, operates under an authorisation or a set of directions issued under that Act. The directions are detailed and they change frequently, so a payment firm operating in India needs a regulatory watch process that tracks new directions, circulars and master directions, and maps each one to the controls it requires.

The Know Your Customer Master Direction is the foundation for onboarding. It sets out the customer acceptance policy, the identification procedures, the monitoring of transactions, and the risk management framework. It distinguishes between simplified, regular and enhanced due diligence, and it sets the rules for video-based identification, for the central KYC registry, and for periodic updating of customer records. A payment firm that onboards customers remotely must follow the video KYC procedure exactly, including the liveness check and the recording, because a shortcut at onboarding becomes an examination finding later. The direction also requires the firm to file suspicious transaction reports with the Financial Intelligence Unit, FIU-IND, under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002.

Tokenisation is the RBI direction that most changed card payments in India. From October 2022, merchants and payment aggregators may no longer store card numbers, and must use tokens instead. A token is a surrogate value issued by the card network that maps to the real card number inside the network, so a breach of a merchant database exposes tokens that are useless elsewhere. The direction also covers card-on-file tokenisation for recurring payments, the e-mandate framework for standing instructions, and the requirement that the customer gives explicit consent before a mandate is registered. For a business analyst the requirements are precise: the token vault, the consent record, the mandate lifecycle, and the pre-debit notification that must reach the customer at least 24 hours before a recurring charge.

Cross-border payments from India are governed by the Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999 and the directions issued under it. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme allows a resident individual to remit up to 250,000 US dollars per financial year for permitted purposes, and the bank must check the purpose, collect the declaration, and report the transaction. Import payments must be made against shipping documents and within the permitted period, and export payments must be realised within the prescribed timeframe, with the bank monitoring outstanding entries. The Payment Aggregator Cross-Border framework governs firms that collect payments from Indian customers for overseas merchants, and the Online Payment Gateway Service Provider framework governs the reverse flow. A firm that handles cross-border payments must map each flow to the correct framework, because the wrong framework means the wrong reporting and the wrong controls.

Data localisation is the RBI rule with the widest operational impact for global firms. The 2018 circular requires all payment system data, including the full end-to-end transaction details and the information collected or processed as part of a payment instruction, to be stored in systems located in India. A global payment firm that processes Indian payments on servers abroad must either move the processing to India or replicate the data into India, and the replication must be complete, not a summary. The rule applies to card networks, to payment aggregators, and to any firm that touches an Indian payment. The compliance work is an architecture exercise: map every system that touches an Indian payment, confirm where its data sits, and close any gap.

UPI has its own rulebook, set by the National Payments Corporation of India and overseen by the RBI. The rules cover transaction limits, dispute handling, chargebacks, and the zero merchant discount rate that makes UPI free for merchants. The dispute framework sets strict timelines for the raising and resolution of complaints, and the RBI Integrated Ombudsman for digital transactions gives customers a route to escalate when the bank does not resolve a complaint. A payment firm that offers UPI must build its dispute workflow to the NPCI timelines, because a missed deadline is a regulatory breach as well as a customer complaint. The firm must also handle the failure modes that are specific to UPI: the collect request that is never approved, the debit that succeeds while the credit fails, and the timeout where the status is unknown.

Enforcement is real and it is public. The RBI imposes monetary penalties for failures in KYC, in reporting, and in compliance with directions, and it publishes the penalties. Examiners look for the basics done well: the customer acceptance policy applied in practice, the transaction monitoring rules tuned to the firm's own risks, the suspicious transaction reports filed on time, and the board oversight of the compliance function. For a business analyst, the RBI work is a mapping exercise: every direction must be traced to a control, every control to a test, and every test to evidence. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a video KYC onboarding is completed and recorded correctly, a test where a tokenised card payment works end to end, a test where a cross-border remittance is checked against the Liberalised Remittance Scheme limit, and a test where a UPI dispute is resolved inside the NPCI timeline.
