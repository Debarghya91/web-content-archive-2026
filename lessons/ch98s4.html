Before you accept an AI model, you have to agree what good looks like, in numbers, and how you will test it. A model requirement is not a vague promise like it should be accurate. It is a clear bar, written down, that you can check on real payment data. Start with the job the model does. Suppose it scores incoming payments for fraud risk and flags the risky ones for review. You need to say how often it should be right, how often it may be wrong, and what kind of wrong matters most. Missing a fraud is very different from flagging a good payment. If the model misses a fraudulent payment of 75,000 pounds, the bank loses money and the customer loses trust. If it wrongly flags a genuine payment of 850 pounds, the customer is annoyed and the operations team does extra work, but no money is lost. So you cannot judge the model on one accuracy number alone. You need to look at both kinds of error, and you need to set a limit for each. Test it on real payment data that the model has not seen before, including the awkward cases, duplicate payments, unusual amounts, new payees, and payments at weekends. Check how it behaves when the amount is high, when the payee is new, and when the data is incomplete. Set the acceptance bar before testing starts, not after you see the results. Write down the minimum pass level, the error limits, and the conditions under which you would stop the release. That written bar is what turns a demo into a product decision.
