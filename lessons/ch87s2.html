A transaction monitoring system has four parts, and a failure in any one of them breaks the whole control. Ingestion takes every payment from every channel and normalises it into a single record: who sent it, who received it, how much, in what currency, through which channel, and with what reference. The rules engine applies scenarios to the normalised records and generates alerts. Scoring ranks the alerts by risk so the team works on the most dangerous first. Case management holds the investigation, the evidence, the decision and the audit trail. A system that ingests only some channels, or that normalises inconsistently, will miss the payment that matters, and the gap will be found by an examiner, not by the team.

The rules are the heart of the system, and they fall into families. Threshold rules fire when a payment exceeds a value, or when the total for a customer exceeds a value in a period. Velocity rules fire when the number of payments, or the total value, exceeds what is normal for that customer. Structuring rules look for multiple payments just under a reporting threshold, for example five payments of 9,500 euros from a customer whose normal pattern is one payment a month. Dormancy rules fire when an account that has been quiet for a year suddenly moves large amounts. Geographic rules fire on payments to or from high-risk jurisdictions, or on payments that pass through an unexpected corridor. Each rule needs a threshold, an owner, and a documented reason, because a rule with no reason cannot be defended.

Tuning is the discipline that keeps the rules honest. A rule that is too sensitive generates thousands of alerts the team cannot review, and the team starts closing them without investigation, which is worse than having no rule at all. A rule that is too loose misses the fraud it was built to catch. Tuning uses above-the-line and below-the-line testing: the team takes a sample of transactions that fell just below the threshold and checks whether any of them were suspicious, and takes a sample above the threshold and checks whether the alerts were genuine. The results are used to adjust the threshold, and the adjustment is documented with the evidence. Tuning is done on a schedule, not when someone complains, and the schedule is part of the governance.

Alert triage is the human part of the system, and it needs a service level agreement by risk. A high-risk alert, such as a payment to a sanctioned geography, must be reviewed within hours. A medium-risk alert must be reviewed within a day. A low-risk alert can wait a week. The triage must record a disposition for every alert: true positive, false positive, or inconclusive, with a reason. The disposition matters, because it feeds back into the tuning: if a rule generates 95 percent false positives, the rule is wrong, not the customers. Quality assurance samples a proportion of closed alerts and re-reviews them, and the sampling rate is higher for the alerts closed as false positives, because that is where a missed fraud hides.

Model risk management applies to monitoring systems in the same way it applies to credit models. In the United States the Federal Reserve guidance on model risk management, SR 11-7, sets the standard: a model must be documented, validated by a team that did not build it, and monitored after deployment. The validation covers the data, the logic, the assumptions and the limitations. A monitoring model that was validated on retail payments and is then used on corporate payments without revalidation has a gap, and the gap will be found. The validation report must be kept, and the board must see it, because the board owns the model risk.

Real-time monitoring is the challenge that instant payments created. When a payment must complete in 10 seconds, the monitoring cannot wait for an overnight batch. The system must score the payment before release, using precomputed features, and must decide in well under a second whether to allow, challenge or hold it. The hold decision is the hardest, because a held instant payment breaches the 10-second rule, so the hold must be reserved for the highest-risk cases and the decision must be logged with the reason. Post-event monitoring still runs, for the patterns that only emerge over days, such as structuring and layering, but the pre-release check is what stops the fraud before the money leaves.

For a business analyst, the monitoring work is a set of requirements that must be measurable. The ingestion must cover every channel and every payment type, and the coverage must be provable. Each rule must have a documented threshold, an owner, and a tuning schedule. The triage must have a service level by risk and a disposition code for every alert. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a structuring pattern generates an alert, a test where a high-risk alert is reviewed inside its service level, a test where a tuning exercise changes a threshold with documented evidence, and a test where an instant payment is scored and released inside the 10-second budget. The management information must show the alert volumes, the false positive rate, and the time to disposition, because a monitoring system without management information is a system nobody is watching.
