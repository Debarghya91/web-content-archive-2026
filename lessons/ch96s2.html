Show transcript

Machine learning sounds technical, but the idea is simple, and you already understand it from your own work. You show the system many examples, it finds the patterns in those examples, and then it uses those patterns to make a judgement on a new case it has never seen. The examples are called training data. The patterns it finds are called the model. The judgement it gives is called a prediction, and it usually comes with a confidence score, which is the system's own measure of how sure it is.

Take fraud scoring, the classic payments example. A bank feeds the model a million past card payments, each one labelled as genuine or fraudulent. The model notices that fraudulent payments are often for odd amounts, at unusual hours, in countries the customer has never visited. Now a new payment arrives, for EUR 4,800, at three in the morning, from a merchant in a country the customer has never used. The model scores it as high risk, say 92 out of 100, and the payment is held for review.

Notice what happened and what did not happen. The model did not understand fraud. It matched a pattern. That is why the training data matters so much. If the training data is old, or it misses a new type of fraud, or it reflects a bias in past decisions, the model will repeat those gaps.

Your job as a BA is to ask three questions. What data was it trained on, how fresh is that data, and what happens when the score is wrong in each direction, a good payment blocked, or a bad payment let through.
