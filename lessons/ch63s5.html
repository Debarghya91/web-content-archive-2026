Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Interchange: A fee paid by the acquirer to the issuer for each card transaction, set by the card scheme. It compensates the issuer for handling the transaction, funding the interest free period and managing fraud...

Correct result. The flow starts when the cardholder at Meridian Bank London buys goods worth EUR 120 from an online merchant and enters the card number, expiry date and security code at checkout. The merchant website sends the transaction to its payment gateway, which forwards it to the acquirer, Harbour Bank Manchester. Harbour Bank checks that the merchant is active and that the transaction format is correct, then sends an authorisation request through the scheme network to Meridian Bank, the issuer. Meridian Bank runs its checks in a fixed order: card status, account status, limit, velocity and fraud.

If all checks pass, Meridian Bank approves the transaction, reserves EUR 120 on the cardholder account so it cannot be spent twice, and records the authorisation with a unique authorisation code. The approval travels back along the same path, usually in less than two seconds, and the merchant can now ship the goods. The approval is not yet a payment, because no money has moved. If any check fails, the flow stops with a decline, and the reason must be clear enough for the cardholder and the merchant to act on it.

A timeout is handled by a reversal, which cancels the reservation.

Why. A business analyst must define the decline codes, the reversal rules and the customer messages. A checkout that cannot explain a decline, or a timeout that leaves funds held, is a failed product.

The flow starts when the cardholder at Meridian Bank London buys goods worth EUR 120 from an online merchant and enters the card number, expiry date and security code at checkout. The merchant website sends the transaction to its payment gateway, which forwards it to the acquirer, Harbour Bank Manchester, together with the merchant details and the amount. Harbour Bank checks that the merchant is active and that the transaction format is correct, then sends an authorisation request through the scheme network to Meridian Bank, the issuer.

The request carries the PAN, the amount EUR 120, the currency, the merchant details, the card-not-present indicator and the authentication result. Meridian Bank receives the authorisation request and runs its checks in a fixed order. It first checks that the card is active, not reported lost or stolen, and not expired. It then checks the cardholder account, confirming that the EUR 120 is within the credit limit or the available balance, and it checks the security code and the authentication result.

It also runs fraud checks, looking at the merchant, the country, the amount and the cardholder spending pattern, and it may ask for extra authentication if the risk is high. If all checks pass, Meridian Bank approves the transaction, reserves EUR 120 on the cardholder account so it cannot be spent twice, and records the authorisation with a unique authorisation code. The approval travels back along the same path, from Meridian Bank through the scheme to Harbour Bank, and then to the merchant gateway and the checkout page, usually in less than two seconds.

The merchant sees an approval code and shows the cardholder a confirmation that the order is accepted, and the merchant can now ship the goods. The approval is not yet a payment, because no money has moved between banks at this stage. The cardholder account shows EUR 120 as reserved or pending, and the merchant account shows nothing yet, because the funds will only move when the transaction is cleared and settled, usually later the same day or the next day. If any check fails, the flow stops with a decline, and the reason must be clear enough for the cardholder and the merchant to act on it.

A decline for insufficient funds tells the cardholder to use another payment method, while a decline for a suspected fraud tells the cardholder to contact the issuer. A timeout, where the issuer does not answer in time, is handled by a reversal, which cancels the reservation so the cardholder is not left with EUR 120 held for a purchase that never happened. A business analyst must define the decline codes, the reversal rules and the customer messages, and a product owner should consider how the checkout explains a decline without revealing fraud logic that criminals could exploit.

Testing must cover approvals, declines, timeouts and reversals, because each path changes the ledger and the customer experience.
