Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Interchange: A fee paid by the acquirer to the issuer for each card transaction, set by the card scheme. It compensates the issuer for handling the transaction, funding the interest free period and managing fraud...

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Correct result. Debit customer account 250,000 pounds, credit Bank of England settlement account 250,000 pounds, debit customer account 25 pounds, credit fee income 25 pounds, and the ledger balances to the penny.

Why. The payment and the fee are two separate postings, so the customer is debited twice, once for each, and each debit has its own matching credit.

The best way to understand ledger postings is to work through the exact entries for a real payment. A corporate customer sends 250,000 pounds to a supplier through CHAPS, and the bank charges a fee of 25 pounds. The payment produces four entries. First, debit the customer's current account 250,000 pounds and credit the bank's settlement account at the Bank of England 250,000 pounds. The customer's liability has fallen and the bank's reserves have fallen by the same amount. Second, debit the customer's current account 25 pounds and credit fee income 25 pounds. The customer's account has now been debited 250,025 pounds in total, the bank's reserves have fallen by 250,000 pounds, and the bank has recognised 25 pounds of income.

An incoming CHAPS payment is the mirror image. A supplier sends 250,000 pounds to the same corporate customer. The bank debits its settlement account at the Bank of England by 250,000 pounds, because its reserves have risen, and credits the customer's current account by 250,000 pounds. There is no fee on the incoming side in this example, although some banks charge for incoming CHAPS payments and would add a fee entry. The customer's balance is 250,000 pounds higher, the bank's reserves are 250,000 pounds higher, and the balance sheet still balances because the asset and the liability moved by the same amount.

A cross-border payment adds foreign exchange and a nostro account to the picture. A customer sends 100,000 US dollars to a supplier in the United States, and the bank's exchange rate for the customer is 1.27 dollars to the pound. The customer is debited 78,740 pounds and 16 pence, which is 100,000 divided by 1.27, and the bank credits its US dollar nostro account with 100,000 dollars, reducing the asset. The bank also charges a fee of 30 pounds, so it debits the customer 30 pounds and credits fee income 30 pounds. The customer's account has fallen by 78,770 pounds and 16 pence in total. The nostro account is recorded in the general ledger in pounds at the day's rate for reporting purposes, so the ledger also carries a sterling equivalent of the dollar position.

The foreign exchange margin is where the bank makes money on the payment, and it must be posted explicitly. Suppose the bank covers its dollar position in the market at a rate of 1.2705. It buys 100,000 dollars for 78,708 pounds and 74 pence, which is 100,000 divided by 1.2705. The customer paid 78,740 pounds and 16 pence for the same dollars, so the bank keeps 31 pounds and 42 pence. That amount is posted as a credit to foreign exchange trading income, and the corresponding debit is to the nostro account. If the market rate moves against the bank between the customer instruction and the cover trade, the difference is a loss, and it is posted the same way. The ledger therefore shows both the customer rate and the market rate, and the difference between them, which is essential for proving that the customer was charged the rate they were quoted.

A card payment shows how authorisation and clearing produce different postings. A customer buys goods for 85 pounds with a debit card. At the moment of purchase the bank places a hold of 85 pounds on the customer's account, which reduces the available balance but does not change the ledger balance, because a hold is a memo posting, not a ledger posting. Two days later the clearing file arrives from the card scheme. The bank now posts the ledger entries: debit the card scheme settlement account 85 pounds and credit the merchant's acquiring account 85 pounds, and separately debit the customer's account 85 pounds and credit the card settlement account 85 pounds. Interchange flows the other way, from the merchant's bank to the cardholder's bank, and scheme fees are posted to their own income and expense accounts.

The general ledger itself is kept in the bank's reporting currency, usually pounds for a UK bank, and every foreign currency position is revalued each day at the closing rate. If the bank holds 1 million dollars in its nostro account and the rate moves from 1.27 to 1.26, the sterling value of that position rises, and the difference is posted to the profit and loss account as a revaluation gain or loss. The revaluation is a system posting, not a manual one, and it is reversed the next day so that the position is always valued at the latest rate. For a business analyst, the requirement is that every payment flow must come with a posting map that shows each entry, each account, each amount and each value date, and the acceptance criteria must include a test where the worked example above is run through the system and every account moves by exactly the expected amount.
