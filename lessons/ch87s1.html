Authorised push payment fraud, APP fraud, is the fastest growing fraud in UK payments and the hardest to stop, because the victim authorises the payment themselves. The fraud starts with a deception: a fake invoice, a fake investment, a romance, or a call from someone pretending to be the bank, the police, or a government department. The victim is persuaded to send money to an account controlled by the fraudster, and the money is then moved on within minutes through a chain of accounts. The payment is genuine in the technical sense, the authentication was correct, the payer intended to send it, and that is exactly why the old controls do not catch it. The control that catches it is verification of payee, because the name on the account does not match the name the victim expects.

The UK reimbursement regime for APP fraud changed the economics of the problem. From 7 October 2024, the Payment Systems Regulator requires the sending and the receiving payment service providers to reimburse victims of APP fraud, up to a maximum of 85,000 pounds per claim, with the cost split equally between the two firms. Reimbursement must be made within 5 business days of the claim, and the sending firm can only refuse where the victim acted with gross negligence or where the claim is not made within 13 months. The regime creates a direct financial incentive for both firms to prevent the fraud: the sending firm must warn and check, and the receiving firm must monitor the accounts that receive the money. A receiving firm that onboards mule accounts and ignores the warning signs now pays for the fraud.

Mule accounts are the infrastructure of payment fraud. A mule is a person who allows their account to be used to receive and forward criminal funds, usually recruited through a job advert, a social media post, or a small payment. The mule account receives the victim money, keeps a small cut, and forwards the rest to the next account in the chain. Detection relies on behaviour, not on identity, because the mule is a real person with a real account. The signals are velocity, a sudden increase in incoming payments from unrelated senders, followed by rapid onward transfers to accounts the customer has never paid before. Device signals matter too: an account that is suddenly accessed from a new device, in a new location, at unusual hours, after a period of dormancy, is a classic mule pattern.

Business email compromise is the most expensive fraud per case. The fraudster gains access to a company email account, or spoofs it convincingly, and then sends a payment instruction that looks legitimate: an invoice with changed bank details, a request from the chief executive to pay a supplier urgently, or a message from the finance director authorising a transfer. The payment is often large, because it is a business payment, and the money is moved offshore quickly. The controls that work are procedural, not technical. A change of supplier bank details must be verified by a phone call to a known number, never by replying to the email that requested the change. A payment above a threshold must require two authorisers, and an urgent payment must not bypass the check. A firm that allows a single person to change a payee and release the payment has no control at all.

Card fraud and account takeover are the older typologies and they have not gone away. Card-not-present fraud, where stolen card details are used online, is still the largest category by volume. Account takeover, where the fraudster gains access to the victim online banking or card account, is growing, because the fraudster then controls the authentication as well as the payment. Synthetic identity fraud, where a fraudster creates a new identity from a mix of real and invented details, is the hardest to detect, because there is no victim to report it. The controls are layered: device fingerprinting, behavioural biometrics that recognise how a person types and moves a mouse, velocity checks on new devices and new payees, and consortium data that shows whether the same device or identity has been involved in fraud elsewhere.

Detection systems work by combining signals that are weak on their own and strong together. A single signal, such as a new payee, means little. A new payee plus a large amount plus a first-time device plus a payment at 3 in the morning means a great deal. The scoring model combines these signals into a risk score, and the score decides whether the payment goes straight through, is challenged with additional authentication, or is held for manual review. The model must be tuned to the population, because a model built on retail customers will misfire on corporate payments, and a model built on domestic payments will misfire on cross-border ones. The tuning is governed, with regular back-testing against confirmed fraud cases, so the team can prove the model catches what it should.

For a business analyst, the fraud work is a set of requirements that must be testable against real cases. The verification of payee check must run before release and must be logged. The reimbursement workflow must meet the 5 business day deadline and must record the decision and the reason. The mule detection rules must be documented, with the thresholds and the evidence for each one. The payee change procedure must require verification through a separate channel. The acceptance criteria must include a test where an APP fraud claim is reimbursed inside 5 business days, a test where a mule pattern generates an alert, a test where a supplier bank detail change is blocked until verified by phone, and a test where a high-risk payment is challenged with additional authentication.
