Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

When a payment travels through several banks, each bank sees only its own leg, and no single bank holds the complete picture of the transaction from end to end. Transaction Manager is the SWIFT service that changes that. It is a central platform that stores the complete ISO 20022 data for a cross border payment, keyed by the Unique End-to-End Transaction Reference, the UETR, so that every bank in the chain can access the full transaction data, even if its own systems cannot yet process the full ISO 20022 message. It is the data backbone of the CBPR+ migration, and the analyst who designs cross border flows must understand what it stores, who can access it, and what problems it solves.

The problem it solves is truncation. During the migration from MT to ISO 20022, not every bank could process the full ISO 20022 message, with its rich structured data. When a payment passed through a bank that could only handle the older format, the rich data was at risk of being cut down to fit, and the beneficiary bank would receive less information than the sending bank had provided. Transaction Manager breaks that chain: the sending bank deposits the complete ISO 20022 data in the platform, and each bank in the chain can retrieve the complete data through the platform, even if the message it received directly was truncated. The payment instruction still travels bank to bank through the normal channels, but the complete data is always available from the central record, keyed by the UETR that travels with the payment.

Access is controlled and audited. A bank can retrieve the transaction data only for payments it is involved in, as sender, intermediary or receiver, and every retrieval is logged. The data is accessed through interfaces that the bank integrates with its payment hub or its investigation tools, so that an operations analyst working a case can pull the complete data without asking the sending bank to resend it. The platform stores the data for the retention period defined by the service, and the bank must still keep its own records under its local regulatory requirements. Transaction Manager is a shared operational record, not a replacement for the bank own books, and the analyst must not design a process that depends on the platform as the only copy of the data.

The platform supports the full lifecycle, not just the payment instruction. Status updates, returns, cancellations and investigation messages all reference the same UETR, and the platform links them to the original transaction, so that the complete history of a payment is visible in one place. When a payment is returned, the return message references the UETR, and the platform shows the return alongside the original. When an investigation is opened, the case messages reference the UETR, and the platform shows the case alongside the payment. This linking is what makes gpi tracking work end to end: the tracking service reads the status events from the platform and presents them as a single journey, rather than as disconnected messages from different banks.

For the analyst, Transaction Manager changes three design decisions. First, the payment hub must capture and store the UETR for every cross border payment, because without the UETR the bank cannot retrieve the central record. Second, the investigation and exception processes must be able to pull the complete data from the platform, rather than relying on the truncated message the bank received, because the complete data is what resolves most cases. Third, the data model must distinguish between the message the bank received, which may be truncated, and the transaction data from the platform, which is complete, and must record which source each field came from. A design that treats the received message as the whole truth will miss data that the platform holds, and a design that ignores the platform will rebuild by correspondence what the platform already provides.

A worked example shows the value. A corporate sends a payment with rich remittance information, including invoice numbers and a structured address, through three banks. The middle bank can only process a truncated version of the message, and the beneficiary bank receives the payment with the remittance information cut short. The beneficiary queries the payment, and the beneficiary bank opens a case. Instead of sending queries up the chain and waiting days for answers, the beneficiary bank retrieves the complete transaction data from Transaction Manager using the UETR, sees the full remittance information and the structured address, and resolves the case the same day. The payment data was never lost. It was in the central record all along, waiting to be retrieved.
