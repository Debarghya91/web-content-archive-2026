Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Use the clearing rules and the netting logic in this lesson.

  1. What to do. List the payments in the cycle, calculate the net position for each bank, and state what settles.

Correct result. Clearing validates, sorts, matches and nets the instructions, then produces a net position per bank. Only the net position settles, which needs far less liquidity than settling every payment individually.

Why. Netting is what makes high volume payments possible. Without it, every payment would need full liquidity at the moment it is sent.

A clearing house is an institution that receives payment instructions from multiple banks, sorts them, calculates net positions, and prepares them for settlement. In a domestic automated clearing house, thousands of payments are collected during the day, netted against each other, and settled in a small number of settlement cycles. The clearing house does not hold customer accounts, but it provides the rules, the file formats, the timetable, and the operational infrastructure that let banks exchange payments efficiently. Without a clearing house, each bank would need a direct connection to every other bank, which would be slow, costly, and hard to control.

The central bank is the institution that provides final settlement in central bank money, which is the safest form of money because it is a direct claim on the central bank. Most countries have a real time gross settlement system operated by the central bank, where high value payments settle individually and immediately in central bank money. The central bank also sets the rules for payment system oversight, for liquidity provision, and for finality. When a payment settles at the central bank, it is final, and the receiving bank can safely credit its customer without taking on interbank credit risk.

The relationship between clearing and settlement is often misunderstood. Clearing is the exchange and netting of instructions, while settlement is the movement of central bank money that makes those instructions final. A payment can be cleared but not yet settled, and that gap creates intraday risk. Analysts must understand the clearing cycle for each system they work with, including the cut off times, the settlement windows, and the consequences of a bank failing to settle. Those details determine whether a payment is same day, next day, or at risk, and they must be documented in any payment product specification.

Run the numbers for one clearing cycle. An automated clearing house collects 1.8 million retail payments during the day with a gross value of EUR 4.1 billion. After netting, the settlement amounts total EUR 52 million, which moves between the banks through their central bank accounts at the 4 in the afternoon settlement. The clearing house itself holds no customer money. It provides the rules, the file formats, the 2 in the afternoon cut off, and the calculation. The central bank provides the final settlement in central bank money, and once it debits and credits the settlement accounts, the cycle is final. Now take the high value side. A bank sends a single EUR 45 million payment through the central bank real time gross settlement system at half past 9 in the morning. It settles individually and immediately, with no netting and no waiting for a cycle, because a payment of that size cannot wait for the afternoon. The two systems do different jobs, and most countries run both side by side.

An analyst must document the clearing cycle for each system the bank uses, in the same format every time, collection cut off, net calculation, funding deadline, settlement time, and what happens if a participant misses each one. The specification should also state the finality point for each system, because a payment that is cleared but not yet settled is still at risk, and the operations team needs to know exactly how much is in that gap at any moment. The reconciliation design should keep cleared but not settled payments in a separate queue, so the team can see the exposure, and the customer communication should never describe a cleared payment as settled before the central bank money has actually moved.
