What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain paying from a subsidiary account and paying on behalf of subsidiaries. Paying from a subsidiary account means the money leaves an account owned by the subsidiary, even though the parent treasury team sends the instruction.

Why it matters. The bank must hold a mandate for either pattern before it will act.

Chapter objective: Learner explains MT101 use cases, multiple debit accounts, subsidiary payments, funds repatriation and the difference between Sequence A and Sequence B. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

  • EUR 50,000 from London to Munich

Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Paying from a subsidiary account means the money leaves an account owned by the subsidiary, even though the parent treasury team sends the instruction. The parent may hold a mandate to operate the subsidiary account, often as part of a cash pooling or shared service agreement. A parent company in London may send an MT101 to HSBC, instructing it to pay 75,000 euros from the account of its French subsidiary, Lyon Trading SARL, to a supplier in Lyon. Field 25 in Sequence B names the Lyon Trading account, and field 50a in Sequence B names Lyon Trading SARL as the ordering customer for that transaction. Paying on behalf of a subsidiary is different. Here the money leaves the parent account, but the payment is made for the benefit of the subsidiary, and the beneficiary should know that the subsidiary is the real buyer. A parent in New York may pay 120,000 dollars from its own account to a supplier in Shanghai, for goods ordered by its subsidiary in Shanghai. Field 25 names the parent account, field 50a in Sequence A names the parent, and field 50a in Sequence B, or field 70 for remittance information, names the subsidiary as the party on whose behalf the payment is made. The supplier can then match the payment to the subsidiary order. The bank must hold a mandate for either pattern before it will act. For a payment from a subsidiary account, the bank must hold a mandate signed by the subsidiary, authorising the parent to instruct debits, and it must check that the mandate covers the amount and the payment type. For a payment on behalf of a subsidiary, the bank must be satisfied that the parent is authorised to act for the subsidiary, and it may ask for a copy of the intercompany agreement. A bank that debits an account without a valid mandate may have to reverse the payment and may face a claim from the account holder. The accounting follows the legal reality, not the message format. A payment from a subsidiary account is a debit in the subsidiary books, and the subsidiary must record the supplier payment as its own expense. A payment on behalf of a subsidiary is first recorded as an intercompany loan or payable in the parent books, and then as an expense in the subsidiary books when the intercompany charge is raised. A treasurer who confuses the two patterns will create a reconciliation break, because the bank statement will show a debit in one entity while the accounts show an expense in another. A group example brings the two patterns together. A group called Ventura Holdings has a subsidiary in India, Ventura India Pvt Ltd, and a subsidiary in Germany, Ventura GmbH. In one MT101 to Citibank, Ventura sends three payments. The first pays 200,000 rupees from the Ventura India account to a supplier in Chennai, with field 50a naming Ventura India. The second pays 50,000 euros from the parent account in London to a supplier in Munich on behalf of Ventura GmbH, with field 70 noting the GmbH order number. The third pays 30,000 dollars from the parent account for a parent expense. Each payment is reported under the correct entity, and the group accountant can post each one without a manual correction.

Paying from a subsidiary account and paying on behalf of subsidiaries are two different patterns, and an SME must keep them apart. In the first pattern, the subsidiary itself is the ordering customer, and the payment is debited from the subsidiary own account. In the second pattern, the parent pays on behalf of the subsidiary, and the payment is debited from the parent account, but the ordering customer named in the transaction is the subsidiary. The MT101 supports both, through the combination of field 50a and field 25 in Sequence B. The difference matters for the reporting, the tax treatment, and the intercompany accounting.

In the subsidiary account pattern, the subsidiary sends the MT101, or the parent sends it with the subsidiary named as the ordering customer. Sequence A field 50a names the parent or the group treasury, and field 25 in Sequence A names the parent account as the default. Sequence B for the subsidiary payment carries field 50a naming the subsidiary, for example Atlas GmbH, Munich, and field 25 naming the subsidiary account, for example DE89 3704 0044 0532 0130 00 at a German bank. The bank debits the subsidiary account, and the beneficiary sees the subsidiary as the ordering customer. The bank checks that the sender is authorised to instruct a payment from the subsidiary account, which normally requires a mandate from the subsidiary to the parent or to the group treasury.

In the payment on behalf pattern, often called POBO, the parent debits its own account but names the subsidiary as the ordering customer. Sequence B carries field 50a naming the subsidiary, Atlas GmbH, and field 25 naming the parent account, GB29 BARC 2012 3456 7890 01. The bank debits the parent account, but the beneficiary and the reporting show the subsidiary as the ordering customer. The parent then records an intercompany receivable from the subsidiary for the amount paid. The subsidiary records an intercompany payable to the parent. The bank does not need to know about the intercompany accounting, but it does need to know that the parent is authorised to pay on behalf of the subsidiary, and that authorisation is normally given in the account mandate or in a separate POBO agreement.

The mandate is the legal basis, and it must be in place before the first payment. A mandate is a document, signed by the subsidiary, authorising the parent or the group treasury to instruct payments from the subsidiary account, or to pay on its behalf from the parent account. The bank holds the mandate on file, and it checks each MT101 against it. If the mandate is missing or has expired, the bank will reject the transaction. A treasurer who sets up a new subsidiary must therefore arrange the mandate at the same time as opening the account, and must renew it when the authorised signatories change. A payment that is rejected because the mandate is missing is a common failure in the first month after a new subsidiary is created.

The reporting differs between the two patterns. In the subsidiary account pattern, the debit appears on the subsidiary bank statement, and the subsidiary reconciles it against its own accounts payable. In the POBO pattern, the debit appears on the parent bank statement, and the parent reconciles it against its intercompany ledger. The beneficiary receives the same information in both cases, the subsidiary name as the ordering customer, so the supplier can match the payment to the correct invoice. The difference is visible only in the bank statements and the intercompany accounting, not in the payment message the beneficiary receives.

Consider a full example. Atlas Holdings has a parent in London and two subsidiaries, Atlas GmbH in Munich and Atlas SAS in Paris. On 12 May 2026 the group treasury sends one MT101 to Barclays with three Sequence B blocks. Sequence B 1 pays 60,000 euros to a supplier in Frankfurt for Atlas GmbH, debited from the Atlas GmbH account in Frankfurt, with field 50a naming Atlas GmbH. Sequence B 2 pays 25,000 euros to a supplier in Lyon for Atlas SAS, debited from the parent account in London, with field 50a naming Atlas SAS, which is the POBO pattern. Sequence B 3 pays 12,000 pounds to a consultant in Manchester for the parent, debited from the parent account, with field 50a naming Atlas Holdings Ltd. One message, three patterns, three different debit accounts and ordering customers, and the bank handles each correctly because the field 50a and field 25 in each Sequence B state the position explicitly.

For a business analyst, the subsidiary requirement must cover the mandate, the ordering customer, and the intercompany accounting. The requirement should state that the system carries field 50a and field 25 per Sequence B, that it checks the mandate before the message is sent, that it records whether each transaction is a subsidiary account payment or a POBO payment, and that it posts the intercompany entries for POBO transactions automatically. A system that cannot distinguish the two patterns will post the accounting incorrectly, and the error will only be found at month end when the intercompany balances do not reconcile.
