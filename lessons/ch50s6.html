Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Paying from a subsidiary account means the money leaves an account owned by the subsidiary, even though the parent treasury team sends the instruction. The parent may hold a mandate to operate the subsidiary account, often as part of a cash pooling or shared service agreement. A parent company in London may send an MT101 to HSBC, instructing it to pay 75,000 euros from the account of its French subsidiary, Lyon Trading SARL, to a supplier in Lyon. Field 25 in Sequence B names the Lyon Trading account, and field 50a in Sequence B names Lyon Trading SARL as the ordering customer for that transaction.

Paying on behalf of a subsidiary is different. Here the money leaves the parent account, but the payment is made for the benefit of the subsidiary, and the beneficiary should know that the subsidiary is the real buyer. A parent in New York may pay 120,000 dollars from its own account to a supplier in Shanghai, for goods ordered by its subsidiary in Shanghai. Field 25 names the parent account, field 50a in Sequence A names the parent, and field 50a in Sequence B, or field 70 for remittance information, names the subsidiary as the party on whose behalf the payment is made.

The supplier can then match the payment to the subsidiary order. The bank must hold a mandate for either pattern before it will act. For a payment from a subsidiary account, the bank must hold a mandate signed by the subsidiary, authorising the parent to instruct debits, and it must check that the mandate covers the amount and the payment type. For a payment on behalf of a subsidiary, the bank must be satisfied that the parent is authorised to act for the subsidiary, and it may ask for a copy of the intercompany agreement.

A bank that debits an account without a valid mandate may have to reverse the payment and may face a claim from the account holder. The accounting follows the legal reality, not the message format. A payment from a subsidiary account is a debit in the subsidiary books, and the subsidiary must record the supplier payment as its own expense. A payment on behalf of a subsidiary is first recorded as an intercompany loan or payable in the parent books, and then as an expense in the subsidiary books when the intercompany charge is raised.

A treasurer who confuses the two patterns will create a reconciliation break, because the bank statement will show a debit in one entity while the accounts show an expense in another. A group example brings the two patterns together. A group called Ventura Holdings has a subsidiary in India, Ventura India Pvt Ltd, and a subsidiary in Germany, Ventura GmbH. In one MT101 to Citibank, Ventura sends three payments. The first pays 200,000 rupees from the Ventura India account to a supplier in Chennai, with field 50a naming Ventura India.

The second pays 50,000 euros from the parent account in London to a supplier in Munich on behalf of Ventura GmbH, with field 70 noting the GmbH order number. The third pays 30,000 dollars from the parent account for a parent expense. Each payment is reported under the correct entity, and the group accountant can post each one without a manual correction.
