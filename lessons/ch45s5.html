What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain roadmaps, prioritisation and stakeholder management. A roadmap is a plan that shows the major features and changes for a product over the next twelve to eighteen months, grouped by quarter.

Why it matters. Stakeholders in a payment product include the customers who use it, the sales team who sells it, the operations team who runs it, the risk team who approves it, the technology team who builds it and the senior executives who fund it.

Chapter objective: Learner applies product management to payments, defining a product vision, roadmap, metrics, and pricing, and writes a product brief for a new payment feature. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key learning points

  • Roadmap: plan shows major features
  • Roadmap: reviewed every quarter
  • Common method
  • Manager must listen to all of them
  • Manager keeps stakeholders informed
  • Meeting ends with a signed

Key terms

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Queue: A holding area in a payment system where payments wait for sufficient funds or for their turn to settle.

DEBT: A charge bearer code in ISO 20022 meaning the debtor pays all charges for the payment. The debtor agent deducts charges from the debtor account or adds them to the amount, and no charges are deducted...

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A roadmap is a plan that shows the major features and changes for a product over the next twelve to eighteen months, grouped by quarter. It is not a detailed project plan, but a statement of intent that helps the bank plan investment and helps customers plan their own changes. A roadmap for an instant payment product might show confirmation of payee in the first quarter, recurring payments in the second quarter, bulk instant payments in the third quarter and an API for software partners in the fourth quarter. Each item on the roadmap has an owner, an estimated cost and a measure of success, so the bank can see what it is buying. The roadmap is reviewed every quarter, because priorities change as customers, competitors and regulators move. Prioritisation is the hardest part of the job, because every request sounds urgent to the person who made it. The manager uses a scoring model that weighs the value to the customer, the revenue impact, the strategic fit and the cost to build. A feature that will earn 2 million pounds a year and cost 300,000 pounds to build scores higher than a feature that will earn 200,000 pounds and cost 500,000 pounds to build. The manager also considers risk, because a feature that fixes a regulatory gap must be built even if it earns no revenue. A common method is to score each idea from one to five on value, revenue, risk and effort, and to rank the ideas by total score. The ranking is then discussed openly, so stakeholders can see why their request is third and not first. Stakeholders in a payment product include the customers who use it, the sales team who sells it, the operations team who runs it, the risk team who approves it, the technology team who builds it and the senior executives who fund it. Each group has different concerns. Sales wants a feature that will win a deal this quarter, operations wants a feature that will reduce the exception queue, risk wants a feature that will reduce fraud, and technology wants time to fix technical debt. The manager must listen to all of them, explain the trade offs honestly, and make a decision that serves the product as a whole rather than one group. A manager who says yes to everyone will deliver nothing well. Managing a difficult stakeholder starts with understanding what they really need, which is often different from what they ask for. A sales director who demands a new currency by next month may really need a way to win a customer who trades in that currency, and a simpler solution such as a correspondent bank arrangement may solve the problem faster. The manager meets the stakeholder, listens to the underlying need, shares the data on cost and effort, and offers a realistic date or an alternative. The manager also keeps stakeholders informed with a short monthly update, so they hear about delays from the manager rather than from a customer. Trust is built by delivering on small promises consistently, not by making large promises that cannot be kept. A roadmap review meeting is where the plan is tested and agreed. The manager presents the proposed roadmap, the scoring behind it and the trade offs that were made, and invites challenge from each stakeholder group. A technology lead may point out that a feature needs six months rather than three, and the roadmap is adjusted. A risk manager may point out that a new feature needs a fraud review, and a milestone is added. The meeting ends with a signed off roadmap that everyone understands, even if not everyone agrees with every choice. The manager then publishes the roadmap internally, so sales can talk to customers with confidence and technology can plan its resourcing. A roadmap that is not communicated is only a private wish list.
