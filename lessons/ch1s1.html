Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain payment and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Understanding payments in this way helps a business analyst avoid a common mistake, which is to treat every money movement as the same thing.

Key learning points

  • Payment: transfer of value
  • Instructions as a message, value through accounts and settlement
  • Analyst questions: obligation, risk and evidence
  • The whole idea of a payment
  • Wrong-amount payment recovered by a second payment
  • Stakeholder pressure to send the money without the detail

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Practise this lesson

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a EUR 50,000, payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full EUR 50,000; the sender pays EUR 50,065 in total. Under SHA the sender pays the sending bank charge of EUR 25, a total of EUR 50,025, and the beneficiary receives EUR 49,960, which is EUR 50,000 less the correspondent charge of EUR 25 and less the beneficiary bank charge of EUR 15. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives EUR 49,935, which is EUR 50,000 less EUR 25 less EUR 25 less EUR 15; the sender pays exactly EUR 50,000.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A payment is the transfer of value from one party to another in order to settle an obligation. When you buy a coffee, pay rent, send salary to an employee, or settle an invoice with a supplier, you are making a payment. The obligation can come from a purchase, a loan, a tax, a salary agreement, or any other commitment where one party owes value to another. The payment discharges that obligation, either fully or partly, and it creates a record that the debt has been reduced or cleared. Without this idea of an obligation being settled, a movement of money is just a transfer, and the distinction matters in banking systems, in law, and in message design. In a modern banking context, a payment almost always involves accounts held with regulated institutions and a set of instructions that tell those institutions what to move, from whom, to whom, and for what reason. The instructions travel as a payment message, while the actual value moves through accounts and settlement systems. A payment can be made in cash, by card, by cheque, by direct debit, or by an electronic transfer, but in every case the same core elements are present. There is a payer who owes, a payee who is owed, an amount, a currency, a value date, and a reason or reference that links the payment to the underlying business event. Understanding payments in this way helps a business analyst avoid a common mistake, which is to treat every money movement as the same thing. A salary payment, a supplier payment, a refund, and a loan repayment may all look similar on a bank statement, but they carry different business meanings, different regulatory expectations, and different risks. A good analyst always asks what obligation is being settled, who bears the risk if the payment fails, and what evidence exists that the obligation was discharged. Those three questions shape how payment products are designed, how exceptions are handled, and how messages are structured. Take a worked example. A manufacturer buys raw materials from a supplier for EUR 50,000, and the invoice is due on the last day of the month. The manufacturer is the payer, the supplier is the payee, the amount is EUR 50,000, the currency is euros, the value date is the last day of the month, and the reference is the invoice number. When the manufacturer sends the payment, the obligation is discharged in full, and the supplier can match the receipt against the invoice. If the manufacturer sent only EUR 30,000, the obligation would be discharged in part, and EUR 20,000 would still be owed. That is the whole idea in one payment. Charges decide what the beneficiary actually receives. A cross border payment passes through several banks, and each bank charges a fee. The charge arrangement, carried in field 71A of the MT103 and in the charge bearer element of the pacs.008, decides who pays those fees. There are three options. OUR means the sender pays all the charges, so the beneficiary receives the full amount. SHA means the charges are shared: the sender pays the sending bank charge, and the correspondent and beneficiary bank charges are deducted from the amount in transit, so the beneficiary receives less. BEN means the beneficiary pays all the charges: every bank deducts its charge from the amount, so the beneficiary receives what is left after all deductions. Take the manufacturer payment of EUR 50,000 from this lesson, and use these example fees: the sending bank charges EUR 25, the correspondent bank charges EUR 25, and the beneficiary bank charges EUR 15. Under OUR, the sender pays EUR 50,000 plus EUR 25 plus EUR 25 plus EUR 15, a total of EUR 50,065, and the beneficiary receives the full EUR 50,000. Under SHA, the sender pays EUR 50,000 plus the sending bank charge of EUR 25, a total of EUR 50,025, and the beneficiary receives EUR 50,000 less the correspondent charge of EUR 25 and less the beneficiary bank charge of EUR 15, which is EUR 49,960. Under BEN, the sender pays exactly EUR 50,000, and the beneficiary receives EUR 50,000 less EUR 25 less EUR 25 less EUR 15, which is EUR 49,935. That is the calculation the exercise asks for, and it is why the charge arrangement matters: a supplier who must receive EUR 50,000 in full needs OUR, and a payment sent as SHA or BEN leaves the invoice short. Worked example with real numbers: a retailer owes a supplier EUR 12,400 for invoice INV-2026-5521, due Friday. The retailer instructs EUR 12,400 by SEPA Credit Transfer, the pacs.008 carries the invoice number as the end to end reference, and the supplier reconciles the receipt against the invoice on the same day. If the retailer had sent EUR 12,000, the supplier would have EUR 400 outstanding, the invoice would stay open, and the supplier credit control team would chase the shortfall. The amount, the reference and the value date are therefore not decoration, they are the proof that the obligation was discharged in full, and a payment without a reference is a payment the beneficiary cannot reconcile. Failure and recovery: a payment sent to the wrong IBAN is not a failed payment, it is a successful payment to the wrong person, and the recovery is a recall with a camt.056, which needs the beneficiary agreement and can take days. A payment sent with the wrong amount is recovered by a second payment for the difference, or by a return of the excess, and the customer must be told which one applies. A payment that is debited but never credited, because the interbank leg failed, is recovered by reversing the debit with a correcting entry and telling the customer the payment did not go through, so the customer does not assume the supplier was paid. Each failure has a different message, a different timeline and a different owner. For a business analyst, product owner or subject matter expert, the lesson is that a payment requirement must name the obligation, the parties, the amount, the currency, the value date and the reference, and must state what happens when each one is wrong. The ISO 20022 pacs.008 carries all six, and the camt.053 statement proves the posting, so the requirement must map the business obligation to the message fields, not just describe the screen. When a stakeholder says just send the money, the analyst must ask what obligation it settles, what evidence proves it, and who bears the risk if it fails, because those answers decide the message, the reconciliation and the failure handling.
