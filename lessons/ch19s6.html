Key terms

Intraday liquidity: Cash available to a bank during the business day to settle payments as they fall due. Intraday liquidity comes from account balances, incoming payments and central bank facilities.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Queue: A holding area in a payment system where payments wait for sufficient funds or for their turn to settle.

Exercise

Work out how much intraday credit a bank can draw from its collateral, and what its fallbacks are.

A bank holds EUR 300 million of government bonds and EUR 100 million of high quality corporate bonds as eligible collateral. The central bank also operates a marginal lending facility where a bank can borrow overnight at a published rate, and a deposit facility where it can place surplus funds overnight.

  1. What to do. State how the collateral limits the credit, what happens if the intraday credit is not repaid, and what the standing facilities are for.

Correct result. The bank can draw intraday credit only up to the eligible collateral it holds, EUR 400 million in total across the two bond holdings, because the collateral requirement protects the central bank. It must repay the intraday credit before the close of business, and if it fails to repay, the credit may convert to overnight borrowing at a penalty rate. The standing facilities give it a predictable fallback: the marginal lending facility for overnight borrowing at a published rate, and the deposit facility for placing surplus funds overnight.

Central banks provide several facilities that help banks manage intraday liquidity. The most important is intraday credit, which is typically provided against eligible collateral, such as government securities or high quality corporate bonds. The bank pledges the collateral, draws the credit during the day, and repays it before the end of the day. If it fails to repay, the intraday credit may convert to overnight borrowing at a penalty rate. The collateral requirement protects the central bank, but it also means a bank's access to intraday credit is limited by the collateral it holds. Central banks also operate standing facilities, such as a marginal lending facility where a bank can borrow overnight at a published rate, and a deposit facility where it can place surplus funds overnight. These facilities set a corridor for short term interest rates and give banks a predictable fallback. In addition, many central banks conduct open market operations, buying or selling securities to add or drain liquidity from the system as a whole. A business analyst does not need to be a monetary policy expert, but should understand how these facilities affect the cost and availability of liquidity on a given day. Collateral management is a discipline in its own right. Banks must hold a portfolio of eligible securities, value them daily, manage haircuts, which are discounts applied to their value to allow for price movements, and ensure that enough collateral is available in the right place at the right time. If collateral is held in a securities account that is not linked to the payment system, it cannot be used for intraday credit without a transfer. Integrating collateral management with payment operations is therefore an important design consideration for any bank that settles high value payments. Price the collateral. A bank holds EUR 300 million of government bonds and EUR 100 million of high quality corporate bonds as eligible collateral. The central bank applies a haircut of 2 percent to the government bonds, giving EUR 294 million of usable credit, and 8 percent to the corporate bonds, giving EUR 92 million. Total intraday capacity is EUR 386 million. Now change one fact. The EUR 300 million of government bonds sits in a securities account that is not linked to the payment system. On paper the bank has EUR 386 million of capacity, but in practice it cannot draw a cent of it until the bonds are transferred to the linked account, which takes hours. That is the most common collateral failure in real operations, and it is an integration problem, not a shortage of assets. The standing facilities matter on the same day. Surplus funds placed overnight earn the deposit facility rate, and emergency overnight borrowing costs the marginal lending rate, so the corridor between the two rates sets the price of getting the day wrong. Collateral management deserves its own section in the design, not a footnote. The analyst should specify the daily valuation job, which reprices the collateral and recalculates the haircuts, the eligibility list, which is updated when the central bank changes its rules, and the linkage check, which confirms every morning that the collateral sits in accounts connected to the payment system. The dashboard should show usable intraday capacity, not just the face value of the bonds, because the face value overstates what the bank can actually draw. And the test pack should include a day when the collateral is deliberately held in the wrong account, to prove that the monitoring catches it before the payment queue does.
