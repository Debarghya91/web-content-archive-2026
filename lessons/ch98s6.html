Show transcript

Guardrails are the rules that decide where the model may act alone, where a human must approve, and how the handoff between the two works. Without them, an AI feature is just a model with a live connection to money, and that is not a product. Start by sorting decisions into three groups. First, the low-risk decisions the model may take alone, such as suggesting a category for a payment or drafting a message for the customer. Second, the medium-risk decisions where the model may recommend but a human must approve, such as releasing a payment that was held for review. Third, the high-risk decisions where the model may only advise and must never act, such as paying a new payee or changing a payment limit.

Take a concrete example. A model reviews a supplier payment of 22,000 pounds that has been flagged because the account details changed. The model can check the history, compare the new details, and show its reasoning, but it must not release the payment on its own. A named person in operations must approve it, and that approval must be recorded.

Design the handoff carefully. The human must see what the model saw, what it recommended, and how confident it was. They must be able to approve, reject, or send it back with a note, in one step, without retyping the payment.

Set a rule for uncertainty. If confidence is below the agreed level, the case always goes to a human, however small the amount. And test the handoff with real staff, not just in a demo, because a guardrail that is hard to use will be worked around, and a guardrail that is worked around is no guardrail at all.
