Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a $10,000 payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full amount. Under SHA the sender pays the sending charges and the beneficiary pays the receiving charges. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives the amount less all fees.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

Show transcript

The payment landscape is not only SWIFT and correspondent banking. Niche networks serve communities that the big commercial banks underserve, or serve differently: postal organisations, cooperative banks, and savings banks, whose customers are retail savers, small businesses, and communities. Two networks illustrate the pattern: Eurogiro, the network of postal organisations and their banking partners, and TIPANET, the network of cooperative banks. Both give their members international reach at shared cost, through a network built for their community.

Eurogiro connects postal organisations and banks for cross border payments. It was founded by European postal services, whose post office networks reach customers in every town and village, including places with no bank branch. Members exchange payment instructions electronically over the Eurogiro network, and settle through the arrangements the network provides. The payments are typically retail: remittances, small transfers, and payments from customers who use the post office as their financial access point. Eurogiro gives a postal organisation in one country the ability to pay a beneficiary through a postal organisation or bank in another, without building a correspondent network of its own.

TIPANET connects cooperative banks across countries for cross border payments and messaging. Cooperative banks are owned by their members, serve local communities and small businesses, and are numerous but individually small. A single cooperative bank cannot justify a global correspondent network, but the cooperative sector together can. TIPANET provides the shared network: member banks exchange payment instructions over it, and settle through correspondent accounts or central arrangements. The network lets a local cooperative bank offer its customers international payments, with the reach of a large bank and the cost base of a shared service.

Both networks exist for the same economic reason: shared infrastructure for institutions that are individually too small to build it alone. The network provides the messaging, the standards, the routing, and the settlement arrangements, and the members provide the customers and the local distribution. The cost is shared, the reach is collective, and the community keeps control of its own payment arrangements instead of depending entirely on the large commercial banks. The model is the payment equivalent of a cooperative: shared ownership, shared benefit.

How the networks work follows the same pattern as any payment network. A member institution takes a payment instruction from its customer, formats it to the network standard, and sends it over the network to the member institution that serves the beneficiary. Settlement follows through the agreed accounts: correspondent accounts, central settlement, or netting, depending on the network rules. The network provides the directories, the standards, and the operating rules, and the members provide the local account services. The customer sees a payment from their post office or their cooperative bank to a beneficiary abroad, and the network is invisible.

For a business analyst, niche networks are a reminder that the payment landscape has many layers. The right network for a payment depends on the customer, the corridor, and the amount: a large corporate payment travels by SWIFT and correspondent banking, a retail remittance may travel by Eurogiro, and a cooperative bank payment may travel by TIPANET. The analyst designing a payment product must know which networks the institution belongs to, which corridors each covers, and how the routing decision is made. Reach is a network question before it is a product question.
