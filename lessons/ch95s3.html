What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain when a sanctions hit fires and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. The sanctions hit workflow is the most time-critical operational process in payments, and it must be rehearsed.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how AML screening, sanctions hits, fraud detection, fraud case handling, APP scams, mule accounts, transaction monitoring and control operations are actually handled step by step inside a bank, and applies the workflows to realistic alerts and cases with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Minute zero
  • Most hits dying at this step
  • Investigation: documented step by step
  • Payment: held in suspense account
  • Customer told what happened and what the bank has done
  • Workflow requirements

Key terms

Suspense account: A temporary account used to hold funds or entries that cannot yet be posted to their final account. Banks use suspense accounts for unidentified receipts, pending investigations and items awaiting...

Queue: A holding area in a payment system where payments wait for sufficient funds or for their turn to settle.

Correct result. Hold the payment, compare the party details against the list entry, check for a licence or exemption, decide true match or false positive, and either block and report or release with the reason recorded.

Why. Every step must be evidenced, because the regulator will ask why the payment was released or blocked, and the answer is in the case file.

Minute zero is when the alert fires, and the first thing that happens is automatic: the payment is held. No one can release it manually, no one can override the hold from the payment screen, and the hold is recorded in the audit trail with the time, the score, and the list entry that matched. The alert enters the triage queue with a risk score and the matched list entry attached. The clock starts immediately. The service level for a high-score alert is typically 2 to 4 hours. For a low-score alert it is 24 hours. The service level is a commitment, not a target, and it is measured and reported, because a payment held beyond its service level is a customer complaint and a regulatory risk. Step one is triage, and it is done by the first-line analyst. The analyst opens the alert, reads the matched name, the score, and the list entry, and pulls the customer record. They compare the identifiers side by side: the full name, the date of birth, the nationality, the address, and the identity document number. Most hits die at this step. The date of birth does not match. The nationality does not match. The name is a common variant, such as a father and son with the same name. The analyst records the comparison, the conclusion, and the evidence, and if the conclusion is that the match is false, the alert is dispositioned as a false positive and the payment is released. The disposition must record the reason, because a false positive closed without a reason will be reopened by quality assurance. Step two is investigation, and it applies when the identifiers are close enough that triage cannot clear the alert. The analyst digs deeper: the account opening documents, the transaction history, the source of funds, and any open-source information that is available. They check whether the customer has other accounts, whether the payment fits the customer expected behaviour, and whether any other party to the payment is connected to the matched name. If the score is high, a second analyst reviews the case, which is the four-eyes control. The investigation is documented step by step, because the file is the evidence that the bank acted reasonably, and because a supervisor or a court may read it. Step three is the decision, and there are three outcomes. A false positive is released, with the reason recorded and the match whitelisted so that the same name does not alert again until the list entry changes. A true match against a blocked person means the payment is blocked and the funds are frozen, and the bank must report the blocking to the relevant authority. A true match against a person who is on a watchlist but is not blocked means the payment is rejected, the customer is told, and the rejection is recorded. The decision must be made by a person with the right authority: a junior analyst can clear a false positive, but only a senior analyst or the money laundering reporting officer can confirm a true match. Blocking mechanics are specific and they matter, because a blocking done wrong is itself a breach. The payment is held in a suspense account, not in the customer account and not in the bank own account, so that the funds are identifiable and cannot be moved. The customer account is flagged, so that no onward payment can be made from it without a further check. The freeze is reported to the authority: in the United Kingdom to the Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation, in the United States to OFAC, and the report states the amount, the parties, the list entry, and the date of the blocking. The customer is told that the payment is held, but is not told the reason in a way that reveals the list entry, because that information is restricted. Unblocking is the process for when a payment was blocked in error, and it must be as disciplined as the blocking. The bank evidences the error: the identifiers do not match, the list entry refers to a different person, and the evidence is documented. The funds are released from the suspense account to the intended destination, or returned to the payer if the payment can no longer proceed. The customer is told what happened and what the bank has done. The whitelist is updated so that the same match does not alert again. The record is kept, including the original blocking report and the unblocking decision, because the authority may ask why a blocked payment was released. Licence applications are the route when a payment is genuinely blocked but there is a legitimate reason to proceed. In the United Kingdom, the Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation issues general licences, which cover a class of payments, and specific licences, which cover a single payment. A common case is legal fees: a sanctioned person is entitled to legal representation, and the bank applies for a licence to pay the lawyer. Another is humanitarian: a payment for food or medicine to a sanctioned jurisdiction. The application states the parties, the amount, the purpose, and the licence sought. While the application is pending, the funds stay frozen. If the licence is granted, the payment proceeds under its conditions, and the bank records the licence number against the payment. For a business analyst, the workflow requirements are the clocks, the authorities, the documentation, and the audit trail. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a false positive is cleared inside the service level, a test where a true match is blocked and reported, a test where a blocked payment is unblocked with evidence, and a test where a licence application is tracked to a decision. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: the UK Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation guidance on licences; OFAC reporting requirements; the European Union guidance on the implementation of restrictive measures.
