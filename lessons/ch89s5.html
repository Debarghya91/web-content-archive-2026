What happens when a participant fails: default handling and loss sharing

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain what happens when a participant fails and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Default handling is the resilience test of a clearing system, and the analyst must know the rules.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how clearing systems work, distinguishes netting from gross settlement, computes multilateral net positions, explains settlement cycles, cut-offs, settlement accounts, intraday liquidity, queueing and gridlock resolution, and describes default handling and the RTGS versus deferred net settlement trade-off, with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Clearing system assuming its participants will settle
  • Payments already
  • Recalculation: mechanical
  • Remaining 2 million
  • Contrast with a gross settlement system
  • Acceptance criteria

Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Why. Loss sharing is agreed in advance, so the system can keep settling while the failure is handled.

A clearing system assumes its participants will settle. When one does not, the system must protect the rest, and the rules for doing so are written in advance, because there is no time to negotiate during a failure. The scenario is always the same: a bank in a netting system becomes insolvent, or has its licence revoked, or is put into resolution, before the settlement cycle completes. It has sent payments into the clearing, and it owes a net debit. The question is who bears the loss. The first step is declaration. The clearing house, or the central bank that operates the system, declares the participant in default, using the criteria in the system rules: insolvency, failure to fund a settlement, or a regulatory order. From that moment the failed bank's new payment instructions are not accepted. Payments already in the clearing are handled according to the rules, and the distinction between settled and unsettled matters. Under the EU Settlement Finality Directive, Directive 98/26/EC, a transfer order entered into a designated system before the insolvency is final and cannot be revoked by the insolvency administrator. That protection is the foundation of the whole default procedure. The second step is unwinding. The clearing house recalculates the multilateral net positions excluding the failed bank. Every payment to or from the failed bank is removed from the netting, and the surviving banks' net positions change. A bank that expected a net credit of 5 million from the cycle may find its net credit is now 2 million, because 3 million of it was due from the failed bank. A bank that expected a net debit of 4 million may find its net debit is now 6 million, because payments it sent to the failed bank no longer offset its receipts. The recalculation is mechanical, but the consequences are real, and the surviving banks must fund the new positions, sometimes at short notice. The third step is loss sharing. If the failed bank's net debit cannot be covered from its own funds, the loss falls on the survivors according to the system's default rules. Most systems maintain a default fund, also called a guarantee fund, built from contributions by all participants. The fund is used first. If the fund is not enough, the remaining loss is shared among the surviving participants, usually pro-rata to their volumes or to their exposures. A worked example shows the arithmetic. Five banks clear together. The failed bank owes a net debit of 12 million euro. It holds 2 million in its settlement account, so 10 million is uncovered. The default fund holds 8 million, which is used in full. The remaining 2 million is shared among the four surviving banks pro-rata to their clearing volumes: a bank with 40 percent of the volume pays 800,000 euro, a bank with 30 percent pays 600,000, and the other two pay 400,000 and 200,000. The loss is painful, but it is bounded, and the system settles the same day. Communication runs in parallel with the arithmetic. The clearing house notifies the surviving participants, the central bank, and the regulator, usually within the hour. The failed bank's customers are told their payments have not been made, and the payments are returned to the debtor banks so that the customers can be told and can make alternative arrangements. The regulator decides whether the failed bank's customers are protected by a deposit guarantee scheme, and the resolution authority decides whether the bank is wound down or resolved. The contrast with a gross settlement system is sharp. In an RTGS system there is no unwinding, because each payment settled individually and is already final. The risk in RTGS is different: a bank that has sent a payment cannot get it back if the receiving bank fails a minute later, and a bank that is waiting for an incoming payment that never arrives faces a liquidity shortfall, not a loss. That is why RTGS systems focus on liquidity management, while netting systems focus on default funds and loss sharing. For a business analyst, default handling is a set of requirements and a runbook. The requirements must state the default declaration criteria, the unwinding procedure, the default fund contribution rules, and the loss-sharing formula. The runbook must state who is called, in what order, and what is said to customers. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a participant is declared in default, the net positions are recalculated, the default fund is applied, and the settlement completes on the same day.
