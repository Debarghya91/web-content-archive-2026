Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Sanctions failures have produced some of the largest fines in banking history. Banks have paid billions of dollars for stripping payment messages of sanctioned country references, for processing payments through US dollar clearing on behalf of sanctioned parties, and for weak screening systems that missed obvious matches. In several famous cases, the problem was not that the bank lacked a screening engine, it was that staff deliberately altered payment messages to hide the sanctioned party, or that the bank's systems were configured to screen only some fields and not others. Regulators treat those as failures of culture and governance, not just technology, and the penalties reflect that view, including criminal charges against individuals in the most serious cases.

Enforcement action usually follows a pattern that BAs should understand. A regulator finds the failure, often through its own testing or a whistleblower, and then examines how long the weakness existed, how many payments were affected, whether the bank knew about the problem and what it did after discovering it. Voluntary self disclosure and genuine remediation reduce penalties, while concealment increases them. After a fine, the bank typically agrees to a consent order that requires independent testing of its screening systems, new governance structures, and regular reporting to the regulator for several years. This is why screening requirements always include testing, monitoring and management reporting, not just the matching logic.

The practical lessons for anyone building or improving payment systems are straightforward. Screen every field of the payment, including free text and remittance information, because sanctioned parties hide in the fields nobody checks. Keep the lists current and prove that updates are applied on time. Test the engine with known test cases, including tricky spellings and transliterations, and keep the evidence. Make sure alerts cannot be cleared without a documented reason, and that nobody can switch off screening for a single payment without a recorded approval. Above all, remember that a screening system is only as good as the people and processes around it, so invest in training, clear procedures and honest escalation routes.

The largest sanctions enforcement in history is the BNP Paribas case of 2014, and every screening analyst should know it. BNP Paribas pleaded guilty to violating US sanctions against Sudan, Iran and Cuba, and agreed to pay 8.9 billion dollars in penalties. The bank had processed thousands of payments for sanctioned entities, using a practice called stripping, where the sanctioned party's name was removed from the payment message so the screening system would not flag it. The bank's own employees knew about the practice, and the bank's compliance team had warned about it. The case is the reason every bank now screens the full payment message, including the fields that are not part of the standard message, and the reason stripping is a criminal offence.

The enforcement pattern is consistent: the regulator finds the breach, the bank pays the fine, the bank's licence is restricted, and the bank must implement a remediation programme. The remediation usually includes a new screening system, a new compliance team, an independent monitor, and a multi-year commitment to report to the regulator. The cost of the remediation is often larger than the fine, and the reputational damage is larger still. A bank that is fined for sanctions breaches will lose correspondent relationships, will find it harder to onboard customers, and will be subject to enhanced supervision for years.

The lessons for a payments BA are concrete. The first is that the screening system must screen the full message, including free-text fields, because the stripped name will be hidden in the free text. The second is that the compliance team must have the authority to stop a payment, and must not be overruled by the business. The third is that the audit trail must be complete, because the regulator will ask for it. The fourth is that the training must be real, because the employees who stripped the messages knew what they were doing, and the bank's culture allowed it. The requirements must state the screening scope, the compliance authority, the audit trail, and the training.
