What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain fraud detection in detail and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Fraud detection is a behaviour problem, and the analyst writes the rules that describe normal behaviour.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how AML screening, sanctions hits, fraud detection, fraud case handling, APP scams, mule accounts, transaction monitoring and control operations are actually handled step by step inside a bank, and applies the workflows to realistic alerts and cases with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Fraud rules engine
  • System records how a customer
  • Single account making 20 payments
  • New device
  • Signal contributes
  • Analyst decides release

Key terms

Velocity check: A fraud control that counts how many transactions a customer or card makes in a short period and flags unusual frequency.

Queue: A holding area in a payment system where payments wait for sufficient funds or for their turn to settle.

PSD2: Payment Services Directive 2, a European Union law that opened banking to third party providers, required strong customer authentication, and set rules for payment services.

Correct result. The velocity rule fires, the fraud system scores the behaviour, the account is stepped up for authentication or temporarily blocked, and the fraud team contacts the customer.

Why. Velocity is one of the strongest fraud signals because it captures behaviour, not just the payment itself.

A fraud rules engine is a library of rules, and each rule has four parts: a condition, a threshold, an action, and an owner. The condition says what to look for. The threshold says when it fires. The action says what happens: allow, challenge with additional authentication, or hold for review. The owner is the person accountable for the rule. A velocity rule might say: more than 5 payments to new payees in 24 hours triggers a hold. An amount rule might say: a payment above 50,000 pounds to a new payee triggers a challenge. A time rule might say: a payment at 3 in the morning from an account that has been dormant for a year triggers a hold. Rules are versioned and deployed under change control, and a new rule is first run in shadow mode, where it generates alerts that are recorded but do not affect payments, so that the team can measure its false positive rate before it goes live. Behavioural analytics is the layer that catches what rules miss, because it learns what normal looks like for each customer. The system records how a customer behaves: how fast they type, how they move the mouse, how they navigate the screens, how long they spend on each page, and in what order they complete a payment. A model builds a profile from this behaviour, and flags a session that deviates. A customer who normally pays two suppliers a month, always from the same device, always in the morning, and who suddenly pays fifteen new payees at midnight from a new device, is behaving differently, and the model scores that deviation. The model must be trained on the bank own population, because a model built on retail customers will misfire on corporate payments, and a model built on domestic payments will misfire on cross-border ones. Velocity checks are the single most effective fraud signal, because fraud moves fast and legitimate payments do not. The checks run on several dimensions at once: velocity per account, per device, per payee, and per IP address. The windows are typically 1 hour, 24 hours, and 7 days, and the thresholds are set per dimension. A single account making 20 payments in an hour is suspicious. A single device accessing 15 different accounts in a day is suspicious. A single payee receiving payments from 50 different accounts in a day is suspicious. A single IP address originating payments for 30 different customers is suspicious. The velocity check must run before the payment is released, because velocity after the fact is a report, not a control. Device signals are the fingerprint of the payment. Device fingerprinting collects the browser, the operating system, the screen resolution, the installed fonts, the plugins, and the time zone, and combines them into an identifier for the device. Device reputation asks whether this device has been involved in fraud before, either at this bank or in the consortium data shared between banks. A device that has been associated with three fraud cases in the past month is a high-risk device, even if the customer using it is genuine, because the device may be shared or compromised. A new device is itself a signal: a first payment from a device the bank has never seen is riskier than the hundredth payment from a known device. IP signals are the geography of the payment. IP reputation asks whether the address is a known proxy, a virtual private network exit, a Tor exit node, or a data centre, because fraudsters hide behind all four. Geolocation asks whether the country of the IP address matches the country of the customer, and whether the customer is travelling. A payment from an IP address in a high-risk jurisdiction, for a customer who lives in the United Kingdom and has never travelled there, is a strong signal. But the signal must be used with care: a customer on holiday, or using a corporate virtual private network, will also appear in an unexpected country, and a control that blocks every traveller will generate complaints. The IP signal is therefore combined with the device and behaviour signals, never used alone. How the signals combine is the risk score. Each signal contributes a weight, and the weights are set by the model. A new device might add 20 points. A high velocity might add 30. An IP mismatch might add 15. A payment to a new payee might add 10. The total score falls into bands: below 40 the payment is allowed, 40 to 70 it is challenged with additional authentication, above 70 it is held for manual review. The score must be explainable: the analyst who reviews a held payment must be able to see which signals contributed and by how much. A score that cannot be explained cannot be challenged, and a control that cannot be challenged will be overturned on appeal. Alert triage is the human part of the detection system. The alerts enter a queue, prioritised by score, with the highest scores first. The analyst opens the alert and sees the score, the signals that fired, the payment details, and the customer history. The service level depends on the risk: a high-score alert must be reviewed within an hour, a medium-score alert within a day. The analyst decides: release, challenge, or block, and records the reason. The feedback loop is what makes the system improve: every confirmed fraud case is fed back into the rules and the model, so that the next similar case is caught earlier. For a business analyst, the requirements are the rule governance, the model explainability, the service levels, and the feedback loop. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a velocity pattern generates an alert, a test where a new device plus a new payee scores above the hold threshold, a test where a high-score alert is reviewed inside its service level, and a test where a confirmed fraud case changes a rule.
