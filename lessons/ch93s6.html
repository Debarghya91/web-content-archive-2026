Key terms

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

SCT Inst: SEPA Instant Credit Transfer, a scheme for euro payments that settle within ten seconds at any time of day.

Show transcript

The European Payments Council, the EPC, is the body that writes and manages the SEPA scheme rulebooks, and understanding how it works is essential to understanding why SEPA changes when it does. The EPC is an association of payment service providers, not a regulator, and its authority comes from the banks that adhere to its schemes. It manages four schemes: SCT, SCT Inst, SDD Core and SDD B2B. Each scheme has a rulebook, and the rulebook is the contract that binds every participating bank to the same behaviour, the same timelines, the same message formats and the same exception handling.

A rulebook has a fixed structure, and knowing the structure saves hours when a change lands. The rulebook itself states the rules: who can participate, what the payment must contain, what the timelines are, and how exceptions are handled. The implementation guidelines state how to build it: the message formats, the field definitions, the character sets and the examples. The scheme management rules state how the scheme is governed: how change requests are made, how they are decided, and how a participant can be suspended or removed. When a bank receives a new rulebook version, the first task is to read the change log, which lists every rule that changed, because the changes are the work, not the whole document.

The change cycle is annual and it is consultative, which means banks can influence it but cannot ignore it. A change request can be submitted by any stakeholder, a bank, a vendor, a corporate, or a regulator, and it is reviewed by the Scheme Management Board. Change requests that are accepted go to a public consultation, typically open for 90 days, where any interested party can comment. After consultation, the board approves the final version, the rulebook is published, and banks are given an implementation period, usually several months, before the effective date. New rulebook versions typically take effect in November, which is why the fourth quarter is rulebook season for every European payments team.

Adherence is the legal act that makes a bank part of a scheme. A payment service provider signs an adherence agreement for each scheme it joins, and the EPC records it in the register of participants. Adherence is per scheme, so a bank can be in SCT and SDD Core but not in SCT Inst, and that choice is visible to every other participant. A bank can withdraw from a scheme, and the EPC can terminate a participant that breaches the rules, and both events are published so that counterparties know the bank is no longer reachable for that scheme. The register is the source of truth for reachability, and it is checked by clearing systems and by other banks when they route payments.

Recent changes show how the cycle works in practice. The SCT Inst amount cap was removed in the 2023 rulebook after a change request argued that the cap blocked legitimate business payments, and the removal took effect in November 2023. The Instant Payments Regulation forced rulebook changes in 2025 to align the scheme with the legal requirements on Verification of Payee and on price parity. Each change followed the same path: a request, a consultation, an approval, a publication, and an implementation deadline. A bank that misses the deadline is non-compliant with the scheme, and non-compliance is visible to its counterparties, because the counterparties see the old behaviour in the messages they receive.

The failure modes are organisational, not technical. A bank that does not track the consultation misses the chance to object to a change that harms its business, and then must implement a rule it argued against. A bank that reads the rulebook but not the implementation guidelines builds a message that is technically valid but fails the counterparty validation. A bank that implements the new rulebook on its payment hub but not on its corporate file channel will reject a valid customer file, and the customer will not know why. A vendor that does not update its software in time leaves its bank customers non-compliant on the effective date. The discipline is to assign an owner to each rulebook, to log every change request, and to test against the new version before it takes effect.

For a business analyst, rulebook work is a traceability exercise. Every requirement should cite the rulebook version and the rule number it implements, every change should be logged with its effective date, and every test should be run against the new version before it goes live. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment is validated against the current rulebook version, a test where a change request is logged and tracked, and a test where the adherence register is checked before a new corridor is opened.
