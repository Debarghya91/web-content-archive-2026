Key learning points

  • Automated top-up when the balance drops below 20 percent
  • Forecast: reviewed daily
  • Fair queue: no jumping ahead of earlier small payments

Key terms

Intraday liquidity: Cash available to a bank during the business day to settle payments as they fall due. Intraday liquidity comes from account balances, incoming payments and central bank facilities.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Instant settlement means the receiver bank is paid at the same time as the beneficiary is credited, rather than hours later in a net batch. In a prefunded model, each participating bank holds a balance in a settlement account at the central bank or with the scheme operator. When a bank sends a USD 1,000 payment, that amount is immediately deducted from its prefunded balance and added to the receiver bank balance. The transfer of value is therefore final within seconds, and the receiver bank can credit its customer without taking credit risk on the sender bank. This is very different from deferred net settlement, where banks settle only the net difference at the end of the day. Prefunding creates a liquidity cost, because money sitting in a settlement account cannot be used for lending or investment. A bank that processes 500,000 instant payments a day, with an average value of EUR 300, might need a prefunded balance of EUR 50 million to cover its peak outbound flow. Treasury teams monitor the balance continuously and top it up from the bank main account when it falls below a threshold. The top-up itself must be fast, because a depleted settlement account blocks all outbound payments. Some banks automate the top-up with rules that trigger when the balance drops below twenty percent of the daily limit. Liquidity forecasting is harder for instant payments than for batch payments, because the flow never stops. Treasury analysts study patterns by hour, by day of the week, and by season, and they build forecasts that predict the peak outflow. Salary days, tax deadlines, and festival periods all create surges that can drain a prefunded account in hours. A bank in India, for example, expects UPI volumes to spike sharply on festival days, when millions of customers send INR 1,001 as a gift within a few hours. The forecast is reviewed daily, and the prefunded balance is adjusted before the surge arrives rather than during it. When a settlement account runs low, the bank must slow down or queue payments rather than let them fail randomly. Most schemes define a clear rule, if the prefunded balance cannot cover a payment, the payment is rejected with a specific reason code, and the sender bank tells the customer to try again shortly. Some banks add an internal queue that holds payments for a few minutes while treasury tops up the balance, then releases them in order. The queue must be fair, so a large corporate payment of GBP 250,000 does not jump ahead of a thousand small retail payments that arrived earlier. Transparency matters, because a customer waiting for a payment needs to know it is queued, not lost. Intraday liquidity tools give central banks a way to support instant settlement without forcing banks to hold enormous idle balances. A central bank may offer intraday credit secured against collateral, which a bank draws in the morning and repays before the close of business. Some schemes also allow a bank to set a bilateral limit with another bank, capping the net exposure between them. The Bank of England, for example, supports Faster Payments settlement through prefunded accounts at the central bank, with facilities that let banks manage their positions during the day. The business analyst must understand these facilities, because a liquidity design that ignores them will either be too expensive or too fragile.
