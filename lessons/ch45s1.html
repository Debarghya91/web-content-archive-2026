What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain payment product manager actually does. A payment product manager owns a product from the first idea to the day it is retired, and is accountable for whether it makes money, serves customers well and stays within the rules.

Why it matters. This matters because this is the work a payments BA, PO or SME actually produces: requirements, mappings, stories, test cases and traceability. It is the job, not the theory.

Chapter objective: Learner applies product management to payments, defining a product vision, roadmap, metrics, and pricing, and writes a product brief for a new payment feature. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key learning points

  • Payment product manager
  • Daily work
  • Manager switching between commercial, customer and technical thinking
  • Product manager vs project manager
  • Good payment product manager
  • Product manager as the link between customer and bank

Key terms

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Show transcript

A payment product manager owns a product from the first idea to the day it is retired, and is accountable for whether it makes money, serves customers well and stays within the rules. For a product such as a corporate bulk payment service, the manager decides which customer segments it serves, which payment types it supports, what the price will be and what service levels the bank will promise. A product manager at a bank such as Barclays or HSBC may own a portfolio worth 40 million pounds in annual revenue, with 12,000 corporate customers using the service every day. The manager does not write code or process payments, but every decision about the product passes through them. The daily work is a mix of customer conversations, data analysis and decision making with other teams. On a Monday, the manager may meet a corporate treasurer who wants to send 8,000 payments a day through a single file, and hear that the current file format takes too long to prepare. On a Tuesday, the manager may review data showing that 12 percent of instant payments fail because the beneficiary name does not match the account, and ask whether a confirmation of payee check would fix the problem. On a Wednesday, the manager may meet compliance to discuss a new fraud rule, and meet technology to discuss the cost of building a new feature. The work is varied, and the manager must switch between commercial, customer and technical thinking in the same day. A product manager works with many teams, and success depends on influencing people who do not report to them. Technology builds the product, so the manager must explain the requirement clearly enough for engineers to build it correctly. Operations runs the product, so the manager must understand the exception queues and the cut-off times before promising a new service level. Risk and compliance approve the product, so the manager must show that the controls are adequate. Sales sells the product, so the manager must provide clear pricing and simple messages that a salesperson can use with a customer. A manager who ignores any one of these teams will find the product delayed, blocked or poorly received. The difference between a product manager and a project manager is the difference between owning the outcome and owning the delivery. A project manager is responsible for delivering a defined change on time and on budget, for example building a new mobile payment feature in six months for 1.2 million pounds. A product manager is responsible for deciding whether that feature should be built at all, whether customers will pay for it, and whether it will still be profitable in three years. A project ends when the feature goes live, while a product continues, with the manager watching usage, revenue and complaints every month and deciding what to change next. A good payment product manager combines four skills that are rarely found in one person. The first is customer understanding, which means listening to treasurers, finance directors and small business owners and hearing what they really need rather than what they ask for. The second is commercial judgement, which means knowing whether a price of 25 pence per payment will cover the cost and leave a margin. The third is technical literacy, which means understanding ISO 20022 messages, APIs and clearing systems well enough to challenge an estimate. The fourth is communication, which means writing a one page business case that a senior executive can approve in five minutes. Banks hire for this combination because the product manager is the link between the customer and the bank.
