Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Break: A difference found during reconciliation between two records that should agree, such as a bank statement and a ledger.

Show transcript

An authorised push payment scam works because the victim authorises the payment, and the fraudster script is designed to make that authorisation feel normal. The typologies are consistent. Investment fraud promises high returns and shows a fake platform where the victim balance appears to grow. Romance fraud builds a relationship over weeks or months and then asks for money for an emergency. Impersonation fraud pretends to be the bank, the police, or a government department, and tells the victim their money is at risk and must be moved to a safe account. Purchase fraud offers goods at a price too good to be true and asks for payment by bank transfer. In every case the fraudster creates urgency and secrecy: act now, do not tell anyone, the bank will block it if you mention it. The control that breaks the script is the warning at the point of payment, and the verification of payee check that shows the name does not match.

Mule networks are the infrastructure that moves the stolen money, and they are built like a business. Recruitment happens through job adverts, social media posts, and small payments: earn 500 pounds a week working from home, just receive payments and forward them. The recruit opens an account, or allows their existing account to be used, and receives the victim money. The mule keeps a small cut and forwards the rest to the next account in the chain. The roles are distinct: the recruiter finds the mules, the mule holds the account, the handler directs the money, and the cash-out converts it to cash or cryptocurrency. A single fraud can involve dozens of mule accounts, and the money moves through them in minutes, which is why detection must be fast.

Detection by graph analysis is how mule networks are found, because a single mule account looks normal and a network does not. The analysis links accounts by shared attributes: a common device, a common IP address, a common phone number, a common payee, or a common pattern of payments. A network of 50 accounts that all pay the same 3 accounts is a mule network, even if each individual account looks ordinary. A worked example makes it concrete. Fifty accounts each receive 5 payments from different victims, each payment between 2,000 and 5,000 pounds, and each account forwards 90 percent of what it receives to the same 3 accounts within 2 hours. No legitimate customer behaves like that. The graph shows the pattern in a single picture, and the picture is the evidence.

Dismantling a network is a coordinated action, and the order matters. The accounts are closed in a single operation, so that the network cannot warn itself and move the money. The closures are authorised in advance, with the evidence pack for each account. Law enforcement is given a referral with the graph, the payment records, and the device and IP evidence. Intelligence is shared with other banks through the fraud intelligence sharing system, so that the same mules cannot simply open accounts elsewhere. The timing is critical: the operation must happen quickly, because the money moves in minutes, and a network that is warned will cash out immediately. A dismantling that closes half the accounts and leaves the other half open has failed.

The reimbursement rules for APP fraud are the Payment Systems Regulator regime that applies from 7 October 2024, and the detail matters. The maximum reimbursement is 85,000 pounds per claim. The cost is split equally between the sending and the receiving payment service providers, which gives the receiving firm a direct incentive to detect mule accounts. Reimbursement must be made within 5 business days of the claim. The sending firm can only refuse where the victim acted with gross negligence, and gross negligence is a high bar: it means the victim ignored a clear warning, not that they were merely careless. The claim must be made within 13 months of the last payment. Vulnerable customers get extra care, and the gross negligence exception is applied differently for them. The claims process must be simple, and the customer must be told the outcome and the reason.

The receiving bank duty is the part of the regime that changed behaviour most. Before the regime, a receiving bank had little incentive to monitor the accounts that received fraud money, because the loss fell on the sending bank. Now the receiving bank pays half the loss, so it has a direct financial reason to detect mule accounts. The duty includes monitoring accounts for mule behaviour, acting on warnings from the sending bank, and sharing information about mule accounts. A receiving bank that ignores mule signals, or that onboards accounts without checking them, now pays for the fraud. The sending bank duty is to warn and to check: verification of payee, warnings at the point of payment, and transaction limits for new payees.

Prevention is the cheapest control, and it works in three places. Verification of payee stops the payment where the name does not match. Warnings at the point of payment break the fraudster script by telling the customer, in plain language, that the payment looks like a scam. Transaction limits for new payees slow the fraud down, so that a victim cannot send their life savings to a payee they added five minutes ago. Customer education helps, but it is the weakest control, because the fraudster script is designed to defeat it. For a business analyst, the requirements are the verification of payee check, the warning text and its triggers, the limits for new payees, and the reimbursement workflow. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a mule network is detected by graph analysis, a test where a network is dismantled in a single operation, a test where an APP fraud claim is reimbursed inside 5 business days, and a test where a warning is shown before a payment to a new payee.
