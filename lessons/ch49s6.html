Practise this lesson

Exercise

A beneficiary presents a signed demand stating that the applicant has defaulted, plus a copy of the unpaid invoice for 85,000 euros, as the standby requires. Work out what the bank must do, how long it has, and how much it pays.

Use the drawing process in this lesson: the documents required, the examination period, the independence principle, and the standby amount as a maximum.

  1. What to do. State whether the bank investigates the default, the deadline for examination, the amount paid, and what happens if the documents do not comply.

Correct result. The bank examines the documents only, and does not investigate whether the applicant really defaulted. If they comply, the bank must pay the 85,000 euros within a maximum of five banking days. If they do not comply, the bank must refuse and state each discrepancy. A partial drawing is possible if the standby permits it.

Why. The drawing rules protect both sides. The beneficiary gets paid on complying documents without a court case, and the bank gets a fixed examination window and a clear refusal duty.

Show transcript

A standby is used to guarantee payment of an advance. A buyer who pays 30 percent in advance for machinery may require the seller to provide a standby for the advance amount, so that the advance can be recovered if the seller fails to deliver. A buyer in Sydney may pay 150,000 Australian dollars in advance to a supplier in Vietnam, and hold a standby for the same amount until delivery is complete. The standby gives the buyer a fast remedy without a court case, which is vital when the supplier is in another country and legal action would be slow.

A standby is also used to guarantee performance in construction and service contracts. A project owner may require the contractor to provide a performance standby for 10 percent of the contract price, which the owner can draw if the contractor fails to finish the work. A 20 million dollar hospital project in Abu Dhabi may carry a 2 million dollar performance standby. A standby is also used for bid bonds, where a bidder provides a standby to show it will sign the contract if it wins the tender, and for lease guarantees, where a tenant provides a standby instead of a cash deposit.

A standby can guarantee a loan or a credit facility. A parent company may provide a standby to a bank that lends to its subsidiary, so that the lender can draw on the standby if the subsidiary defaults on the loan. A bank in London lending 3 million pounds to a subsidiary in Kenya may hold a standby issued by the parent bank in Nairobi. The standby is also used in trade to guarantee payment for goods delivered on open account, where the buyer pays 60 days after delivery and the seller holds a standby as security for that deferred payment.

A drawing starts when the beneficiary presents a demand and the required documents to the nominated bank or the issuing bank, at the place and before the expiry date stated in the standby. The beneficiary may present a signed demand stating that the applicant has defaulted, plus a copy of the unpaid invoice for 85,000 euros, as the standby requires. The presenting bank checks that the presentation is made in time, that the demand is in the required form, and that the documents are as listed. A presentation after expiry is not a valid drawing.

The bank examines the documents on their face, to see whether they appear to comply with the standby terms. The bank has a maximum of five banking days after the day of presentation to examine the documents and decide. It checks that the demand is signed as required, that the amount claimed does not exceed the available amount, and that each document is present and appears consistent. The bank does not check whether the applicant really defaulted in fact. If the documents comply, the bank must pay.

If they do not comply, the bank must refuse and state each discrepancy.
