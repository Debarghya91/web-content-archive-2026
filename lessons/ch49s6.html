What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain it is used and how banks process a drawing. A standby is used to guarantee payment of an advance.

Why it matters. If the documents comply, the bank must pay.

Chapter objective: Learner explains what a standby letter of credit is, distinguishes it from a commercial letter of credit, and describes issuance, notification, drawing and expiry. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Standby: used to guarantee payment
  • Standby guaranteeing performance in construction and service contracts
  • Standby guaranteeing a loan or a credit facility
  • Drawing starting with the beneficiary presenting a demand
  • Presentation after expiry
  • Document compliance triggering payment

Key terms

UCP 600: Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, revision 600, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs documentary credits.

ISP98: International Standby Practices 98, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs standby letters of credit.

Exercise

A beneficiary presents a signed demand stating that the applicant has defaulted, plus a copy of the unpaid invoice for 85,000 euros, as the standby requires. Work out what the bank must do, how long it has, and how much it pays.

Use the drawing process in this lesson: the documents required, the examination period, the independence principle, and the standby amount as a maximum.

  1. What to do. State whether the bank investigates the default, the deadline for examination, the amount paid, and what happens if the documents do not comply.

Correct result. The bank examines the documents only, and does not investigate whether the applicant really defaulted. If they comply, the bank must pay the 85,000 euros within a maximum of five banking days. If they do not comply, the bank must refuse and state each discrepancy. A partial drawing is possible if the standby permits it.

Why. The drawing rules protect both sides. The beneficiary gets paid on complying documents without a court case, and the bank gets a fixed examination window and a clear refusal duty.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A standby is used to guarantee payment of an advance. A buyer who pays 30 percent in advance for machinery may require the seller to provide a standby for the advance amount, so that the advance can be recovered if the seller fails to deliver. A buyer in Sydney may pay 150,000 Australian dollars in advance to a supplier in Vietnam, and hold a standby for the same amount until delivery is complete. The standby gives the buyer a fast remedy without a court case, which is vital when the supplier is in another country and legal action would be slow. A standby is also used to guarantee performance in construction and service contracts. A project owner may require the contractor to provide a performance standby for 10 percent of the contract price, which the owner can draw if the contractor fails to finish the work. A 20 million dollar hospital project in Abu Dhabi may carry a 2 million dollar performance standby. A standby is also used for bid bonds, where a bidder provides a standby to show it will sign the contract if it wins the tender, and for lease guarantees, where a tenant provides a standby instead of a cash deposit. A standby can guarantee a loan or a credit facility. A parent company may provide a standby to a bank that lends to its subsidiary, so that the lender can draw on the standby if the subsidiary defaults on the loan. A bank in London lending 3 million pounds to a subsidiary in Kenya may hold a standby issued by the parent bank in Nairobi. The standby is also used in trade to guarantee payment for goods delivered on open account, where the buyer pays 60 days after delivery and the seller holds a standby as security for that deferred payment. A drawing starts when the beneficiary presents a demand and the required documents to the nominated bank or the issuing bank, at the place and before the expiry date stated in the standby. The beneficiary may present a signed demand stating that the applicant has defaulted, plus a copy of the unpaid invoice for 85,000 euros, as the standby requires. The presenting bank checks that the presentation is made in time, that the demand is in the required form, and that the documents are as listed. A presentation after expiry is not a valid drawing. The bank examines the documents on their face, to see whether they appear to comply with the standby terms. The bank has a maximum of five banking days after the day of presentation to examine the documents and decide. It checks that the demand is signed as required, that the amount claimed does not exceed the available amount, and that each document is present and appears consistent. The bank does not check whether the applicant really defaulted in fact. If the documents comply, the bank must pay. If they do not comply, the bank must refuse and state each discrepancy.

A standby letter of credit is used when the beneficiary needs security for an obligation, and the applicant is willing to give a bank undertaking as that security. The most common uses are performance guarantees, financial guarantees, advance payment guarantees, bid bonds, and payment guarantees. A performance guarantee secures the performance of a contract, for example a construction contract. A financial guarantee secures the repayment of a loan. An advance payment guarantee secures the repayment of an advance payment if the supplier does not deliver. A bid bond secures a bid, and it is drawn if the bidder withdraws its bid or refuses to sign the contract. A payment guarantee secures the payment for goods or services. In each case the beneficiary holds the standby, and draws it only if the applicant fails.

The drawing process is as follows. The beneficiary presents a written demand to the issuing bank, or to the confirming bank, stating that the applicant has defaulted, and presenting any documents the standby requires. The demand must be presented before the expiry date, and it must comply with the standby terms. The bank examines the demand, and it has a maximum of five banking days to examine it, under UCP 600 and ISP98. If the demand complies, the bank pays the beneficiary the amount demanded, up to the standby amount. If the demand does not comply, the bank refuses to pay, and it must notify the beneficiary of the refusal and the reasons, within the five banking day period. The beneficiary may present a corrected demand before the expiry date, if time permits.

The documents required for a drawing are normally minimal. The standby will state the documents, and they are usually a written demand, signed by the beneficiary, stating that the applicant has defaulted and that the amount demanded is due. Sometimes the standby also requires a certificate of default, signed by the beneficiary, or a copy of an unpaid invoice, or a copy of a court order. The beneficiary should prepare the demand carefully, because a demand with a wrong amount, a wrong reference, or a missing signature will be refused. The bank examines the documents for compliance with the standby terms, not for the truth of the statement that the applicant defaulted. The bank does not investigate whether the applicant really defaulted, and it does not wait for a court to decide. It pays on the documents.

The examination period is five banking days, and it is a maximum, not a target. The bank should examine the demand as quickly as possible, and should pay or refuse within the period. If the bank does not respond within five banking days, it loses the right to refuse on the basis of a discrepancy, and it must pay. This is a strict rule, and it is a common source of error. A bank that takes six banking days to examine a demand, and then refuses it, will be liable to pay, because it missed the deadline. An SME who reviews a standby process should check that the system diaries the five banking day deadline from the date of presentation, and that it prompts the examiner to complete the examination in time.

The expiry of a standby is the last date a demand can be presented. A demand presented after the expiry date will be refused, even if the applicant defaulted before the expiry. The beneficiary must therefore diary the expiry date, and must present the demand before it. If the standby has an evergreen clause, the expiry will extend automatically unless the bank gives notice of non-extension. The beneficiary should check whether the standby is evergreen, and should ask the bank to confirm the current expiry date before it relies on the standby. A beneficiary that assumes a standby is still valid, when it has expired, will find that it has no security.

A partial drawing is possible if the standby permits it. The beneficiary may draw part of the amount, for example USD 200,000 of a USD 1,000,000 standby, and the standby remains available for the balance, until the expiry date or until the full amount is drawn. The standby should state whether partial drawings are permitted, and whether multiple drawings are permitted. A standby that permits only one drawing, for the full amount, is less flexible than a standby that permits multiple partial drawings. The beneficiary should check this term, and the bank should record the amount drawn and the balance available, so that it does not pay more than the standby amount.

For a business analyst, the drawing requirement must cover the presentation, the examination, the five banking day deadline, and the payment. The requirement should state that the system records the date and time of presentation, that it calculates the five banking day deadline, that it prompts the examiner to complete the examination before the deadline, that it records the amount drawn and the balance available, and that it generates the payment instruction when the demand complies. A system that cannot calculate the five banking day deadline, or that cannot record a partial drawing, cannot process a standby drawing safely.
