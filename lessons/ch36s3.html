Chapter objective: Learner applies system design to payments, covering throughput, latency, idempotency, exactly-once processing, and failure recovery, and designs a system for a stated load. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key learning points

  • Message queues, all available at same time
  • IBM MQ
  • Retry logic
  • Retry count
  • Error inspection: data corrected, resubmitted or returned
  • Released in priority order

Key terms

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Retry: Repeating a failed request or message delivery after a delay, usually with a limit on attempts and a back off period.

Queue: A holding area in a payment system where payments wait for sufficient funds or for their turn to settle.

Show transcript

Message queues exist because the systems in a bank do not all run at the same speed and are not all available at the same time. A channel may receive 10,000 payment instructions in a single minute during a payroll run, while the payment engine can only process 2,000 per minute. Without a queue, the extra 8,000 instructions would be rejected. With a queue, they wait safely and are processed in order. Queues also protect the bank when a downstream system is taken down for maintenance. The hub keeps accepting payments, stores them in the queue, and releases them when the system returns, so customers see no interruption. IBM MQ is the traditional queue technology in large banks, and it has been used for payment processing for more than twenty years. It guarantees that a message is delivered exactly once and in order, and it stores messages on disk so they survive a server restart. A payment placed on an IBM MQ queue will still be there after a power failure, which is essential when the message represents real money. Apache Kafka is the newer technology, and it is used where a bank needs to stream very large volumes of events, such as logging every status change of every payment for analytics. Kafka keeps a permanent record of events that many different systems can read, while IBM MQ is usually chosen where guaranteed single delivery of a payment instruction matters most. Retry logic decides what happens when a payment fails to reach its destination on the first attempt. A common pattern is exponential backoff, where the system waits a short time before the first retry, then waits twice as long before the second, and twice as long again before the third. A payment that fails at 10 in the morning might be retried at 1 minute past 10 in the morning, then at 3 minutes past 10 in the morning, then at 7 minutes past 10 in the morning, then at 15 minutes past 10 in the morning. This pattern gives a briefly overloaded system time to recover without being flooded by thousands of immediate retries. The retry count is capped, often at five attempts, and each attempt is logged so operations can see exactly what happened. A retry must never create a duplicate payment, so the hub checks the unique reference before sending again. When retries are exhausted, the payment moves to a dead letter queue, which is a special holding area for messages that could not be delivered. A payment lands in the dead letter queue for reasons such as a downstream system rejecting it repeatedly, a message format error that no retry can fix, or a missing piece of reference data. Operations analysts monitor the dead letter queue continuously, because every item in it represents a customer payment that has not moved. They inspect the error, correct the data if possible, and either resubmit the payment or return it to the customer. A bank that ignores its dead letter queue will quickly accumulate stuck payments and unhappy customers. When a downstream system is down for a long period, the bank follows a planned sequence. First, the hub stops releasing new payments to that system and holds them in the queue, while continuing to accept new instructions from customers. Second, operations confirms the size of the backlog and estimates the recovery time, and customer service is told what to say to callers. Third, if the outage passes a defined threshold, often two hours, the bank may switch to a contingency route, such as sending urgent payments through an alternative correspondent bank. Fourth, when the system returns, the queued payments are released in priority order, with urgent and high value payments first, and the backlog is cleared gradually so the recovering system is not overwhelmed.
