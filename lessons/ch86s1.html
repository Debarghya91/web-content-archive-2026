PSD2 vs PSD3 vs the Payment Services Regulation: what changes and when

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain PSD2 vs PSD3 vs the Payment Services Regulation and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. A business analyst must know which legal instrument applies, because a directive and a regulation create different implementation work.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the regulatory framework that binds payments in Europe, the UK, the US and India, distinguishes PSD2 from PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation, and applies DORA, the Instant Payments Regulation, sanctions regimes, AML rules, RBI directions and FFIEC guidance to realistic payment scenarios with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Second Payment Services Directive
  • Strong customer authentication reduced fraud
  • Regulation applies directly
  • Cash access
  • UK has its own timetable
  • Test evidence must be kept

Key terms

Authorisation: The licence a payment institution or electronic money institution must hold to provide payment services, granted by the competent authority under PSD3 after it checks the firm's governance, safeguarding and capital.

Open banking: A system where banks share customer account data and payment services with licensed third parties through secure APIs, with customer consent.

Refund: A repayment of funds to a customer after a payment fails, is cancelled or is disputed. In card payments a refund returns money to the cardholder account.

Exercise

A bank operates in Germany, France and Spain. Under PSD2 it maintains three slightly different authentication flows. Work out what changes under the Payment Services Regulation.

Use the rules, numbers and worked examples in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Work through the scenario and state the outcome with the reason.

Correct result. The conduct rules are the same in every member state, because the Payment Services Regulation applies directly without transposition. But national options, competent authority expectations and the technical standards for strong customer authentication still leave room for variation, so the bank must confirm the authentication flow in each country rather than assuming one flow works everywhere.

Why. A regulation applies directly, so the same rule means the same thing in every member state, which is the point of the PSR. But direct application does not remove every national difference, so the bank cannot assume one authentication flow works in every country.

Takeaway and next step

What to remember

PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation split the old directive in two: PSD3 is a directive for authorisation and supervision, and the Payment Services Regulation is a directly applicable regulation for conduct rules. The Second Payment Services Directive, Directive (EU) 2015/2366, known as PSD2, has governed European payments since January 2018. It did three big things. It opened bank accounts to licensed third parties through application programming interfaces, so a payment initiation service could start a payment from a customer account and an account information service could read balances and transactions. It made strong customer authentication mandatory for most electronic payments, requiring two independent factors from knowledge, possession and inherence. And it licensed a wider population of payment institutions and electronic money institutions, with conduct, safeguarding and reporting duties. Any payment firm operating in the European Economic Area still runs on PSD2 today, and the UK retained it in domestic law as the Payment Services Regulations 2017. PSD2 worked, but it worked unevenly, and the European Commission review that ran from 2021 to 2022 found the weak points. Strong customer authentication reduced fraud, but it also created friction, especially where banks applied it more strictly than the law required. Open banking interfaces varied in quality from bank to bank, and some third parties kept using screen scraping because the official interfaces were unreliable or slow. Refund rights for unauthorised payments were applied inconsistently, and the liability rules for fraud were unclear when a payment had passed through several intermediaries. Cross-border enforcement was patchy, because a directive must be transposed into national law and each member state did it slightly differently. The replacement package was proposed in June 2023 and it splits the old directive in two. PSD3 remains a directive and keeps the authorisation and supervision rules: who needs a licence, what capital they must hold, how supervisors cooperate. The Payment Services Regulation, the PSR, is a directly applicable regulation and holds the conduct rules that apply to firms and customers: strong customer authentication, refund rights, open banking access, fraud prevention and transparency. Because a regulation applies directly in every member state without transposition, the same rule will mean the same thing in every country, which is the single biggest structural change in the package. The concrete changes matter more than the structure. Strong customer authentication is refined, with clearer exemptions and a duty on payment service providers to share fraud data so that low-risk payments can skip the challenge. Verification of payee becomes a general duty under the PSR, so a payer can check that the name on an account matches the IBAN before sending money, which is the main defence against authorised push payment fraud. Verification of payee is already mandatory for euro transfers under the Instant Payments Regulation (EU) 2024/886 since October 2025; the PSR extends the name check to all credit transfers. Open banking access is strengthened with a duty on banks to provide a single dedicated interface and a dashboard where customers can see and revoke the permissions they have granted. Non-bank payment service providers gain a right of access to payment systems, which removes a barrier that kept smaller firms dependent on a sponsor bank. Cash access is protected, so banks cannot withdraw cash services without an assessment. The status and the dates are the part a business analyst must track week by week. The package was proposed in June 2023 and moved through the Council and the Parliament during 2024 and 2025. The application date will be set in the final text, with a transition period after entry into force, and firms should plan on the basis that the new rules will bite in the late 2020s. The practical approach is to build a gap analysis now: list every PSD2 obligation in the current product, map each one to the PSD3 and PSR articles that replace it, and mark which obligations change, which stay the same, and which are new. The gap analysis is the document the board and the supervisor will ask for first. The UK adds a second track that a firm operating in both markets must satisfy. The UK kept PSD2 as the Payment Services Regulations 2017, and the Financial Conduct Authority supervises it, while open banking in the UK rests on a separate Competition and Markets Authority order. The UK has its own timetable for reform. The Future Regulatory Framework Review concluded with the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023, and the live UK work is the National Payments Vision and the review of the Payment Services Regulations. UK rules on strong customer authentication, refunds and open banking already differ in detail from the EU. A payment that crosses from London to Frankfurt therefore passes through two rulebooks, and the product must satisfy both wherever they apply. Where the two regimes impose incompatible requirements, the conflict must be escalated rather than resolved by picking the stricter rule. For a cross-border payment this means the authentication flow, the refund clock and the information given to the customer must all be checked against both regimes. For a business analyst, the PSD3 and PSR work is a requirements discipline with a legal clock. Every obligation must be traced to a system control: the authentication challenge, the exemption logic, the verification of payee check, the permission dashboard, the refund workflow. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a low-risk payment passes without a challenge, a test where a high-risk payment is challenged, a test where a verification of payee mismatch warns the payer and records the decision, and a test where a refund request is decided inside the statutory period. The test evidence must be kept, because the supervisor can ask for it, and because a rule that is not tested is a rule that will fail in production.
