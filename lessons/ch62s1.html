Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A SEPA Direct Debit is a payment in which the creditor pulls funds from the debtor account, under a mandate that the debtor has signed in advance. The creditor initiates the collection, the debtor bank checks the mandate and the account, and the funds move from debtor to creditor on the due date. This reversal of the usual direction is what makes direct debits useful for recurring payments such as utilities, subscriptions and rent, because the creditor does not have to wait for the debtor to remember to pay.

The mandate is the legal basis for the whole flow, and without a valid mandate the collection must not happen. The SDD Core scheme is the scheme for all debtors, including consumers, and it is the scheme used in our worked example. Under Core, a debtor can claim a refund within eight weeks of the debit, with no questions asked, and within thirteen months if the collection was unauthorised. That refund right protects consumers who may not notice a wrong collection immediately, and it means the creditor carries the risk that a collection can be reversed weeks after the money arrived.

Core is the default scheme, and any creditor that collects from consumers must use it. The SDD B2B scheme is the scheme for business debtors only, and it works differently in three important ways. First, the debtor bank must check that a valid mandate exists before it allows a B2B collection, and it must refuse the collection if no mandate is on file. Second, the debtor has no eight week no questions asked refund right, so a business that wants its money back must agree the return with the creditor.

Third, the debtor must inform its bank that it accepts B2B collections, and the bank must record that agreement. B2B suits businesses that want certainty that a collection will not be reversed, and it suits creditors that collect large amounts from other businesses. The worked example for this chapter is a monthly utility collection in Germany. Stadtwerke Linden, a municipal utility in Hanover, supplies electricity to a household customer named Jonas Weber. Jonas Weber signed a mandate online, authorising Stadtwerke Linden to collect EUR 89.99 each month from his account with Meridian Bank Munich.

The mandate reference is SWL-JW-2026-0417, the creditor identifier is DE98ZZZ00001234567, and the collection is due on the first working day of each month. The next collection is due on Monday 2 November 2026, and this chapter follows it from mandate to settlement.
