What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain notification, advising and confirmation. Notification starts when the issuing bank sends the standby to the advising bank.

Why it matters. This matters because cross-border payments involve more banks, more charges, more time and more ways to fail than any domestic payment. Getting the chain right is the job.

Chapter objective: Learner explains what a standby letter of credit is, distinguishes it from a commercial letter of credit, and describes issuance, notification, drawing and expiry. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Standby transfer
  • Advising: the advising bank delivers the standby
  • Advising without any undertaking to pay
  • Confirmation before signing
  • Amendments to a standby travel by MT767

Key terms

Documentary credit: A letter of credit, a bank undertaking to pay a seller when the seller presents documents that comply with the credit terms.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Reject: A payment instruction that is refused before settlement because it fails validation, screening or account checks.

Exercise

An issuing bank sends a standby to an advising bank, which must authenticate it before delivering it to the beneficiary. The beneficiary is considering asking a second bank to confirm the standby. Explain what the advising bank must do if it cannot establish authenticity, and what confirmation would add.

Use the notification, advising and confirmation rules in this lesson.

  1. What to do. State the advising bank duty on failed authentication, and state what a confirming bank undertaking means for the beneficiary.

Correct result. If the advising bank cannot establish authenticity, it must tell the issuing bank at once and must not advise the beneficiary. Confirmation adds a second bank payment undertaking to the standby, in addition to the issuing bank undertaking, so the beneficiary holds two bank promises instead of one.

Why. Advising without authentication would hand the beneficiary a document it cannot rely on. Confirmation is a credit decision by the confirming bank, and it changes who the beneficiary can call on for payment.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Notification starts when the issuing bank sends the standby to the advising bank. In SWIFT terms, the issuing bank sends an MT760, which is the message type for the issue of a documentary credit, and the standby is a form of documentary credit. The MT760 contains the full undertaking, the amount, the expiry date, the place of presentation, and the documents required. The advising bank receives the message through the SWIFT network, where authentication is built into the system, and it must verify that the message is genuine before it passes it on to the beneficiary. Advising means the advising bank delivers the standby to the beneficiary. The advising bank uses reasonable care to check the apparent authenticity of the message, and it must advise the standby exactly as received, without changing the text. If the advising bank cannot establish authenticity, it must tell the issuing bank at once and must not advise the beneficiary. A bank in Paris advising a standby issued by a bank in Jakarta will check the SWIFT authentication and the issuing bank details before it forwards the text to the French beneficiary by secure mail or online banking. An advising bank may advise the credit without any undertaking to pay. That is the normal case. The beneficiary must understand that the advising bank name on the advice does not mean that bank will pay. If the advising bank is asked to confirm the standby, it makes a separate decision, based on the issuing bank credit risk, the country risk, and its own limits. Confirmation is not automatic, and the advising bank may decline to confirm even though it is willing to advise. The beneficiary should ask for confirmation before the deal is signed, not after. When a bank agrees to confirm, it sends its confirmation to the beneficiary, often through a MT767 amendment or a separate advice, and it adds its own undertaking to honour a complying presentation. The beneficiary can then present documents to the confirming bank in its own country, and be paid in local time and local practice. A beneficiary in Mexico may prefer confirmation by JPMorgan Chase in New York on a standby issued by a bank in Argentina, for 900,000 dollars, because it trusts the confirming bank and can present documents without sending them abroad. Amendments to a standby travel by MT767, and the same advising and confirmation rules apply. The issuing bank may amend the amount, extend the expiry date, or change the documents required, but an amendment is only effective when the beneficiary accepts it. A beneficiary who receives an MT767 reducing the amount from 500,000 dollars to 300,000 dollars can reject it, and the original terms remain in force. The beneficiary should check every amendment against the underlying agreement, because a silent acceptance can remove protection that the beneficiary still needs.

Notification is the process by which the issuing bank tells the beneficiary that a standby has been issued, and advising is the process by which the advising bank delivers the standby to the beneficiary. The two words are often used together, but they are different steps. The issuing bank notifies the advising bank by sending the MT700. The advising bank advises the beneficiary by delivering the standby. The beneficiary must receive the advice before it can rely on the standby, and it must examine the advice to confirm that the terms are correct.

The advising bank authenticates the standby before delivering it. It checks that the MT700 comes from the issuing bank, that the SWIFT authentication is valid, and that the message is complete. If the MT700 is received by a channel other than SWIFT, for example by telex or by letter, the advising bank must authenticate it by other means, such as a test key or a signature verification. The advising bank should not deliver a standby it cannot authenticate, because the beneficiary might rely on a forged standby. The authentication is a control, and it protects the beneficiary and the advising bank.

The beneficiary, on receiving the advice, should examine the standby carefully. It should check the amount, the expiry date, the drawing conditions, the governing rules, and the name of the issuing bank. It should check that the beneficiary name is correct, that the applicant name is correct, and that the currency is correct. If any term is wrong, the beneficiary should ask the applicant to arrange an amendment immediately, before it relies on the standby. A beneficiary that accepts a standby with a wrong amount or a wrong expiry date may find that it cannot draw the full amount, or that the standby expires before it can draw. The examination should be done on receipt, not when the beneficiary needs to draw, because by then it may be too late to amend.

Confirmation is the addition of a second bank payment undertaking to the standby. A beneficiary asks for confirmation when it does not trust the issuing bank, or when the issuing bank is in a country with political or transfer risk, or when the beneficiary wants to be paid by a bank in its own country. The confirming bank examines the demand and pays the beneficiary, and then claims reimbursement from the issuing bank. Confirmation costs a fee, normally a percentage per annum on the amount, and it is charged to the applicant or to the beneficiary, as agreed. The confirming bank must be authorised by the issuing bank to confirm, or it must agree to confirm without authorisation, which is called silent confirmation. Silent confirmation is a confirmation that the issuing bank does not know about, and it is a risk for the confirming bank, because the issuing bank may not reimburse it if the standby terms are not followed.

The decision to confirm is a credit decision by the confirming bank. It assesses the issuing bank creditworthiness, the country risk, and the standby terms. If it confirms, it takes on the risk that the issuing bank will not reimburse it. The confirming bank therefore charges a fee that reflects the risk, and it may require collateral from the issuing bank or from the applicant. A beneficiary that asks for confirmation should understand that it costs money, and that the confirming bank may refuse if the issuing bank is weak or the country risk is high. A standby that cannot be confirmed is a standby that the beneficiary should examine carefully, because it may indicate a problem with the issuing bank.

The advising bank may also be asked to confirm, and it may agree or refuse. If it agrees, it becomes the confirming bank as well as the advising bank. If it refuses, the beneficiary may ask another bank to confirm. The confirmation is added by an MT700 with field 49 stating CONFIRM, or by a separate MT799 free format message. The beneficiary should receive a clear statement that the standby is confirmed, and by which bank, because a confirmation that is not documented is not a confirmation. An SME should check the field 49 in the MT700, and should confirm that the standby advice states the confirmation explicitly.

For a business analyst, the notification and advising requirement must cover the authentication, the delivery, the examination, and the confirmation. The requirement should state that the system authenticates the MT700 before advising, that it records the date the beneficiary received the advice, that it prompts the beneficiary to examine the terms, and that it records whether the standby is confirmed and by which bank. A system that cannot record a confirmation, or that cannot distinguish an advised standby from a confirmed standby, will misstate the risk to the beneficiary.
