What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain reconciliation between ledgers and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Reconciliation is the detective control that catches everything else, and the analyst defines the break types.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how a bank's ledgers record every payment, distinguishes customer accounts, internal accounts, nostro and vostro ledgers, suspense and transit accounts, writes the exact general ledger postings for outgoing and incoming payments, and explains intraday processing, value dating, subledger feeds, reconciliation and error correction, with correct terminology.

Key terms

Nostro reconciliation: Reconciliation of a bank nostro account against the statement received from the correspondent bank. Operations teams match each nostro entry to a statement entry, investigate breaks, and confirm the...

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Reconciliation is the daily proof that two records of the same money agree, and it is the control that catches almost every error a bank makes. The bank's nostro mirror is reconciled against the correspondent's statement. The general ledger is reconciled against each subledger. The customer ledger is reconciled against the payment hub. The settlement account is reconciled against the clearing system. The suspense account is reconciled against its list of open items. Each reconciliation compares two sources, matches what agrees, and lists what does not, and the unmatched items are called breaks. A bank that reconciles daily finds its errors within a day. A bank that reconciles weekly finds them a week late, and by then the money may have moved. Nostro reconciliation is the most important of all, because it involves money held by another bank. Each morning the reconciliation system loads the correspondent's statement, usually an MT950 or MT940 message or a camt.053 statement, and matches it against the bank's own nostro mirror. Most items match on amount, value date and reference. The breaks fall into four types. Timing breaks are items that are on one side but not the other because of a value date difference, and they usually clear the next day. Amount breaks are items where the amounts differ, usually because the correspondent deducted a charge. Missing items are payments the bank sent that the correspondent has not recorded, or payments the correspondent recorded that the bank has not. Duplicates are the same payment recorded twice, which is the most dangerous type because it means the money may have been paid twice. A break between the general ledger and a subledger means the summary and the detail disagree, and the cause is usually a failed posting or a manual journal. The payment hub posts a daily summary of 10,000 Faster Payments worth 50 million pounds, but the general ledger shows 49.9 million pounds, a difference of 100,000 pounds. The investigation starts with the control totals: did the hub send the full file, did the general ledger receive it, and did any item fail validation. A single payment of 100,000 pounds may have failed because its value date was a bank holiday, so it sits in the hub's queue while the general ledger has no record of it. The fix is to post the missing item, not to adjust the total. Breaks are aged, and ageing drives escalation. A break that is one day old is usually a timing difference and is watched. A break that is five days old is investigated by the reconciliations team. A break that is 30 days old is escalated to management, reported to the audit committee, and may need a provision in the accounts, because money that cannot be accounted for after a month may never be found. Unreconciled items can also hide fraud: a payment that was diverted to a fraudster's account will appear as a break, because the money left the bank but never arrived where it should have. This is why regulators expect daily nostro reconciliation and timely break clearance, and why the Financial Conduct Authority asks firms how old their oldest break is. Investigating a break is detective work with a fixed method. First, find the transaction in both systems and confirm it is the same item. Second, check the value date, because most breaks are timing. Third, check the amount and the charges, because correspondents deduct fees that the bank may not have expected. Fourth, check whether the item is a duplicate, by searching for the same reference and the same amount on the same day. Fifth, contact the counterparty if the item is genuinely missing on their side. The tools are the reconciliation system, the payment log, the statement, and the reference data. A good reconciliation system stores the history of each break, so the investigator can see whether the same break has happened before. Clearing a break means posting a correcting entry that is linked to the break, never deleting anything. If a charge of 15 dollars was deducted by the correspondent and not expected, the bank posts a charge to the customer or absorbs it, and the break is marked cleared with the entry reference. If a break cannot be resolved, for example a rounding difference of 50 pence from a legacy system, it is written off, but only with approval, and the write-off is recorded with the reason and the approver. For a business analyst, the requirements must state the matching rules, the ageing thresholds, the escalation path, and the write-off authority. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a nostro statement arrives with a timing break, an amount break and a duplicate, and each one is resolved by the correct method.
