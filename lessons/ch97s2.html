Exercise

Trace the payment in this lesson through its lifecycle stages.

Use the lifecycle stages in this lesson: acceptance, processing, clearing, settlement, beneficiary credit, ledger posting and reconciliation.

  1. What to do. Name each stage, state what happens, and say when the payment is truly final.

Correct result. Acceptance validates the instruction. Processing prepares it. Clearing sorts and nets it. Settlement moves the funds. Beneficiary credit puts the money in the creditor account.

Ledger posting records it. Reconciliation proves it. Finality is only at settlement, not at acceptance or at status update.

Why. The lifecycle is the map of a payment. If you cannot say which stage a payment is in, you cannot answer where the money is.

User stories are a good job for AI, because the format is simple and the tool has seen thousands of examples, but the acceptance criteria are where the value lives, and that is where you must check hardest. Start with good inputs. Give the tool the persona, the goal, and the context, not just a one-line request.

Instead of saying write a story about payment tracking, say this. As a corporate treasurer, I want to see the status of my payment of EUR 45,000 to a supplier in Singapore, so that I can confirm it arrived before I release the goods. Now the tool has something to work with, and it can produce a story with a real actor, a real amount, and a real reason.

A good AI draft will give you the story in the standard format, and then a list of acceptance criteria. Your job is to test every criterion with one question. Can I test this, yes or no, with a real payment. A criterion that says the status is shown clearly is not testable, because clearly means different things to different people. A criterion that says the status shows as received within two seconds of the screen loading, for a payment of EUR 45,000, is testable.

A criterion that says the user is notified when the payment settles is not testable until you add how, by email or in the portal, and within how long. AI will happily write ten criteria that all sound good and none of which can be tested, and a developer will then build whatever they think clearly means. So take the AI draft, rewrite every vague criterion into a number, a time, a state, or a message, and delete any criterion you cannot test.

Using AI to Write User Stories and Acceptance Criteria

User stories are a good job for AI, because the format is simple and the tool has seen thousands of examples, but the acceptance criteria are where the value lives, and that is where you must check hardest. Start with good inputs. Give the tool the persona, the goal, and the context, not just a one-line request.

Instead of saying write a story about payment tracking, say this. As a corporate treasurer, I want to see the status of my payment of EUR 45,000 to a supplier in Singapore, so that I can confirm it arrived before I release the goods. Now the tool has something to work with, and it can produce a story with a real actor, a real amount, and a real reason.

A good AI draft will give you the story in the standard format, and then a list of acceptance criteria. Your job is to test every criterion with one question. Can I test this, yes or no, with a real payment. A criterion that says the status is shown clearly is not testable, because clearly means different things to different people. A criterion that says the status shows as received within two seconds of the screen loading, for a payment of EUR 45,000, is testable.

A criterion that says the user is notified when the payment settles is not testable until you add how, by email or in the portal, and within how long. AI will happily write ten criteria that all sound good and none of which can be tested, and a developer will then build whatever they think clearly means. So take the AI draft, rewrite every vague criterion into a number, a time, a state, or a message, and delete any criterion you cannot test.
