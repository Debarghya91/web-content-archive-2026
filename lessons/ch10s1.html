Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

Exercise

Decide which settlement model fits the scenario in this lesson and explain why.

Use the settlement models, the liquidity trade-off and the finality rules in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

The United States runs its domestic payments on a mix of old and new systems, and the mix can confuse anyone who expects a single national design like the one India built around NPCI. In the US, the Federal Reserve operates Fedwire and FedNow and also operates the FedACH service, while a private organisation called Nacha governs the ACH network rules, and a private company called The Clearing House operates CHIPS and the RTP network. Commercial banks connect to several of these systems at once, and a payment can travel through more than one of them before it reaches the beneficiary.

A business analyst working on US payments must learn which operator runs each system, because the operator decides the rules, the hours and the support model. The four systems in this chapter serve different needs, and the differences are sharper than they first appear. Fedwire is the Federal Reserve's real time gross settlement system for high value, time critical payments, and it has been running since 1918 in various forms. FedNow is the Federal Reserve's newer instant payment service, launched in 2023, which lets banks send and receive payments in seconds around the clock.

ACH is the batch network that moves payroll, direct debits, bill payments and government benefits in files, and it is the highest volume system in the country. CHIPS is the private sector system that settles large value payments, many of them connected to international business, by netting them through the day. One feature of the US system that surprises analysts from other countries is that there is no single national account identifier like an IBAN or a VPA. US payments are addressed with a routing number, which identifies the bank, and an account number, which identifies the account at that bank.

The routing number is nine digits long and is managed by the American Bankers Association. This addressing is simple but it creates its own risks. A wrong routing number sends the payment to the wrong bank, and a wrong account number sends it to the wrong customer. Validation of routing numbers and account numbers, and the use of services that confirm the account holder name, is therefore a standard part of US payment design.
