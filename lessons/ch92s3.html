Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

The Automated Clearing House, the ACH, is the batch electronic payment network of the United States, and it is the system that moves payroll, direct debits, business-to-business payments and government benefits. It is governed by the NACHA Operating Rules, and it is operated by two operators, FedACH, run by the Federal Reserve, and the Electronic Payments Network, run by The Clearing House. The ACH processes around 30 billion payments a year, with a total value of around 80 trillion dollars, and the average payment is therefore a few thousand dollars. It is the cheapest payment system in the United States, and it is the system that a business uses when it does not need the money to move in seconds. The file structure. An ACH payment does not travel alone. It travels inside a file, and the file has a strict structure. The file header record identifies the originator and the file. Each batch header record identifies a batch of entries that share a service class, an effective entry date, and a company name. Each entry detail record is one payment, with the receiving bank's routing number, the account number, the amount, and the individual identification. Each batch ends with a batch control record that totals the entries, and the file ends with a file control record. If any total does not match, the file is rejected. A business analyst must understand this structure, because most ACH failures are file failures, a batch total that does not add up, a routing number that does not exist, or an effective entry date that has already passed. SEC codes. Every ACH entry carries a Standard Entry Class code that says what kind of payment it is. PPD is a prearranged payment and deposit, the code for payroll credits and consumer debits. CCD is a cash concentration or disbursement, the code for business-to-business payments. CTX is a corporate trade exchange, a CCD that can carry up to 9,999 addenda records, used for payments that need detailed remittance data. WEB is an internet-initiated entry, used when a customer authorises a payment on a website. TEL is a telephone-initiated entry. IAT is an international ACH transaction, used when any party to the payment is outside the United States. RCK is a re-presented check entry, used when a paper check is converted to an electronic debit. POP, ARC and BOC are the check conversion codes for point of purchase, accounts receivable and back office conversion. The SEC code matters because the rules are different for each one. A WEB debit has different authorisation requirements from a PPD debit, and an IAT entry has additional screening and reporting obligations. The roles. The originator is the company or person that initiates the payment. The Originating Depository Financial Institution, the ODFI, is the originator's bank, and it takes the file, warrants that the entries are authorised, and sends the file to the operator. The Receiving Depository Financial Institution, the RDFI, is the receiver's bank, and it posts the entry to the receiver's account. The operator sorts the entries and delivers them. The ODFI bears the risk. If an entry is unauthorised, the ODFI is responsible, and that is why the ODFI must know its originators, must monitor their return rates, and must be able to stop an originator that is generating bad entries. Returns and notifications of change. When an ACH entry cannot be posted, the RDFI returns it with a return reason code. R01 means insufficient funds. R02 means the account is closed. R03 means no account exists, or the account number does not match. R07 means the authorisation was revoked by the customer. R10 means the customer advises that the entry was not authorised. Most returns must be sent within two banking days, but an unauthorised consumer debit can be returned for up to 60 days, and that 60 day window is the largest risk in the ACH system. A company that debits a consumer's account without a valid authorisation can see the money pulled back two months later. NACHA monitors return rates, and an originator whose unauthorised returns exceed 0.5 percent, or whose administrative returns exceed 3 percent, will be investigated. A notification of change, an NOC, is different from a return. It tells the originator that the account details have changed, for example the account number is wrong, and the originator must correct its records within six banking days or before the next entry, whichever is sooner. Worked example. A company runs a payroll of 4,800 employees with a total value of 6.2 million dollars. The payroll file has one batch, SEC code PPD, effective entry date Friday. The ODFI validates the file, checks the batch total, and sends it to FedACH on Wednesday. FedACH delivers the entries to the RDFIs on Thursday, and the employees are paid on Friday. Twelve entries are returned. Eight are R01, insufficient funds, because the employees' accounts were overdrawn. Three are R03, because the account numbers were mistyped. One is R07, because the employee had revoked the direct deposit authorisation when they left the company. The company corrects the three mistyped account numbers, reissues the eight R01 entries after confirming the accounts are funded, and does not reissue the R07. The RDFI sends a notification of change for two employees whose accounts were merged, and the company updates its payroll system within six banking days. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: NACHA Operating Rules and Guidelines; Federal Reserve, FedACH service description; The Clearing House, Electronic Payments Network rules.
