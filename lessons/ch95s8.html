Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Controls fall into three kinds, and a payment operation needs all of them. Preventive controls stop the bad thing from happening: maker and checker, payment limits, authentication, and system-enforced segregation of duties. Detective controls find it after it has happened: reconciliations, transaction monitoring, exception reports, and audits. Corrective controls fix it: incident response, remediation, and re-testing. The balance matters. A operation with only preventive controls will miss the fraud that gets through, because no preventive control is perfect. An operation with only detective controls will find the fraud after the money is gone. The right balance is preventive controls on the high-risk actions, detective controls on everything, and corrective controls that are tested and ready.

Maker and checker in practice is a workflow, not a principle. The maker creates the payment, the payee, or the limit change. The checker is a different person who reviews it and approves or rejects it. The system enforces the separation, so that the same person cannot approve their own work even if they try, and the enforcement covers all channels, including the emergency procedures. The checker duty is real: the checker must verify the payment details, the payee, the amount, and the supporting evidence, not just click approve. The failure mode is rubber-stamping, where the checker approves without reading, and it is detected by measuring approval times and approval rates: a checker who approves 50 payments in 5 minutes is not checking. The remedy is sampling, where a proportion of approved items is re-reviewed, and feedback to the checker.

Dual authorisation in practice has thresholds and duties. The threshold is set by value and by risk: a payment above 100,000 pounds needs two authorisers, a payment to a new payee needs two authorisers whatever the amount, and a payment outside the normal pattern needs two authorisers. The second authoriser duty is independent verification: they must check the payment details themselves, not rely on the first authoriser. When the second authoriser is unavailable, the escalation must be to another authorised person, never to a bypass. The emergency procedure, for use when the system is down, must still require two authorisers, and every emergency authorisation must be reviewed after the event. Both decisions are logged, with the name, the time, and the basis for the decision.

Control testing has two questions, and they are different. Design effectiveness asks whether the control is designed to work: does the maker and checker rule cover all payment types, does the limit apply to all channels, does the reconciliation cover all accounts. Operating effectiveness asks whether the control works in practice: did the checker actually check, did the limit actually block, did the reconciliation actually balance. Testing uses sampling. A statistical sample of 25 payments is enough to test a control that operates daily, provided the sample is random. A judgmental sample is used where the risk is concentrated, such as the largest payments or the most complex cases. The test procedure is written in advance, the evidence is recorded, and the result is a pass or a fail, not a narrative.

What happens when a control fails is a defined process, not an improvisation. The failure is declared as an incident, and the impact is assessed: how many payments were affected, how much money is at risk, and whether any customer was harmed. Containment comes first: the control is fixed, or a compensating control is put in place, so that the failure cannot continue. Remediation follows, typically within 30 days, and it addresses the root cause, not just the symptom. Re-testing follows within 60 days, to prove the fix works. The reporting goes to the risk committee, to the board, and to the regulator if the failure is material. A control failure that is found by an examiner, rather than by the bank own testing, is a more serious matter, because it shows the testing did not work.

Control ownership is the governance that holds the framework together. Every control has a named owner, a description, a frequency, and a test plan. The control inventory is the single source of truth: it lists every control, its owner, its frequency, and its last test result. The inventory must be kept current, and a change to a process, a system, or a product must trigger a review of the controls it affects. A control with no owner is a control that will fail, because nobody is responsible for testing it, and nobody will notice when it stops working. A control inventory that is out of date is worse than no inventory, because it gives false assurance.

For a business analyst, the control framework work is an inventory and a test programme. The inventory must list every control, its type, its owner, its frequency, and its test plan. The test programme must cover every control on a schedule, with design and operating effectiveness tested separately. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a maker tries to approve their own payment and is blocked, a test where a control is tested for design and operating effectiveness, a test where a control failure is declared, contained, remediated and re-tested inside the required timescales, and a test where the control inventory is verified as current after a process change. The evidence must be kept, because the control framework is the first thing an internal auditor tests, and one of the easiest to fail. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
