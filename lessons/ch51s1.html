What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain documentary collection is. A documentary collection is a payment method in which an exporter ships goods and then asks banks to handle the shipping documents on agreed terms.

Why it matters. This matters because cross-border payments involve more banks, more charges, more time and more ways to fail than any domestic payment. Getting the chain right is the job.

Chapter objective: Learner explains documentary collections, the roles of remitting and collecting banks, and the SWIFT Category 4 messages used at each stage. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key terms

Bill of exchange: A written order by one party instructing another party to pay a stated amount to a named person on a stated date.

SWIFT: The Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, a member owned cooperative that provides secure messaging services for financial institutions.

A documentary collection is a payment method in which an exporter ships goods and then asks banks to handle the shipping documents on agreed terms. The exporter hands the documents to its own bank with instructions. That bank forwards them to a bank in the importer country. The importer can only obtain the documents, and therefore claim the goods from the carrier, by paying for them or by accepting a bill of exchange. The banks act as intermediaries and document handlers. They do not guarantee payment, and they do not check whether the goods match the contract in quality or quantity. The power of a collection comes from control of the documents. In international trade the bill of lading is often a document of title, which means that whoever holds the original can claim the goods at the destination port. If a French exporter ships wine to a buyer in Spain and keeps the original bill of lading inside the collection, the Spanish buyer cannot clear the wine from the port of Barcelona without first satisfying the collection terms. That control gives the exporter more protection than sending an invoice and waiting for payment, while costing far less than a letter of credit. A typical collection contains several documents that together prove the shipment and its value. The commercial invoice states the price, the quantity, the currency and the payment terms, for example EUR 120,000 for 4,000 cases of wine. The packing list describes the contents of each carton. The certificate of origin states where the goods were made. The insurance certificate proves the cargo is covered during transit. The bill of lading proves the carrier received the goods. A draft, also called a bill of exchange, is the formal demand for payment or acceptance that sits on top of the set. Collections are different from open account trading and from letters of credit, and the difference is about who carries the risk. In open account trading the exporter ships first and simply invoices the buyer, so the exporter carries all the risk if the buyer does not pay. In a letter of credit the issuing bank gives a payment undertaking, so the bank carries the risk if the documents comply. In a collection the exporter keeps control of the documents but still relies on the buyer to pay or accept, so the risk is shared in practice. The buyer must act to get the goods, yet no bank promises to pay if the buyer refuses. Collections suit established trading relationships where the buyer is known and reliable, but the exporter still wants a layer of control. They are common in food, textiles, machinery parts, chemicals and consumer goods, where shipments are regular and values are moderate. A German exporter of machine tools might use a collection for a EUR 180,000 order from a long standing customer in Poland, while reserving letters of credit for new buyers or high risk countries. The fees are modest, often a few hundred euros per collection, and the process is faster than negotiating a letter of credit.

A documentary collection is a payment method in international trade where a bank handles the documents that control the goods, and releases them to the buyer only when the buyer pays or accepts a bill of exchange. The exporter ships the goods, draws a bill of exchange on the importer, and gives the shipping documents to its bank with an instruction to collect the payment. The exporter bank, the remitting bank, sends the documents to the importer bank, the collecting bank, which presents them to the importer. The importer pays or accepts, the collecting bank releases the documents, and the importer uses the documents to take delivery of the goods. The money flows back to the exporter through the same banks.

A documentary collection is not a letter of credit. In a letter of credit, the bank gives a payment undertaking, and the exporter is paid when it presents complying documents, whether or not the importer pays. In a documentary collection, the bank gives no payment undertaking. The bank acts as an agent, handling the documents and the collection, but it does not promise to pay. The exporter therefore carries the risk that the importer refuses to pay or to accept, and the goods are then stuck at the destination port. The collection is cheaper than a letter of credit, and it is simpler to arrange, but it gives the exporter less protection. An exporter chooses a collection when it trusts the importer, when the amount is small, or when the goods are easy to resell if the importer defaults.

The documents in a collection are the heart of the method, because they control the goods. The commercial invoice states the price and the terms. The bill of lading is the document of title to the goods, and whoever holds the original bill of lading can claim the goods from the carrier. The certificate of origin states where the goods were made. The insurance certificate covers the goods in transit. The packing list describes the contents. The bill of exchange is the payment instrument, drawn by the exporter on the importer, ordering the importer to pay a stated amount on a stated date. The collection instruction tells the collecting bank what to do with the documents, whether to release them against payment or against acceptance, and what to do if the importer refuses.

The rules that govern documentary collections are the Uniform Rules for Collections, URC 522, published by the International Chamber of Commerce. URC 522 defines the roles of the parties, the duties of the banks, the handling of the documents, and the procedures for payment, acceptance, and dishonour. The collection instruction should state that it is subject to URC 522, and the banks will then follow those rules. An SME must know that URC 522 is a set of rules the parties agree to follow, not a law, and that the banks liability under URC 522 is limited to acting with reasonable care. The bank is not liable if the importer refuses to pay, and it is not liable for the genuineness of the documents.

A collection is used when the exporter and the importer have a trading relationship and the exporter is willing to accept the risk of non-payment in exchange for a lower cost and a simpler process. It is common in trade between established partners, in trade where the goods are standard and easy to resell, and in trade where the amount is too small to justify the cost of a letter of credit. It is not used when the exporter does not trust the importer, when the goods are custom made and cannot be resold, or when the political risk in the importer country is high. A business analyst who advises a client on payment methods should ask about the trust, the goods, and the country risk before recommending a collection over a letter of credit.

The cost of a collection is lower than a letter of credit, but it is not free. The remitting bank charges a collection commission, usually a percentage of the amount with a minimum and a maximum. The collecting bank charges its own commission, and it may deduct its charges from the proceeds before remitting them. The courier and SWIFT message costs are additional. The exporter should agree the charges in advance, and should state in the collection instruction who pays each charge, using the same OUR, SHA, and BEN logic as in a normal payment. A collection where the charges are not agreed in advance will produce a dispute when the proceeds arrive short of the invoice amount.

An SME should remember that a documentary collection is a documents business, not a money business. The bank handles the documents, and the money follows the documents. If the documents are wrong, the collection fails, even if the importer is willing to pay. If the documents are right, the importer can take the goods, and the exporter can be paid. The rest of this chapter follows the documents through the process, from the exporter to the remitting bank, to the collecting bank, to the importer, and back, with the SWIFT Category 4 messages that carry the instructions and the proceeds at each step.
