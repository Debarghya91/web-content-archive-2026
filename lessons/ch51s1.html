Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A documentary collection is a payment method in which an exporter ships goods and then asks banks to handle the shipping documents on agreed terms. The exporter hands the documents to its own bank with instructions. That bank forwards them to a bank in the importer country. The importer can only obtain the documents, and therefore claim the goods from the carrier, by paying for them or by accepting a bill of exchange. The banks act as intermediaries and document handlers.

They do not guarantee payment, and they do not check whether the goods match the contract in quality or quantity. The power of a collection comes from control of the documents. In international trade the bill of lading is often a document of title, which means that whoever holds the original can claim the goods at the destination port. If a French exporter ships wine to a buyer in Spain and keeps the original bill of lading inside the collection, the Spanish buyer cannot clear the wine from the port of Barcelona without first satisfying the collection terms.

That control gives the exporter more protection than sending an invoice and waiting for payment, while costing far less than a letter of credit. A typical collection contains several documents that together prove the shipment and its value. The commercial invoice states the price, the quantity, the currency and the payment terms, for example EUR 120,000 for 4,000 cases of wine. The packing list describes the contents of each carton. The certificate of origin states where the goods were made.

The insurance certificate proves the cargo is covered during transit. The bill of lading proves the carrier received the goods. A draft, also called a bill of exchange, is the formal demand for payment or acceptance that sits on top of the set. Collections are different from open account trading and from letters of credit, and the difference is about who carries the risk. In open account trading the exporter ships first and simply invoices the buyer, so the exporter carries all the risk if the buyer does not pay.

In a letter of credit the issuing bank gives a payment undertaking, so the bank carries the risk if the documents comply. In a collection the exporter keeps control of the documents but still relies on the buyer to pay or accept, so the risk is shared in practice. The buyer must act to get the goods, yet no bank promises to pay if the buyer refuses. Collections suit established trading relationships where the buyer is known and reliable, but the exporter still wants a layer of control. They are common in food, textiles, machinery parts, chemicals and consumer goods, where shipments are regular and values are moderate.

A German exporter of machine tools might use a collection for a EUR 180,000 order from a long standing customer in Poland, while reserving letters of credit for new buyers or high risk countries. The fees are modest, often a few hundred euros per collection, and the process is faster than negotiating a letter of credit.
