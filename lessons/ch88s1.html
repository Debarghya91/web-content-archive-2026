Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Creditor: The party that is owed money and receives a payment, such as a supplier, a seller or a service provider.

Why. Netting is the efficiency of a CSM: thousands of payments become one settlement entry.

A clearing and settlement mechanism, usually called a CSM, is the infrastructure that sits between the banks that send payments and the banks that receive them. When a bank sends a payment instruction, it does not send it directly to every other bank. It sends it to a CSM. The CSM checks the instruction, sorts it, works out what each bank owes and is owed, and then moves the money. In the SEPA world the CSM is a defined concept. The SEPA rulebooks say a CSM is a mechanism that provides clearing and settlement services for payment transactions, and the European Payments Council expects CSMs to exchange payments with each other so that any bank can reach any other bank. Three layers work together, and a business analyst must keep them apart. The scheme writes the rules. The European Payments Council writes the SCT rulebook, which says what a SEPA credit transfer is, which fields it carries, how long it may take, and what happens when it fails. The CSM runs the processing. It receives the files, validates them, sorts them by creditor bank, and calculates the settlement positions. The settlement agent provides the money. In the euro area that is the central bank, and settlement happens in central bank money through TARGET2, now called T2. The scheme never moves money. The CSM never writes the rules. The central bank never sorts payment files. When a payment goes wrong, the first question is always which layer failed. A CSM is not the same as an ACH, although the two terms overlap. An automated clearing house is a type of CSM, and in the United States the term ACH describes the whole model: batch files, netting, deferred settlement. In Europe the term CSM is broader. It covers batch ACHs such as STEP2 and the national clearing houses, and it also covers real-time mechanisms such as TIPS, which settles each payment individually and immediately. The useful test is functional, not terminological. If an infrastructure receives payment instructions, clears them, and settles them, it is a CSM, whatever it is called locally. CSMs are operated by different kinds of organisations. EBA CLEARING operates STEP2 and is owned by its member banks. Several national CSMs are operated by the national central bank, for example the Bundesbank runs the retail payment system in Germany. Others are operated by private companies or by banking associations. What matters to a bank is not who owns the CSM but what it offers: which instruments it clears, how many cycles it runs a day, what its cut-off times are, what it charges, and how it settles. Reachability is the practical reason a bank joins a CSM. Under the SEPA Regulation a payment service provider must be reachable for SEPA payments, which means other banks must be able to send it payments. A bank achieves reachability by joining a CSM that other banks also use, or by being reachable through a CSM that interoperates with others. Each CSM publishes a reachability table listing the BICs it can deliver to. A bank that is not in any reachability table cannot receive SEPA payments, and that is a commercial and a regulatory failure. A worked example shows the scale. A mid-size German bank sends 40,000 SEPA credit transfers in one day, worth 120 million euro in total, to creditor banks in twelve countries. It sends one file to its CSM. The CSM validates the file, rejects 12 payments for invalid IBANs, sorts the remaining 39,988 by creditor bank, and calculates a single net position for the sending bank against each other bank. Instead of 39,988 individual settlements, the bank settles a handful of net amounts. That is the whole point of a CSM: it turns thousands of individual obligations into a small number of settlement entries. For a business analyst, the CSM is a set of requirements, not a black box. The requirements must state which CSM is used for each instrument, the file format and the validation rules, the cut-off time for each cycle, what happens to a rejected payment, and how the settlement account is funded. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a file is sent to the CSM, a payment is rejected for an invalid IBAN, and the rejection is reported back to the customer with the correct reason code.
