Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Finality: The point at which a payment settlement becomes irrevocable and unconditional, so it cannot be reversed by the payer or the system.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

FCDPS and CDFCPS are two names for the same Chinese payment system, and treating them as separate systems is the most common mistake analysts make with this topic. The CIPSP syllabus uses FCDPS in its module title and then gives the fuller form in the module text, namely the China Domestic Foreign Currency Payment System, shortened to CDFCPS. Documents from the People's Bank of China use the same fuller name and also shorten it to FCPS. In the early 2020s the English rendering changed again, to the China Foreign Exchange Payment System, shortened to CFXPS, while the underlying system stayed the same. This lesson uses FCDPS because that is the heading learners meet, but every fact here describes the CDFCPS and CFXPS system, a real time gross settlement system launched on 28 April 2008.

The system settles payments that are domestic in geography and foreign in currency, which is the combination that makes it unusual. Both the sending bank and the receiving bank are inside China, and the payment is denominated in a foreign currency rather than in renminbi. Eight currencies are handled: the US dollar, the Hong Kong dollar, the Japanese yen, the euro, the Australian dollar, the Canadian dollar, the UK pound sterling and the Swiss franc, and the US dollar accounts for the overwhelming majority of the value. No currency conversion takes place inside the system, so a payment that needs one currency changed into another is not a job for this system at all.

China needs this system because foreign currency is used inside the country as well as across its borders, and because clearing and settlement are organised to keep such payments onshore. A Chinese exporter may hold US dollars in a domestic account, a domestic supplier may invoice in US dollars for goods linked to foreign trade, and banks may lend to each other in a foreign currency. Before a dedicated system existed, a payment between two banks inside China could still have to travel through correspondent banks, sometimes outside China, even though neither the payer nor the payee had left the country. The China National Clearing Center, run by the People's Bank of China, receives, checks, sorts and forwards payment instructions, while settlement is carried out by designated settlement banks, in whose books participating banks hold foreign currency settlement accounts.

A payment through the system follows a short sequence that an operations analyst should be able to trace from end to end. A company in Dalian instructs its bank to pay a supplier in Wuhan in US dollars, and the sending bank first checks that the customer holds enough US dollars. The bank then carries out its sanctions, anti money laundering and fraud checks, and applies China's foreign exchange controls and reporting rules, because a domestic payment in a foreign currency is still foreign currency business. The bank sends the instruction into the system, the clearing center validates it and checks the sending bank's settlement account, and the settlement bank moves the US dollars from the sending bank's account to the receiving bank's account. Once settlement is confirmed, the receiving bank credits the supplier's US dollar account, and settlement is on the same day.

The difference from CIPS turns on two questions, where the payment is going and which currency it is in. CIPS, the Cross-border Interbank Payment System, was launched in October 2015 to clear and settle renminbi payments that cross China's border, so its geography is cross border and its currency is renminbi. The domestic renminbi systems handle payments that stay inside China in renminbi. FCDPS completes the pattern by handling payments that stay inside China in a foreign currency. One extension blurs the neat picture: since March 2009 a linkage with Hong Kong's real time gross settlement systems has allowed multi currency payments between mainland banks and Hong Kong banks, initially for four currencies.

When something goes wrong, the handling follows the same pattern as in other payment systems, and finality is the point that matters most. A message with a format error is corrected and sent again, a payment without enough funds in the settlement account is queued or rejected at the cut off, a compliance stop goes to review, and wrong beneficiary details lead to a return. A payment is not final until settlement has been confirmed, so the receiving bank should not treat an instruction that has merely arrived as settled funds. The test is therefore simple: both banks inside China, one foreign currency from end to end, and no conversion. If any of those three conditions fails, a different system is carrying the payment.
