What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain roles: remitting bank and collecting bank. The remitting bank is the exporter bank, the bank to which the exporter entrusts the documents and the collection instruction.

Why it matters. If the instruction says release documents only against payment of EUR 95,000, the collecting bank must not release them for EUR 90,000, or against a promise to pay next week.

Chapter objective: Learner explains documentary collections, the roles of remitting and collecting banks, and the SWIFT Category 4 messages used at each stage. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Remitting bank
  • Its duty
  • Collecting bank following the remitting bank instructions
  • Release only against payment of EUR 95,000
  • Collecting bank contacting the importer, presenting documents, asking payment
  • Collecting bank funds by MT103

Key terms

Bill of exchange: A written order by one party instructing another party to pay a stated amount to a named person on a stated date.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

MT400: A SWIFT message used to advise a bank of the collection of a documentary credit or a bill, and to request payment or acceptance.

Correct result. The remitting bank is the exporter bank, entrusted with the documents and the collection instruction. The remitting bank is the exporter bank, the bank to which the exporter entrusts the documents and the collection instruction. The exporter, known as the principal or drawer, delivers the documents and a collection instruction form to this bank. The remitting bank checks that the instruction is complete, records the collection reference, and forwards the documents and the instruction to the collecting bank in the importer country. It does not examine the documents in detail for compliance, as it would under a letter of credit. Its duty is to transmit the instruction faithfully and to pass back any proceeds or messages it receives. The collecting bank is any bank in the importer country that is involved in handling the collection, other than the remitting bank. In many cases the collecting bank is also the presenting bank, which is the bank that actually presents the documents to the importer, known as the drawee, and asks for payment or acceptance. If the collecting bank is not in the same city as the importer, it may appoint a presenting bank closer to the importer. The collecting bank follows the instructions it receives from the remitting bank, presents the documents, collects the payment or acceptance, and remits the proceeds. The roles matter because each bank has limited liability, and the exporter must understand those limits before choosing this method. The remitting bank is not liable if the collecting bank fails to present the documents promptly, and the collecting bank is not liable if the importer refuses to pay. Both banks must act in good faith and with reasonable care, and they must follow the collection instruction exactly. If the instruction says release documents only against payment of EUR 95,000, the collecting bank must not release them for EUR 90,000, or against a promise to pay next week. A clear instruction protects everyone. A practical example makes the chain concrete. An exporter in Lyon banks with BNP Paribas, and its customer in Madrid banks with Banco Santander. The Lyon exporter hands its documents to BNP Paribas, which becomes the remitting bank. BNP Paribas sends the documents and an MT400 series instruction, or a couriered collection instruction, to Banco Santander in Madrid, which becomes the collecting bank and the presenting bank. Santander contacts the Madrid importer, presents the documents, and asks for payment. Santander then sends the EUR 250,000 proceeds back to BNP Paribas, which credits the exporter account. Correspondent relationships shape how the two banks communicate, and they explain why SWIFT messages matter so much in collections. The remitting bank and the collecting bank may hold accounts with each other, or they may settle through a third correspondent bank. If BNP Paribas holds a euro account with Banco Santander, the proceeds can move by a simple book transfer. If not, the collecting bank may send the funds by MT103 to the remitting bank account at a correspondent. The collection instruction, the advice of payment and the remittance of funds are separate steps, and each one needs its own message, reference and reconciliation.

The remitting bank is the bank to which the exporter, the principal, has entrusted the handling of the collection. The exporter gives the documents and the collection instruction to the remitting bank, and the remitting bank sends them to the collecting bank in the importer country. The remitting bank is normally the exporter own bank, in the exporter country. Its duties under URC 522 are to check that the instruction is complete, to forward the documents and the instruction to the collecting bank without delay, and to remit the proceeds to the exporter when they arrive. The remitting bank does not examine the documents for compliance, and it does not guarantee payment. Its role is to transmit and to collect.

The collecting bank is the bank in the importer country that receives the documents from the remitting bank and presents them to the importer, the drawee. The collecting bank may be a correspondent of the remitting bank, or it may be a bank the importer nominates. Its duties under URC 522 are to present the documents to the drawee without delay, to release the documents only against payment or acceptance as instructed, to protest the bill of exchange if the drawee refuses to pay or accept, and to remit the proceeds to the remitting bank. The collecting bank is the bank that faces the importer, and it is the bank that must handle a refusal, a partial payment, or a request for more time.

The presenting bank is a further role that sometimes appears. When the collecting bank is not in the same city as the drawee, it may ask another bank, the presenting bank, to present the documents to the drawee. The presenting bank acts on behalf of the collecting bank, and it follows the same instruction. The distinction between the collecting bank and the presenting bank matters for the message flow, because the MT400 advice of payment is sent by the collecting bank to the remitting bank, and the presenting bank does not send it. An SME who reads a collection case should identify which bank is the remitting bank, which is the collecting bank, and whether a presenting bank is involved, because the responsibilities and the message flow depend on those roles.

The principal is the exporter, the party that entrusts the collection to the remitting bank. The principal gives the instruction, and the instruction states the documents, the amount, the terms of release, and what to do if the drawee refuses. The drawee is the importer, the party on whom the bill of exchange is drawn, and the party to whom the documents are presented. The drawee pays or accepts, and then receives the documents. The drawer is the exporter, who draws the bill of exchange. The payee is the party to whom the bill is payable, normally the exporter or its bank. These roles are defined in URC 522, and they should be used precisely, because a confusion between the collecting bank and the presenting bank, or between the principal and the drawer, will cause a misunderstanding in the instruction.

The banks duties under URC 522 are limited, and an SME must understand the limits. The bank must act in good faith and exercise reasonable care. It must follow the instruction. It must present the documents without delay. It must not release the documents except against payment or acceptance as instructed. It must advise the remitting bank if the drawee refuses. But the bank is not liable for the genuineness, the accuracy, or the legal effect of the documents. It is not liable if the goods do not match the documents. It is not liable if the drawee refuses to pay. The bank liability is for its own handling of the collection, not for the underlying trade. An exporter who thinks the bank guarantees payment has misunderstood the method.

The commission structure is agreed between the principal and the remitting bank, and between the remitting bank and the collecting bank. The remitting bank normally charges the principal a collection commission, and the collecting bank charges a commission that it deducts from the proceeds or charges to the remitting bank. The instruction should state who pays the collecting bank charges, and the collecting bank should not deduct charges that the instruction says the drawee will pay. A dispute over charges is one of the most common problems in a collection, and it is avoided by stating the charge arrangement clearly in the instruction, using the same OUR, SHA, and BEN logic as in a payment message.

For a business analyst, the roles requirement must state each party, each bank, and the message that connects them. The requirement should say that the principal gives the instruction to the remitting bank, that the remitting bank sends the documents and the instruction to the collecting bank by courier and by SWIFT, that the collecting bank presents the documents to the drawee, that the collecting bank releases the documents only against payment or acceptance, and that the collecting bank sends the MT400 advice of payment to the remitting bank when the proceeds are received. A system that cannot distinguish the remitting bank from the collecting bank cannot process a collection correctly.
