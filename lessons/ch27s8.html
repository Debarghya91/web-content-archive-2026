What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain stablecoin regulation in 2026: MICA and beyond. Stablecoin regulation has moved from discussion to law.

Why it matters. This matters because the next payment product you work on may use one of these technologies. Knowing what is real and what is still a proposal is what separates a good analyst from a confused one.

Chapter objective: Learner explains central bank digital currencies, stablecoins, and AI in payments, comparing them with existing rails on speed, settlement, risk, and regulation, and assesses a realistic use case. Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Capture: The step in a card transaction where the merchant collects the authorised amount from the issuer, usually when goods are shipped.

Issuer: A bank or licensed institution that issues payment cards to customers and holds their accounts. It authorises transactions, debits cardholder accounts, and manages credit limits and fraud controls.

Exercise

Compare MiCA and the GENIUS Act for a bank that wants to issue a stablecoin.

In the European Union, MiCA is now fully in force. It requires stablecoin issuers to be licensed as electronic money institutions or credit institutions, to hold reserves in segregated accounts, to publish white papers, and to meet governance and risk management requirements. In the United States, the GENIUS Act, passed in 2025, created a federal framework for payment stablecoins. It requires issuers to hold reserves in cash or short-term US Treasury securities, to publish monthly reserve reports, to be audited annually, and to give holders a first claim on reserves in insolvency.

  1. What to do. State what a bank must do to issue a euro stablecoin under MiCA, and what a US issuer must do under the GENIUS Act.

Correct result. To issue a euro stablecoin under MiCA, the bank must be licensed as an electronic money institution or credit institution, hold reserves in segregated accounts, publish a white paper, and meet governance and risk management requirements. Asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens are the two categories that matter for payments, and both are now regulated. A bank that wants to accept stablecoin payments must check that the issuer is licensed. Under the GENIUS Act, a US issuer must hold reserves in cash or short-term US Treasury securities, publish monthly reserve reports, be audited annually, and give holders a first claim on reserves in insolvency. The Act also allows banks and non-banks to issue payment stablecoins under a federal licence.

Why. Stablecoin regulation has moved from discussion to law, so a bank that issues or accepts stablecoins must treat licensing, reserves and reporting as requirements, not as optional good practice.

Stablecoin regulation has moved from discussion to law. In the European Union, the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, known as MiCA, is now fully in force. MiCA requires stablecoin issuers to be licensed as electronic money institutions or credit institutions, to hold reserves in segregated accounts, to publish white papers, and to meet governance and risk management requirements. Asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens are the two categories that matter for payments, and both are now regulated. A bank that wants to issue a euro stablecoin must comply with MiCA, and a bank that wants to accept stablecoin payments must check that the issuer is licensed. In the United States, the GENIUS Act, passed in 2025, created a federal framework for payment stablecoins. It requires issuers to hold reserves in cash or short-term US Treasury securities, to publish monthly reserve reports, to be audited annually, and to give holders a first claim on reserves in insolvency. The Act also allows banks and non-banks to issue payment stablecoins under a federal licence, and it prohibits issuers from paying interest on stablecoin holdings. The result is a clear, if demanding, path for regulated stablecoins in the US dollar market. Banks are now issuing their own stablecoins and tokenised deposits. JPMorgan has JPM Coin for institutional clients. Several European banks have issued euro stablecoins under MiCA. The use cases are cross-border settlement, where a stablecoin can settle in minutes rather than days, and tokenised trade finance, where payment and delivery can be linked in a smart contract. Central banks are also progressing on CBDCs. The digital euro is in preparation phase, the Bank of England is consulting on a digital pound, and several Asian central banks have live CBDC pilots. For a payments BA, the practical work is integration, not speculation. Your requirements must cover how a stablecoin payment is initiated, how it is screened for sanctions and fraud, how it is reconciled against the bank ledger, and how it is reported to regulators. Your test cases must cover the stablecoin lifecycle. Minting, transfer, redemption, and the failure modes when a reserve report is late or a wallet is frozen. And your stakeholder communication must distinguish between what is live today, such as regulated stablecoins for institutional settlement, and what is still experimental, such as retail CBDCs, so that the business does not confuse a pilot with a product. That is a high bar, and it is deliberately high, because a stablecoin that is used as money must be as safe as money.
