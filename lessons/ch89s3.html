Settlement accounts and central bank money: how a bank funds its settlement account

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain settlement accounts and central bank money and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. The settlement account is where liquidity meets finality, and the analyst must understand both.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how clearing systems work, distinguishes netting from gross settlement, computes multilateral net positions, explains settlement cycles, cut-offs, settlement accounts, intraday liquidity, queueing and gridlock resolution, and describes default handling and the RTGS versus deferred net settlement trade-off, with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Settlement: movement of money
  • Central bank money
  • Intraday credit from the central bank
  • 30 million movement from operating account
  • Arrangement: governed by settlement agreement
  • Settlement account

Key terms

Indirect participant: A bank or institution that uses a payment system through a direct participant, without holding its own settlement account.

Direct participant: A bank or institution that holds an account in a payment system and sends and receives payments directly.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement is the movement of money that discharges the obligation, and in a modern payment system that money is almost always central bank money. A settlement account is the account a bank holds at the central bank, or with a settlement agent, through which its net positions are settled. In the euro area the settlement account is the TARGET2 account, now the T2 account, held with the national central bank. When STEP2 calculates a net debit of 8 million euro for a bank, that 8 million is debited from the bank's T2 account and credited to the accounts of the banks with net credits. The movement is final and irrevocable the moment it is posted. Central bank money is different from commercial bank money, and the difference is the reason central banks sit at the centre of settlement. Commercial bank money is a claim on a commercial bank: a deposit is the bank's promise to pay. If the bank fails, the claim may not be honoured in full. Central bank money is a claim on the central bank, and a central bank cannot fail in its own currency, because it can always create more of it. Settlement in central bank money therefore carries no credit risk between the settling banks. That is why the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures say that a systemically important payment system should settle in central bank money where practical. Funding the settlement account is a daily treasury operation. The bank starts the day by moving liquidity from its operating accounts into its T2 account, so that it can meet the net debits from the morning clearing cycles. The treasury team forecasts the day's net positions from the previous days' patterns and from the payments already in the queue. A bank that expects a net debit of 40 million euro in the morning cycle and a net credit of 15 million in the afternoon cycle will fund 40 million in the morning and let the afternoon credit rebuild the balance. Intraday credit from the central bank covers the gaps. The Eurosystem provides intraday credit to banks that hold an account with it, against eligible collateral, usually government bonds. The bank pledges collateral, receives an intraday credit line, and can draw on it during the day to meet a settlement. The credit must be repaid by the end of the day. If it is not repaid, it automatically becomes overnight borrowing through the marginal lending facility, at a penalty rate. The collateral is subject to a haircut, which means a bond worth 100 million euro might support only 95 million of credit, and the haircut can change if the bond's rating or the central bank's policy changes. A worked example shows the day. A bank holds 60 million euro in its T2 account at 08:00. The morning STEP2 cycle settles a net debit of 40 million, leaving 20 million. The midday cycle settles a net debit of 12 million, leaving 8 million. The treasury team moves 30 million from its operating account into T2, bringing the balance to 38 million. The afternoon cycle settles a net debit of 25 million, leaving 13 million. At the end of the day the bank sweeps the remaining balance back to its operating account, or leaves it for the next morning. If the midday cycle had been a net debit of 25 million instead of 12 million, the balance would have gone negative, and the bank would have drawn on its intraday credit line. Not every bank holds a central bank account. A small bank, a payment institution, or a foreign branch may settle through a direct participant, called its settlement bank. The settlement bank holds the T2 account and settles the net positions on behalf of its indirect participants, debiting or crediting their accounts with it. The indirect participant's risk is that its settlement bank fails, and the settlement bank's risk is that its indirect participant does not fund its net debit. The arrangement is governed by a settlement agreement, and the indirect participant must monitor its settlement bank's health. Reconciliation closes the loop. Every day the bank receives a statement of its T2 account, a camt.053 message, and reconciles it against its own ledger. Every settlement entry must match a clearing cycle and a net position. A break, where the statement shows a debit the bank does not expect, must be investigated the same day, because a settlement error that is not found quickly becomes a liquidity problem the next morning. Edge cases are where the funding breaks down. A collateral haircut increase can suddenly reduce the intraday credit line. A large unexpected net debit, from a corporate customer paying salaries a day early, can drain the account before treasury can top it up. A settlement account frozen by a regulator, because of a sanctions or insolvency action, stops the bank settling at all. For a business analyst, the settlement account is a set of requirements about funding, forecasting, intraday credit, and reconciliation. The acceptance criteria must include a test where the day's net positions are forecast and funded, a test where an unexpected net debit triggers an intraday credit draw, and a test where the T2 statement reconciles to the ledger with zero breaks. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: the TARGET2 Guideline and the T2 user detailed functional specifications; the Bank for International Settlements Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures, Principle 9 on money settlements; the European Central Bank TARGET annual report.
