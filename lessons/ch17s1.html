What Clearing Really Means

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain clearing really means. Clearing is the process that sits between a payment instruction and its final settlement.

Why it matters. For a business analyst, understanding clearing means understanding where in the flow a payment can be stopped, corrected or returned, and what information is needed at each step to keep the process reliable and auditable.

Chapter objective: Learner explains clearing, bilateral netting, multilateral netting, and the role of a clearing house, and calculates net settlement positions from a set of gross payment obligations. Learner demonstrates this skill using realistic banking examples, explaining each step clearly and using correct terminology throughout.

Key learning points

  • Clearing: process sits between payment
  • Individual settlement is impractical
  • Understanding clearing
  • Clearing and settlement
  • Cross border payments clear through correspondent
  • Key questions

Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Clearing is the process that sits between a payment instruction and its final settlement. When a customer sends money, the instruction does not go straight to the receiving bank as a single movement of central bank funds. It first enters a clearing process where it is validated, sorted, matched against other instructions and prepared for settlement. Clearing answers a simple question before money moves, which is who owes what to whom after all the payments in a given period are taken together. Without clearing, every single payment would need its own separate settlement, which would be slow, expensive and wasteful of liquidity. Think of clearing as the back office work that makes high volume payments possible. A retail payment system may process millions of transactions in a single day, from salary credits and bill payments to card purchases and direct debits. It would be impractical to settle each one individually in central bank money. Clearing groups these transactions, checks them for errors, removes duplicates and invalid instructions, and produces a clear statement of obligations. Only after this sorting and validation is complete does the system move to settlement, where the actual transfer of value takes place between banks. Clearing also creates a vital control point. It is here that a payment can be rejected if the account details are wrong, if the amount exceeds a limit, or if the sending bank does not have enough funds or credit to cover its obligations. Clearing systems apply rules agreed by all participants, and those rules determine cut off times, file formats, error handling and the treatment of late or failed payments. For a business analyst, understanding clearing means understanding where in the flow a payment can be stopped, corrected or returned, and what information is needed at each step to keep the process reliable and auditable. Follow a payment through clearing step by step. The instruction arrives from the customer, through online banking, a file, or a card transaction. The clearing system validates it, checking the account details, the amount, and the format. It sorts the instruction by receiving bank, checks for duplicates, and removes anything invalid. It then groups the valid instructions with all the other instructions in the same clearing cycle and calculates what each bank owes and is owed. Only after that calculation is complete does the system produce settlement instructions and move to settlement, where the actual transfer of value takes place. Clearing and settlement are different jobs, and confusing them causes real mistakes. Clearing is about information. It validates, sorts, matches, and nets, and it answers the question of who owes what to whom. Settlement is about value. It moves central bank money from the banks that owe to the banks that are owed, and once settlement happens, the payment is final and cannot be reversed. A payment can clear successfully and still fail to settle, if a bank does not have enough funds in its settlement account. Clearing systems come in different shapes. Retail systems clear in batches on fixed cycles, often once or several times a day, and they handle salary credits, bill payments, direct debits, and card transactions. Card systems clear transactions in batches and settle the net amounts between the card schemes and the banks. Cross border payments clear through correspondent banks or clearing houses before they settle. Contrast that with real time gross settlement systems, such as CHAPS, Fedwire, and T2, the Eurosystem service that replaced TARGET2 in March 2023, where each payment settles individually in central bank money as soon as it is sent, with no netting at all. Exceptions in clearing are handled by rules agreed by all participants. An instruction with invalid account details is rejected before clearing, and the sending bank must tell the customer. A duplicate instruction is removed so the customer is not paid twice. A file that arrives after the cut off is held for the next cycle, which changes the net positions for both cycles. A bank that does not have enough funds or credit to cover its obligations may have its payments held or unwound, which means the net positions are recalculated without that bank. For a business analyst, the key questions are always the same: where in the flow can a payment be stopped, what information is needed at each step, and what happens when a step fails.
