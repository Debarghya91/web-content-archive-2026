Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

SWIFT: The Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, a member owned cooperative that provides secure messaging services for financial institutions.

MT103: A SWIFT message that carries a single customer credit transfer instruction from one bank to another. It names the ordering customer, the beneficiary, the amount, the currency and the value date.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Every payment carries personal data. A SEPA credit transfer carries the payer name, the payer IBAN, the payee name, the payee IBAN and a remittance reference that often names a person or a contract. A card payment carries the cardholder name, the card number, the merchant name and the location. A SWIFT MT103 carries field 50a for the ordering customer, field 59a for the beneficiary customer, field 70 for the remittance information and field 72 for sender to receiver information. None of this is anonymous, and none of it can be processed without a lawful basis.

The General Data Protection Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2016/679, applies to every payment a European bank or payment institution processes, and to any payment that touches a person in the European Economic Area. GDPR defines personal data as any information relating to an identified or identifiable person, and an IBAN is personal data because it identifies the account holder. Payment data is not a special category under Article 9, so the ordinary lawful bases apply, but the volume and the sensitivity of the data make the obligations serious.

The lawful basis for processing payment data is usually performance of a contract, because the bank must move the money the customer instructed, and legal obligation, because anti-money-laundering law requires the bank to screen, record and report. Consent is almost never the right basis for a payment, because the customer cannot meaningfully refuse consent and still have the payment made. A business analyst must be able to name the lawful basis for every data field a new payment feature collects, and must challenge any field that has no basis.

Data minimisation is the rule that bites hardest in payments. Article 5 requires personal data to be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary. A payment screen that shows the full beneficiary address to every operations user fails this test. A remittance reference that carries a free-text note about a medical bill fails it. The practical design rule is to show each user only the fields their task needs, to mask account numbers outside the payment engine, and to strip free-text fields before they reach a data warehouse.

Retention is where GDPR and anti-money-laundering law collide. GDPR says data must not be kept longer than necessary, while the Fifth Anti-Money-Laundering Directive requires transaction records to be kept for five years after the end of the business relationship, extendable by a further five years. The resolution is a retention schedule that keeps the payment record for the AML period and deletes or anonymises the marketing and analytics copies sooner. The schedule must be written, approved and enforced by the system, not left to individual judgement.

A data breach in payments is a reportable event. Under Article 33 the controller must notify the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to people. Under Article 34 the affected people must be told without undue delay when the risk is high. A payment file sent to the wrong correspondent bank, a statement printed with the wrong address, or an API that returns another customer's payment history are all breaches, and the 72 hour clock starts when the bank knows, not when it finishes investigating.

For a business analyst, GDPR is a requirements discipline, not a legal footnote. Every requirement that touches payment data must state the lawful basis, the retention period, who can see each field, and what happens on a subject access request. The acceptance criteria must include a test that proves a user without the right role cannot see the full IBAN, and a test that proves a deleted customer record stays deleted in the analytics copy. Last reviewed 7 November 2026. Further reading: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 5, 6, 33 and 34; European Data Protection Board guidelines on data protection by design; the Fifth Anti-Money-Laundering Directive, Article 40.
