Key terms

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Issuer: A bank or licensed institution that issues payment cards to customers and holds their accounts. It authorises transactions, debits cardholder accounts, and manages credit limits and fraud controls.

Refund: A repayment of funds to a customer after a payment fails, is cancelled or is disputed. In card payments a refund returns money to the cardholder account.

Correct result. Minimum KYC: mobile number verified through an OTP plus basic details such as name and date of birth. Full KYC: an official document such as a PAN card and address proof, plus a video KYC session or in person verification. Why. KYC tier decides what the wallet can do, so a requirement that confuses the tiers will promise a feature the product cannot legally offer.

Minimum KYC is the lighter tier, designed to let a customer start using a wallet quickly with only a mobile number verified through an OTP and basic details such as name and date of birth. The customer can load and spend money, but the limits are deliberately low. Historically the monthly load and spend limits for minimum KYC wallets were around 10,000 rupees, and the wallet cannot be used for fund transfers to another person or for cash withdrawal in most cases. The wallet must also be revalidated periodically, and if the customer does not complete full KYC within the permitted period, the issuer must restrict or close the wallet. A BA should document the exact limits as configurable values, not as hard coded numbers, because the regulator revises them from time to time.

Full KYC requires a stronger identity check, usually an official document such as a PAN card and an address proof, plus a video KYC session or an in person verification where the customer shows the original document and confirms their identity on camera. Once full KYC is complete, the wallet limits rise sharply. The customer can hold a larger balance, often up to 2,00,000 rupees in a full KYC PPI, can transfer money to other wallets and bank accounts, and can withdraw cash where the product supports it. The KYC record must be stored with the document type, the document number, the verification date, the verification method and the expiry date of the document. A BA must also specify what happens when a document expires, when a name mismatch is found, and when a customer fails video KYC twice.

The RBI PPI guidelines sit behind all of this, and a BA working on a wallet project should know the main rules rather than guessing them. The guidelines define who can issue a PPI, the capital requirements for the issuer, the need for a nodal or escrow account with a bank, the KYC tiers, the interoperability requirements, and the customer grievance and refund timelines. They also require the issuer to display fees clearly, to provide a transaction history, and to protect customer funds so that the money in the pooled account cannot be used for the issuer business expenses. A BA should maintain a compliance traceability matrix that maps every regulatory clause to a requirement, a design element and a test case, so that an audit can prove the system was built to the rule rather than to a developer interpretation.

Wallet onboarding is a sequence of steps that must be designed as a single smooth journey with clear failure handling at each step. The customer downloads the app, enters a mobile number, verifies the OTP, provides basic details, chooses a PIN, and then either stops at minimum KYC or proceeds to full KYC by uploading documents and completing video verification. Each step must have a defined timeout, a retry limit and a help message, and the system must never create a usable wallet before the OTP verification succeeds. A common defect is allowing a wallet to be loaded before KYC is complete, or allowing a minimum KYC wallet to exceed its limit by splitting a large load into smaller loads. The BA should specify velocity checks that aggregate loads across a rolling 30 day window, not just a calendar month.

From a documentation perspective, a BA should produce a KYC requirements pack that includes the customer journey map, the field level data dictionary, the document acceptance rules, the limit table for each KYC tier, the revalidation schedule, and the exception flows for rejected documents and failed video sessions. The pack should also define the audit trail. Every KYC decision must record who or what made it, when it was made, and what evidence was used. For example, if a customer PAN is verified through the official verification service, the system should store the verification reference, the response code and the timestamp. This level of detail is what separates a wallet project that passes a regulatory audit from one that fails it, and it is the BA who owns that detail.
