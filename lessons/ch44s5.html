Key terms

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Repair: Manual correction of a payment that failed straight through processing because of missing or invalid data.

Operational controls are the rules and checks that prevent errors, detect them quickly when they happen, and limit the damage. The most important control is the four eyes principle, which means a second person checks any payment above a defined threshold before it is released. In many banks that threshold is 100,000 pounds, so a payment of 250,000 euros to a supplier in France needs a maker and a checker. Another control is the segregation of duties, which means the person who sets up a new beneficiary cannot also approve the first payment to that beneficiary. A third control is the limit on what one analyst can release in a day, so a single compromised account cannot drain a large sum.

Queues are the safe waiting areas where payments sit when they cannot go straight through. The main queues are the validation queue for payments with missing or incorrect fields, the sanctions queue for payments that match a name on a screening list, the fraud queue for payments that look unusual, the liquidity queue for payments waiting for funds, and the repair queue for payments that failed in a downstream system. Each queue has an owner, a target age and a priority order. A payment of 2 million dollars in the sanctions queue takes priority over a payment of 150 pounds in the validation queue, because the value at risk and the customer impact are far greater.

The most common exceptions are simple data errors that can be fixed in minutes. A payment may fail because the beneficiary account number is one digit short, the SWIFT code is out of date, the currency does not match the beneficiary account, or the amount exceeds the customer daily limit of 50,000 pounds. A payment of 75,000 euros to a company in Italy may be held because the IBAN is missing two characters. The analyst opens the payment, compares the instruction with the customer record or the previous payment to the same beneficiary, corrects the field and resubmits the payment for validation. Every correction is logged with the old value, the new value, the analyst name and the time, so an auditor can reconstruct the change.

Sanctions and fraud exceptions need more care, because the analyst must judge risk rather than fix a typing error. A payment of 120,000 dollars to a company whose name is similar to a name on the United States OFAC sanctions list is held until compliance confirms whether the match is a true hit or a false positive. The analyst checks the full name, the address, the country and the date of birth of the individual where available, and passes the evidence to compliance. A fraud exception may involve a customer who normally pays 2,000 pounds a month suddenly sending 48,000 pounds to a new beneficiary in another country. The analyst calls the customer to confirm the payment is genuine before releasing it, and the call is recorded.

Exception handling is measured every day, because the numbers reveal whether the process is working. The team tracks the straight through processing rate, which is the share of payments that need no manual touch, the average age of items in each queue, the number of payments repaired per analyst per hour, and the number of payments returned to customers. A retail bank processing two million payments a day at 98 percent straight through processing still has 40,000 exceptions to handle, which is why staffing is planned around the exception rate rather than the total volume. When the exception rate rises from two percent to four percent, the team leader investigates the cause, which is often a bad file from one corporate customer or a change in a screening rule.

Operational Controls, Queues and Exception Handling

Operational controls are the rules and checks that prevent errors, detect them quickly when they happen, and limit the damage. The most important control is the four eyes principle, which means a second person checks any payment above a defined threshold before it is released. In many banks that threshold is 100,000 pounds, so a payment of 250,000 euros to a supplier in France needs a maker and a checker. Another control is the segregation of duties, which means the person who sets up a new beneficiary cannot also approve the first payment to that beneficiary. A third control is the limit on what one analyst can release in a day, so a single compromised account cannot drain a large sum.

Queues are the safe waiting areas where payments sit when they cannot go straight through. The main queues are the validation queue for payments with missing or incorrect fields, the sanctions queue for payments that match a name on a screening list, the fraud queue for payments that look unusual, the liquidity queue for payments waiting for funds, and the repair queue for payments that failed in a downstream system. Each queue has an owner, a target age and a priority order. A payment of 2 million dollars in the sanctions queue takes priority over a payment of 150 pounds in the validation queue, because the value at risk and the customer impact are far greater.

The most common exceptions are simple data errors that can be fixed in minutes. A payment may fail because the beneficiary account number is one digit short, the SWIFT code is out of date, the currency does not match the beneficiary account, or the amount exceeds the customer daily limit of 50,000 pounds. A payment of 75,000 euros to a company in Italy may be held because the IBAN is missing two characters. The analyst opens the payment, compares the instruction with the customer record or the previous payment to the same beneficiary, corrects the field and resubmits the payment for validation. Every correction is logged with the old value, the new value, the analyst name and the time, so an auditor can reconstruct the change.

Sanctions and fraud exceptions need more care, because the analyst must judge risk rather than fix a typing error. A payment of 120,000 dollars to a company whose name is similar to a name on the United States OFAC sanctions list is held until compliance confirms whether the match is a true hit or a false positive. The analyst checks the full name, the address, the country and the date of birth of the individual where available, and passes the evidence to compliance. A fraud exception may involve a customer who normally pays 2,000 pounds a month suddenly sending 48,000 pounds to a new beneficiary in another country. The analyst calls the customer to confirm the payment is genuine before releasing it, and the call is recorded.

Exception handling is measured every day, because the numbers reveal whether the process is working. The team tracks the straight through processing rate, which is the share of payments that need no manual touch, the average age of items in each queue, the number of payments repaired per analyst per hour, and the number of payments returned to customers. A retail bank processing two million payments a day at 98 percent straight through processing still has 40,000 exceptions to handle, which is why staffing is planned around the exception rate rather than the total volume. When the exception rate rises from two percent to four percent, the team leader investigates the cause, which is often a bad file from one corporate customer or a change in a screening rule.
