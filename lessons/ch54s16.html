Use the message structure, fields and example in this lesson.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

Corporates connect to SWIFT through closed user groups, and three models dominate. They differ in who administers the group, what the corporate signs, and what traffic the group carries. The models are TRCO, the Treasury Counterparty model, MA-CUG, the Member Administered Closed User Group, and SCORE, the Standardised Corporate Environment. A corporate choosing how to connect to SWIFT is choosing between these models, and the choice shapes the legal agreements, the onboarding effort, and the number of banks the corporate can reach.

TRCO is the oldest of the three, dating from the late 1990s. It is the Treasury Counterparty model: a corporate exchanges foreign exchange and option transaction confirmations, the MT300 series, with any connected financial institution. TRCO is about treasury confirmations, not payments: it lets a corporate confirm its trades with many banks over one connection. It was the first model to let corporates onto the network in a standard way, and it remains the route for confirmation traffic where the corporate trades with multiple counterparties.

MA-CUG followed in 2001. In a Member Administered Closed User Group, a bank administers its own group: the bank admits the corporate, controls the membership, and onboards the connection. The corporate signs a separate agreement with each bank it connects to through MA-CUG. The model suits a corporate whose banking is concentrated with one or two banks, or a bank that wants close control over its corporate connections. The bank owns the relationship, the onboarding, and the support, and the corporate gets a connection shaped by that bank.

SCORE arrived in the mid 2000s and changed the economics. In the Standardised Corporate Environment, SWIFT administers a single closed user group, and the corporate signs one agreement that reaches all participating financial institutions. One onboarding, one agreement, many banks. SCORE standardised the legal and technical terms, so a corporate with twenty banking relationships does not negotiate twenty connections. The standardisation also covers the message usage: SCORE members exchange payments, statements, and confirmations under common guidelines, which reduces the per bank customisation that used to make multi bank connectivity expensive. SCORE Plus extends the model to the FINplus network and the ISO 20022 pain.001 version 9, bringing the single agreement model to the new messaging standard.

Choosing between the models is a question of traffic and reach. TRCO fits treasury confirmations with many counterparties. MA-CUG fits a concentrated banking relationship where the bank leads the connection. SCORE fits a corporate with many banks that wants one agreement and one onboarding. The underlying connectivity is the same in all three: the corporate connects through interface software, a service bureau, or a cloud service, and the messages are the same payment, statement, and confirmation messages. The CUG model shapes the agreements and the administration, not the message formats, and the choice should be revisited as the corporate banking footprint changes.

For a business analyst, the CUG model matters at onboarding and at troubleshooting. Onboarding a corporate means knowing which model applies, which agreements must be signed, and which administrator admits the member. Troubleshooting a failed corporate message means checking CUG membership alongside RMA authorisation and routing: a corporate outside the group cannot exchange the group traffic, however correct the message. The three models are the doors through which corporates enter the network, and each door has its own key, its own administrator, and its own onboarding path.
