Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Most cross border payments in Africa settle in dollars or euros, through correspondent banks outside the continent, even when the trade is between two African countries. The cost, the delay, and the dependence are widely recognised as problems. Regional settlement systems are the response: shared infrastructure that lets banks in neighbouring countries settle cross border payments directly, in regional or local currencies, without routing through correspondent banks in New York or London. Two systems illustrate the pattern: SADC-RTGS in Southern Africa, and the COMESA Regional Payment and Settlement System in Eastern and Southern Africa.

SADC-RTGS is the real time gross settlement system of the Southern African Development Community. It settles cross border payments between banks in SADC member states, in real time, in central bank money, hosted in South Africa. The system initially settled in rand, the currency of the region largest economy, and the design allows for additional currencies as member states join and the arrangements develop. A bank in one member state can pay a bank in another through SADC-RTGS, with the settlement happening in the regional system rather than through a correspondent chain. The result is faster settlement, lower cost, and less dependence on external correspondent banking.

The COMESA system takes a different approach: local currency settlement through a central clearer. The Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa, COMESA, operates the Regional Payment and Settlement System, known as REPSS, run by the COMESA Clearing House. REPSS lets banks settle cross border payments in their local currencies: a bank in one member state pays in its own currency, the Clearing House converts and settles, and the receiving bank is credited in its own currency. The central clearing model reduces the number of currency conversions and correspondent hops, and it keeps the settlement inside the region.

Both systems address the same structural problem. Intra-African trade is a small share of Africa total trade, and the payment infrastructure is part of the reason: settling a payment between two African countries through dollars, through two correspondent banks, takes days and costs a significant share of the payment value, particularly for small payments. Regional systems cut the chain: fewer hops, fewer conversions, faster settlement, and lower cost. The systems are part of a wider integration agenda, alongside the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System, PAPSS, which aims to connect the regional systems into a continental network.

The design questions for a regional system repeat everywhere. Settlement currency: a single regional currency, or local currencies with central conversion? Membership: which banks, which countries, and under what access criteria? Liquidity: how do participants fund their positions, and in which currency? Legal framework: which law governs settlement finality, and is it recognised in all member states? And governance: who operates the system, who sets the rules, and how are the costs shared? The answers differ by region, but the questions are the same, and a system that cannot answer them clearly will not attract participants.

For a business analyst, regional settlement systems are a study in integration patterns. Shared infrastructure, multi-currency settlement, local currency clearing, and central conversion are patterns that recur in every region building cross border payment links. The analyst designing a corridor into or within Africa must know which regional systems cover it, which currencies they settle, and how the routing decision is made between the regional system, the correspondent chain, and the continental platforms. Regional integration is built payment system by payment system, and the systems described here are the Southern and Eastern African chapters.
