What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain documents against payment and documents against acceptance. Documents against payment, usually written as D/P, means the collecting bank may release the documents to the importer only after the importer has paid the full amount stated in the collection instruction.

Why it matters. This matters because cross-border payments involve more banks, more charges, more time and more ways to fail than any domestic payment. Getting the chain right is the job.

Chapter objective: Learner explains documentary collections, the roles of remitting and collecting banks, and the SWIFT Category 4 messages used at each stage. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Documents against payment
  • Documents against acceptance
  • Bill of exchange
  • Risk difference between D/P and D/A
  • Exporters mixing D/P and D/A by buyer

Key terms

Negotiable instrument: A document, such as a cheque, bill of exchange or promissory note, that can be transferred from one person to another, with the holder having the right to receive payment.

Bill of exchange: A written order by one party instructing another party to pay a stated amount to a named person on a stated date.

SWIFT: The Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, a member owned cooperative that provides secure messaging services for financial institutions.

Exercise

For a EUR 60,000 shipment of textiles from Portugal to the Netherlands, explain the difference between documents against payment and documents against acceptance: when the documents are released, who carries the risk, and what the buyer must do in each case.

Use the D/P and D/A definitions in this lesson, the bill of exchange, and the rule that a refused sight bill or a refused usance bill must be protested unless the instruction says otherwise.

  1. What to do. State when the documents are released under each term, who carries the risk, and what the bill of exchange does in a D/A collection.

Correct result. Under D/P the Dutch buyer must pay EUR 60,000, plus any collection charges, before it can collect the fabric. Under D/A the buyer would receive the documents by accepting a bill of exchange, promising to pay on a future date, such as 60 days after sight or a fixed date. D/P gives the exporter payment before the importer takes the goods, and D/A gives the importer the goods before it pays.

Why. D/P and D/A allocate risk in opposite directions. Choosing the wrong term hands the goods to the buyer before payment, or demands payment before the buyer has seen the goods.

Documents against payment, usually written as D/P, means the collecting bank may release the documents to the importer only after the importer has paid the full amount stated in the collection instruction. The importer pays at the presenting bank counter, or by transfer to the collecting bank, and the bank then hands over the original bill of lading and the other documents. The exporter keeps control until the money is in hand. For a EUR 60,000 shipment of textiles from Portugal to the Netherlands, D/P means the Dutch buyer must pay EUR 60,000, plus any collection charges, before it can collect the fabric from the port of Rotterdam. Documents against acceptance, usually written as D/A, means the collecting bank may release the documents when the importer accepts a bill of exchange, also called a draft, promising to pay at a future date. The importer signs the draft, which states the amount, the currency and the maturity date, for example EUR 140,000 payable 90 days after sight. The bank hands over the documents, the importer takes the goods and sells them, and the exporter waits for the maturity date to receive payment. The exporter has given trade credit, and the only security is the signed acceptance and the commercial relationship. The bill of exchange is the key instrument in a D/A collection, so it deserves close attention. It is a written order by the exporter, the drawer, instructing the importer, the drawee, to pay a stated sum at a stated time. When the importer signs it, the importer becomes the acceptor and is legally bound to pay on the due date. The draft may be payable at sight, which means immediately, or at a fixed period after sight, such as 60 days, or on a fixed calendar date, such as 15 March 2027. A draft accepted by a strong buyer can sometimes be discounted at a bank, so the exporter can receive funds early for a fee. The risk difference between D/P and D/A is stark, and exporters must weigh it against the commercial need to offer credit. Under D/P the exporter risk is limited, because if the buyer does not pay, the documents stay with the bank and the goods can be resold or returned. Under D/A the exporter releases the documents and the goods on a promise, so if the buyer becomes insolvent before the maturity date, the exporter may lose both the goods and the money. A EUR 200,000 D/A collection with 120 days credit is therefore a far larger risk decision than the same amount on D/P terms, and it should be approved as a credit decision, not just a shipping decision. In practice, many exporters use a mix of the two terms according to the buyer and the market. A new buyer in a new market will usually be asked for D/P, or even payment in advance, until trust is built. A long standing buyer with a clean payment record may be offered D/A at 30, 60 or 90 days as a competitive advantage. Some collections combine the two, for example part payment on presentation and acceptance of a draft for the balance. The collection instruction must state the terms in plain words, including the amount, the currency, the maturity date if any, and whether partial payment or partial delivery is allowed.

Documents against payment, D/P, is the form of collection where the collecting bank releases the documents to the drawee only when the drawee pays the amount of the bill of exchange. The drawee pays, the bank releases the documents, and the drawee uses the documents to take delivery of the goods. The exporter is paid when the drawee pays, and the exporter carries the risk that the drawee refuses to pay. D/P is used when the exporter wants payment before the importer takes the goods, and when the goods are shipped on a sight bill of exchange, payable on presentation.

Documents against acceptance, D/A, is the form where the collecting bank releases the documents to the drawee when the drawee accepts the bill of exchange, promising to pay on a future date. The drawee accepts the bill, the bank releases the documents, and the drawee takes the goods immediately, but pays later. The exporter carries the risk that the drawee does not pay on the maturity date. D/A is used when the importer needs the goods before it can pay, for example when it needs to sell the goods to raise the money, or when the trade terms give the importer a credit period, such as 60 days or 90 days after shipment. The bill of exchange in a D/A collection is a usance bill, payable at a future date, rather than a sight bill.

The bill of exchange is the instrument that makes the collection work. It is a written order, signed by the drawer, the exporter, ordering the drawee, the importer, to pay a stated sum to a stated person on a stated date. A sight bill is payable on presentation, when the drawee sees it. A usance bill is payable at a future date, such as 60 days after sight or 90 days after the bill of lading date. The drawee accepts a usance bill by signing it, and the acceptance is a promise to pay on the maturity date. The accepted bill is a negotiable instrument, and the exporter bank may discount it, paying the exporter before the maturity date and collecting from the drawee on the maturity date.

The aval is a guarantee added to a bill of exchange by a bank, normally the collecting bank or the drawee bank. An avalised bill is one where the bank guarantees that the drawee will pay. An aval increases the value of the bill, because the exporter can rely on the bank rather than only on the drawee. An aval is not a letter of credit, and it is not automatic. The drawee bank adds the aval only if it agrees to guarantee the drawee, and it charges a fee for doing so. An SME should ask whether a bill in a D/A collection is avalised, because an avalised bill is much safer for the exporter than a bill accepted only by the drawee.

If the drawee refuses to pay a sight bill, or refuses to accept a usance bill, the collecting bank must protest the bill, unless the instruction says otherwise. A protest is a formal statement, made by a notary or a public authority, that the bill was presented and was dishonoured. The protest preserves the exporter right to take legal action against the drawee and against any endorser of the bill. The collecting bank should protest without delay, and should advise the remitting bank of the dishonour by SWIFT, normally with an MT416 advice of non-payment or non-acceptance. A bill that is not protested may lose its legal force, and the exporter may lose its remedy. The instruction should therefore state whether the collecting bank should protest, and the collecting bank should follow that instruction.

The maturity of a usance bill is the date the drawee must pay. The maturity is calculated from the tenor, for example 60 days after sight, or 90 days after the bill of lading date. The collecting bank should present the bill for payment on the maturity date, and should advise the remitting bank if the drawee does not pay. The exporter bank may hold the accepted bill until maturity, or it may discount it. If the drawee does not pay on maturity, the collecting bank protests the bill and advises the remitting bank, and the exporter may take legal action. The maturity date should be stated clearly in the collection instruction, and the bank should diary it, because a missed maturity date is a common operational failure.

An SME should remember that D/P gives the exporter payment before the importer takes the goods, and D/A gives the importer the goods before it pays. The choice between them is a credit decision, and it should be made by the exporter based on its trust in the importer and its need for cash. A business analyst who advises on collections should ask whether the collection is D/P or D/A, whether the bill is sight or usance, whether the bill is avalised, and what the instruction says about protest. Those four questions determine the risk, the timing, and the handling of the collection.
