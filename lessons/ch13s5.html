Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Liquidity is the most practical problem in correspondent banking, and it is the problem that operations and treasury teams spend most of their time managing. A nostro account must have enough balance to fund the payments that will be sent from it today, but holding too much balance is wasteful, because money sitting in a nostro account earns little or no interest and could be used elsewhere. The treasury team must forecast the payments that will go out and the payments that will come in, and must fund the account to cover the net outflow, with a buffer for unexpected payments.

If the forecast is wrong and the balance runs out, payments will be queued or rejected, and customers will be disappointed. If the forecast is too generous, the bank is holding idle money in a foreign currency for no reason. Funding a nostro account is itself a payment, and it takes time. To increase the dollar balance in New York, a bank in India must send dollars from another account or buy dollars in the foreign exchange market and settle the purchase into the nostro account. This funding must be arranged before the payments go out, which means the treasury team works a day ahead, forecasting tomorrow's payments today and funding the account tonight.

The cut off times of the correspondent matter here. A funding payment that arrives after the correspondent's cut off will not be available until the next day. A business analyst who writes requirements for a cross border payment product must include the funding process, the forecasting, and the cut off handling, because a payment product that cannot fund its nostro account cannot send payments at all. The cost of holding liquidity is also a reason why cross border payments are more expensive than domestic payments.

A domestic payment settles in central bank money within the same country and the same day, so the liquidity need is small. A cross border payment sits in a nostro account, sometimes for days, while the payment is processed, cleared and settled. The bank charges the customer for the payment, for the foreign exchange, and for the use of its balance sheet, because all three have a cost. New models, including payment system linkages and central bank digital currency arrangements, aim to reduce the need to hold large nostro balances by settling payments directly between central banks, but these models are still in development.

For now, the nostro account and the liquidity it holds are the engine of cross border payments, and the analyst must understand how that engine is funded.
