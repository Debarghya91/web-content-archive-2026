Key terms

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Show transcript

UBS payment operations are built around its private banking and wealth management business. A private banking client in Zurich may hold accounts in Swiss francs, euros, US dollars, and pounds, and may instruct payments in any of those currencies to beneficiaries in 50 countries. The UBS payment hub handles these instructions with a high level of manual service, a relationship manager may call the payments team to confirm a payment of 2 million Swiss francs to a property seller in London, and the team will check the instruction, confirm the exchange rate, and release the payment through SWIFT or through the local clearing system. The volumes are lower than at a retail bank, but the value per payment is far higher, and the tolerance for error is close to zero. Goldman Sachs payment operations are built around its institutional business. The bank moves money for hedge funds, pension funds, asset managers, and large corporations, and it settles trades in equities, bonds, and derivatives across the world. A single day can see Goldman Sachs settle more than 1 trillion dollars of securities transactions, each of which involves a cash payment leg. The payment hub is therefore tightly integrated with the trading and settlement systems, when a trade is executed, the payment instruction is generated automatically, matched against the counterparty instruction, and settled through the relevant clearing system. The bank also runs a transaction banking business, Goldman Sachs Transaction Banking, which offers corporate clients a modern API-based platform for payments, liquidity management, and virtual accounts. The private banking and wealth payment flows at both banks have features that a retail bank rarely sees. A UBS client may instruct a payment by signed letter, by telephone with a call-back confirmation, or through a secure messaging system, and each channel has its own authentication rules. A Goldman Sachs client may need to pay a capital call of 10 million dollars to a private equity fund, with the payment released only when two authorised signatories have approved it online. Both banks apply enhanced due diligence to large payments from wealth clients, checking the source of funds and the purpose of the payment before release. A payment of 5 million dollars from a client in Singapore to a beneficiary in Switzerland will be reviewed by the compliance team even if the automated screening clears it, because the value alone triggers a manual review. The institutional payment services at Goldman Sachs include custody payments, where the bank pays dividends and interest to the owners of securities it holds in custody, and fund administration payments, where the bank pays redemptions to investors in a fund. These payments are generated in bulk, often tens of thousands at a time, and must be accurate to the cent, because an error of one cent on a dividend payment to 100,000 investors creates 100,000 complaints. UBS offers similar custody and fund services through its global custody business. Both banks have invested in automation for these bulk flows, with straight-through processing rates above 99 percent, because manual handling at that volume would be impossible. The approach to payment technology at both banks is to buy the best specialist systems and integrate them, rather than building everything in-house. UBS uses a central payment hub supplied by a specialist vendor, connected to its core banking system and to SWIFT, and it has added API layers so that wealth clients can instruct payments through the UBS mobile app. Goldman Sachs built its transaction banking platform on modern cloud technology, with APIs as the primary channel, and it markets the platform to corporate clients who want to integrate payments directly into their own treasury systems. The lesson for a business analyst is that the right technology choice depends on the client base, a wealth manager needs security and service, an investment bank needs speed and integration, and a retail bank needs volume and cost. There is no single best payment platform, only the platform that fits the business.
