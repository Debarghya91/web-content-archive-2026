Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

SWIFT Go is the SWIFT service for low-value cross-border payments, designed for the payments that individuals and small businesses send every day, such as a EUR 400 payment to a family member or a USD 2,500 payment to a supplier, where the customer cares more about speed, cost and certainty than about the correspondent chain underneath. It is built on gpi, so every SWIFT Go payment carries a UETR and can be tracked, but it adds a promise that the payment will be fast, that the fees will be known upfront, and that the amount the beneficiary receives will be predictable. Source: SWIFT Go service description, and SWIFT CBPR+ usage guidelines. Why SWIFT Go exists is that low-value payments were the worst served by correspondent banking. A EUR 400 payment could take three days, lose EUR 25 in correspondent charges, and arrive as EUR 375, and the sender would not know any of that until the beneficiary complained. The customer experience was so poor that customers moved to fintechs and card schemes for small payments, even though the bank could have served them. SWIFT Go answers by setting rules for participating banks: the payment must be processed quickly, the charges must be disclosed upfront, and the beneficiary must receive the amount that was promised, so the customer can compare the bank with a fintech on equal terms. The parties are the debtor bank, which offers SWIFT Go to its customer, the beneficiary bank, which must be a SWIFT Go participant to receive the payment under the service rules, and the intermediaries, which must forward the payment quickly and without deducting unexpected charges. The customer is the reason for the service, and the customer experience is the product: the sender sees the fee before sending, the tracker shows the payment moving, and the beneficiary receives the promised amount. A bank that offers SWIFT Go but does not meet the service rules will have its payments fall back to ordinary gpi, and the customer will see the difference. How it works is shown by a worked example. A customer in Paris sends EUR 850 to a relative in Manila on Monday at 09:00 using SWIFT Go. The Paris bank charges a fee of EUR 8, disclosed upfront, and sends the payment with the UETR. The payment travels through one intermediary, which deducts no charge because the service rules require the charges to be covered by the sender fee, and the Manila bank credits the beneficiary EUR 850, less no further deduction, at 11:45 on the same day. The customer tracks the payment on the gpi tracker, sees it credited at 11:45, and the beneficiary receives exactly EUR 850. The total cost was EUR 8, known before the payment was sent, and the total time was under three hours. On success the customer gets a fast, cheap, predictable payment, and the bank keeps a customer who would otherwise have used a fintech. On failure the payment can fall outside the service rules, for example because the beneficiary bank is not a SWIFT Go participant, and then the payment travels as an ordinary gpi payment, takes longer, and may lose charges along the way, so the customer experience is worse than promised. The recovery is to tell the customer upfront whether the destination is covered by SWIFT Go, and to offer an alternative if it is not, because a promise made and broken is worse than no promise at all. The data that makes SWIFT Go work is the UETR, the amount and currency, the beneficiary bank BIC, and the charge arrangement, which must be set so that the beneficiary receives the promised amount. The amount matters because SWIFT Go is for low-value payments, typically up to USD 10,000, and a payment above that limit is not eligible and must travel as an ordinary gpi payment. A BA must specify the eligibility check, the upfront fee disclosure, the tracker updates and the fallback when the destination is not covered, because each of those is a customer promise, and a missing one is a complaint waiting to happen. Edge cases show the boundaries. A SWIFT Go payment that is held for sanctions screening will be delayed, and the bank must tell the customer that the delay is a compliance hold, not a service failure, and must give a realistic time for release. A SWIFT Go payment in a currency that needs conversion will have an exchange rate applied, and the rate must be disclosed upfront, because a rate that is worse than the customer expected is a fee by another name. A SWIFT Go payment to a country with no participating beneficiary bank cannot be a SWIFT Go payment at all, and the bank must say so before the customer sends it. The effect on the bank is a product it can sell against fintechs, with a tracker that proves the speed and a fee that is disclosed upfront, which builds trust. The effect on the customer is a payment that arrives fast, costs what was promised, and can be tracked, which is what customers now expect for every payment. The effect on operations is a flow that must be straight through, because a low-value payment cannot afford manual handling, and a manual exception on a EUR 850 payment costs more than the fee earned on it. What a BA, PO or SME must understand is that SWIFT Go is a service promise, not just a message type, and the promise covers speed, cost and predictability together. The requirement must name the eligibility limit, the fee disclosure, the tracker updates, the participating destinations and the fallback when the destination is not covered. The test is to send one low-value payment, track it, and check that the beneficiary received exactly the promised amount in the promised time, and if any of those three fails, the service is not working. Further reading: SWIFT Go service description; SWIFT gpi service description; SWIFT CBPR+ usage guidelines. Last reviewed 7 November 2026.
