Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

AML: Anti money laundering, the set of laws, rules and controls that stop criminals from hiding the proceeds of crime through the financial system.

Anti-money-laundering screening comes in two kinds, and a bank that confuses them will miss the risk. Name screening checks people and organisations against lists: the customer, the beneficial owners, the directors, and any other party to the relationship. Transaction screening checks payments against lists: the payer, the payee, the intermediary banks, and the information in the message. Name screening runs at onboarding, at periodic review, and whenever a list changes, which is called delta screening. Transaction screening runs on every payment before it is released. The difference matters in practice. A customer who becomes sanctioned after onboarding must be caught by name screening on the day the list changes, not on the day they next make a payment, because by then the money may already have moved. Fuzzy matching is how a screening engine compares names, and it is the most technically demanding part of the control. The engine first normalises the name: it lowercases it, removes punctuation, and strips accents. Then it transliterates it, converting names written in Arabic, Cyrillic or Chinese script into Latin characters, because the lists are published in Latin script and the customer record may not be. Then it compares, using token-based matching that breaks the name into words and scores each word, phonetic matching that catches names that sound the same but are spelt differently, and edit distance, which counts how many single-character changes turn one name into another. The engine produces a score from 0 to 100. A score of 100 is an exact match. A score of 85 might be the same person with a different transliteration. A score of 60 is probably a different person with a similar name. The threshold decides which scores become alerts, and the threshold is a policy decision that must be tested. A worked example shows why fuzzy matching matters. The name Mohammed can be spelt Muhammad, Mohamed, Mohammad, Mehmet, or Mahmoud, and all of them can be the same person. A list entry for Muhammad Ali and a customer named Mohamed Aly will score highly on phonetic matching even though the spelling differs. The engine must match all the variants, and the analyst must then use secondary identifiers to decide whether it is the same person: the date of birth, the nationality, the address, and the identity document number. A match on name alone, with a different date of birth and a different nationality, is almost certainly a false positive. A match on name and date of birth together is almost certainly a true match. The system must show the analyst all the identifiers side by side, because a decision made on the name alone is a guess. List management is the operational discipline behind screening, and it is where most failures happen. The lists come from the authorities: the OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list, the European Union consolidated list, the United Nations lists, and the UK consolidated list. Updates arrive as daily deltas and as periodic full files. The update pipeline has five steps: download the file, parse it into the screening format, load it into the engine, test it with seeded names, and activate it. Every screening decision records the list version that was in force at the time, so that an investigation can reconstruct exactly what the engine knew. The service level is that a list update is applied within 24 hours of publication, and the update is tested before it goes live. A list update that is loaded but not tested can block thousands of legitimate payments, and a list update that is tested but not loaded leaves the bank screening against an old list. False positives are the cost of screening, and they are large. In a retail bank, 90 to 95 percent of screening alerts are false positives: the name matched, but the person is different. The cost is analyst time, payment delays, and customer complaints. A bank that generates 10,000 alerts a month and clears 9,500 of them as false positives is spending most of its compliance budget on noise. The false positive rate is measured per rule, per list, and per customer segment, and it is tracked as a key performance indicator. A rule whose false positive rate rises above 95 percent must be reviewed, because it is either badly tuned or badly designed. False positives are reduced in four ways, and a good screening operation uses all of them. Whitelisting records a cleared match, so that the same name does not alert again until the list changes; the whitelist entry records who cleared it, when, and why, and it expires when the list entry changes. Threshold tuning sets different thresholds for different segments: a higher threshold for low-risk retail customers, where a false positive is expensive and a true match is rare, and a lower threshold for high-risk corporate customers. Secondary identifiers auto-clear matches where the date of birth or nationality does not match, without human review. And good data reduces ambiguous matches: a customer record with a full name, a date of birth, and a nationality generates far fewer false positives than a record with a name alone. Each of these must be governed, because a whitelist that is never reviewed becomes a hole, and a threshold that is raised to cut the workload becomes a risk. For a business analyst, the screening work is a set of precise system requirements. The engine must screen names at onboarding, at review, and on list change, and must screen every payment before release. The matching must be fuzzy, with a documented threshold per segment. The list update pipeline must complete within 24 hours and must record the list version on every decision. The whitelist must expire when the list entry changes. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a known true match is caught, a test where a known false positive is auto-cleared by a secondary identifier, a test where a list update is applied and tested within 24 hours, and a test where a whitelisted match re-alerts when the list entry changes. The evidence for each test must be kept, because the supervisor will ask for it. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
