Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Recall: A request by the sending bank to the receiving bank to return funds for a payment already sent, usually because of a duplicate, a technical error or fraud.

Show transcript

The alert arrives with a score, a set of signals, and a payment, and the first question the analyst asks is whether the payment has already been released. If it has not, the case is a prevention case: the payment can still be stopped. If it has, the case is a recovery case: the money is gone, and the work is to get it back and to put the loss right. The distinction changes everything: the urgency, the customer conversation, and the accounting. A prevention case is measured in minutes. A recovery case is measured in days. The analyst checks the payment status first, before anything else, because the next step depends on it.

Customer contact is the most delicate step, because the analyst must verify the customer without giving the fraudster information. The script is written in advance and it is followed exactly. The analyst confirms the customer identity using the bank own verification, never by asking for a full password or a one-time code, because a fraudster who has taken over the account will know those. The analyst asks about the payment: did you make it, do you know the payee, did you authorise it. The answers are recorded word for word, because they are evidence. If the customer cannot be reached, the payment stays held, the team tries again on a schedule, and the case is escalated if the service level is at risk. Vulnerable customers get extra care: a longer call, a simpler explanation, and a named contact.

Blocking the account has levels, and each level needs authority. A soft block stops new payments but lets existing direct debits and standing orders continue, so that the customer rent and bills are still paid. A hard block stops all payments, including direct debits, and is used when the account itself is compromised. Closure is the final step, used when the account is a mule account or when the customer asks for it. Each level must be authorised by a person with the right authority, and the authorisation must be recorded. The customer must be told, in plain language, what is blocked and why, and what they need to do to get it unblocked. A customer who finds their account blocked with no explanation will complain, and the complaint will be upheld.

Evidence gathering is what the reimbursement decision and any law enforcement referral rest on. The evidence pack contains the payment record, the device and IP logs, the authentication record, the customer contact notes, and the analyst investigation notes. Each item is timestamped and attributed. The pack must show the whole story: what happened, when, how the bank detected it, what the bank did, and what the customer said. A pack that is missing the authentication record cannot prove the payment was authenticated. A pack that is missing the customer contact notes cannot prove the customer was warned. The pack is assembled as the case progresses, not at the end, because evidence gathered late is evidence that may already be gone.

The reimbursement decision follows the rules, and in the United Kingdom the rules are the Payment Systems Regulator regime that applies from 7 October 2024. The maximum reimbursement is 85,000 pounds per claim. The cost is split equally between the sending and the receiving payment service providers. Reimbursement must be made within 5 business days of the claim. The sending firm can only refuse where the victim acted with gross negligence, or where the claim was not made within 13 months. The assessment asks three questions: was the customer deceived, did the bank warn them, and did the bank check. If the bank warned the customer and the customer proceeded anyway, the claim may be refused. If the bank did not warn, or did not check, the claim must be paid. The decision and the reason must be recorded, and if the claim is refused the customer must be told why and how to complain to the ombudsman.

Fraud loss accounting is the part of the case that the finance team owns, and it must be right, because the loss is reported to the board and to the regulator. The loss is recorded in the general ledger as a fraud loss, with the amount, the date, and the category: authorised push payment fraud, card fraud, or account takeover. A provision is raised for the expected reimbursement, and it is released when the reimbursement is paid. If funds are recovered, through a recall to the receiving bank, the recovery is recorded against the same loss, so that the net loss is visible. The recall process itself has a workflow: the request to the receiving bank, the response, the partial recovery, and the write-off of the balance. The loss must be attributed to the right category, because the industry reporting aggregates by category and a misattributed loss distorts the picture.

Case closure has a checklist, and the case is not closed until every item is done. The money is accounted for, either recovered or written off. The customer has been told the outcome and the reason. The control gap that allowed the fraud has been identified and fixed, whether it was a rule that was too loose, a warning that was not shown, or a procedure that was not followed. The lessons learned are recorded and fed back into the rules, the procedures, and the training. For a business analyst, the workflow requirements are the clocks, the authorities, the evidence pack, and the closure checklist. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a prevention case stops a payment before release, a test where a recovery case follows the recall workflow, a test where a reimbursement decision is made inside 5 business days with a recorded reason, and a test where a fraud loss is recorded in the ledger with the correct category.
