Show transcript

A monitoring scenario starts with a typology and ends with a rule, and the path between them is the design work. Take structuring: the typology is that a criminal splits a large amount into smaller payments below a reporting threshold. The observable behaviour is multiple payments just under the threshold from the same customer in a short period. The scenario logic is: three or more cash deposits of 9,000 to 9,999 euros from the same customer in 7 days. The parameters are the count, the amount band, and the window. Each parameter must be justified: the count of three because two could be coincidence, the amount band because it sits just under the 10,000 euro threshold, and the window of 7 days because structuring happens quickly. A scenario without a documented justification for each parameter cannot be defended.

Threshold setting uses statistics, peer analysis, and business judgement together. The statistical method looks at the distribution of the population: if 99 percent of retail customers make fewer than 5 payments a day, a threshold of 10 payments a day will catch the outliers. Peer analysis compares a customer to their peer group: a cash-intensive business is compared to other cash-intensive businesses, not to a salaried employee, because the normal pattern is different. Business judgement overlays the risk appetite: a bank that is worried about a particular typology sets a lower threshold for it. A threshold that is right for retail is wrong for corporate, and a threshold that is right for domestic payments is wrong for cross-border payments, so the thresholds must be set per segment.

The tuning cycle is the discipline that keeps the scenarios honest, and it runs on a schedule, typically quarterly. Above-the-line testing takes a sample of transactions that fell just below the threshold and checks whether any of them were suspicious: if they were, the threshold is too high. Below-the-line testing takes a sample above the threshold and checks whether the alerts were genuine: if most were false positives, the threshold is too low. The metrics are alert volume, false positive rate, true positive rate, and time to disposition, and they are tracked per scenario. A threshold change must be approved, documented, and tested before it goes live, and the change must be reversible, so that a bad tuning decision can be undone.

Alert disposition is the most important field in the monitoring system, because it drives the tuning. The codes are standard: true positive, where the alert was genuine suspicious activity; false positive, where the activity was explained; inconclusive, where the evidence was not enough to decide; and duplicate, where the alert repeats an earlier one. Each disposition needs evidence: a true positive needs the suspicious activity report reference, a false positive needs the explanation and the evidence, an inconclusive needs the reason it could not be decided. A disposition of false positive with no explanation is not a disposition, it is a guess, and quality assurance will reopen it.

The analyst workflow for an alert has a defined sequence. The analyst opens the alert and reads the scenario, the threshold, and the transactions that triggered it. They pull the customer profile: the risk rating, the expected behaviour, the account history. They review the transactions: the amounts, the counterparties, the countries, and the pattern over time. They check external data: sanctions lists, adverse media, and the customer file. They decide: file a report, clear as false positive, or escalate for more investigation. They document the decision and the evidence. The service level depends on the risk: a high-risk alert within hours, a medium-risk alert within a day. An alert that sits unreviewed past its service level is a breach of the control.

Quality assurance sampling is the check on the checkers. A proportion of closed alerts is sampled and re-reviewed, typically 5 to 10 percent, with a higher rate for alerts closed as false positives, because that is where a missed true positive hides. The re-review is done by a different analyst, or by the quality assurance team, and the result is recorded. If quality assurance finds a missed true positive, the case is reopened, the original analyst is given feedback, and the scenario is reviewed, because a missed true positive may mean the scenario is wrong, not just the analyst. The quality assurance results are reported to the money laundering reporting officer, who owns the monitoring system.

Management information is what the money laundering reporting officer sees, and it must show whether the system is working. The report covers alert volumes by scenario, false positive rates, ageing of unreviewed alerts, the SAR conversion rate, which is the proportion of alerts that become reports, and the time to disposition. A SAR conversion rate that is very low means the scenarios are generating noise. A conversion rate that is very high means the scenarios may be missing activity. The officer uses the report to challenge the system: why is this scenario generating so many alerts, why is this team slower than the others, why has the conversion rate changed. For a business analyst, the requirements are the scenario design, the threshold governance, the disposition codes, and the management information. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a structuring pattern generates an alert, a test where a tuning exercise changes a threshold with documented evidence, a test where a false positive disposition is recorded with evidence, and a test where quality assurance finds and reopens a missed alert.
