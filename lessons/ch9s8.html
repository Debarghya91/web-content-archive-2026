Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Finality: The point at which a payment settlement becomes irrevocable and unconditional, so it cannot be reversed by the payer or the system.

Use the settlement models, the liquidity trade-off and the finality rules in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Transcript and full text

Consider a mid sized company in Bengaluru that needs to make four payments on the same morning, ten thousand rupees to a vendor who supplied office stationery, one lakh fifty thousand rupees to a consultant, twenty five lakh rupees to a supplier for raw material, and salaries to two hundred employees. A good analyst would not send all four through the same rail. The ten thousand rupee vendor payment could go through UPI if the vendor accepts it, or through IMPS if the payment starts from the corporate account.

The consultant payment of one lakh fifty thousand rupees fits IMPS comfortably and will be credited in seconds. The twenty five lakh rupee supplier payment must go through RTGS because of its value and because the supplier needs final settlement today before dispatching the goods. This example shows the decision logic an analyst should apply to every payment requirement. First, ask the value. Below the RTGS minimum, RTGS is not an option.

Second, ask the urgency. If the credit must happen in seconds, choose UPI or IMPS. Third, ask the volume. If there are hundreds of payments, choose a bulk system like NACH. Fourth, ask the operating hours.

If the payment must move at midnight on a holiday, choose UPI or IMPS, because RTGS will be closed. Fifth, ask the cost sensitivity. If the payment is low value and high volume, choose the cheapest rail that meets the timing need. Sixth, ask the finality requirement. If the payment must be legally final the moment it is sent, choose RTGS.

Writing these six questions into the requirements document makes the rail selection transparent and reviewable, which is exactly what a payments BA is expected to do. The same logic applies to failure handling and customer communication. If the UPI payment to the vendor fails, the app should offer IMPS as the fallback and explain that the money will still arrive in seconds. If the RTGS payment is submitted after the cut off, the system should warn the user before submission and offer NEFT as the alternative with a clear statement of when the credit will happen.

If the salary file has an error, the system should reject the file before submission with a clear list of the bad records, not after half the employees have been paid. These are the details that separate a payment product that works in a demo from a payment product that works in production, and they are the details this chapter has been building towards.
