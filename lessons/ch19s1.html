What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain liquidity. Liquidity, in the context of payment systems, is the ready availability of funds in a bank's central bank account to settle payments as they fall due.

Why it matters. For a business analyst, liquidity management is where payment operations meet treasury.

Chapter objective: Learner manages intraday liquidity, explains funding sources, queues, and gridlock, and calculates a bank liquidity position from opening balance, inflows, outflows, and reserves. Learner applies this skill in realistic banking scenarios, with clear examples and correct terminology throughout.

Key learning points

  • Large bank may send and receive payments
  • Payments team sees individual transactions
  • Reuse of incoming funds to pay outgoing
  • No customer noticed
  • And the daily review should compare

Key terms

Intraday liquidity: Cash available to a bank during the business day to settle payments as they fall due. Intraday liquidity comes from account balances, incoming payments and central bank facilities.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity, in the context of payment systems, is the ready availability of funds in a bank's central bank account to settle payments as they fall due. A bank can be solvent, with assets comfortably exceeding its liabilities, and still fail to settle a payment if it does not have enough liquid funds at the exact moment the payment is due. This is the central challenge of intraday liquidity management. Payments arrive and leave all day, in amounts that are hard to predict precisely, and the bank must have enough funds available at every moment to keep them flowing. The scale of the challenge is easy to underestimate. A large bank may send and receive payments worth many times its total central bank balance in a single day, because funds that arrive in the morning can be reused to fund payments in the afternoon. This reuse, often called liquidity recycling, is what makes the system work. It also means that timing matters enormously. A large incoming payment that arrives an hour late can leave a bank short for that hour, forcing it to queue its own outgoing payments or to borrow intraday funds at a cost. For a business analyst, liquidity management is where payment operations meet treasury. The payments team sees individual transactions, while the treasury team sees the overall funding position. The two must work together, with shared dashboards, agreed thresholds and clear escalation procedures. When liquidity is well managed, customers never notice it. When it is badly managed, payments queue, cut off times are missed, and in extreme cases settlement fails. Understanding this link is essential for anyone designing payment systems or operating them. See the scale with one bank's day. Bank A opens with EUR 120 million in its central bank account. During the day it sends EUR 900 million and receives EUR 880 million, so it turns its balance over seven and a half times. That reuse of incoming funds to pay outgoing payments is what makes the system work, and it is also what makes timing so dangerous. At 11 in the morning a EUR 150 million incoming payment that was expected at 10 in the morning still has not arrived, because the sending bank is itself waiting for funds. Bank A's balance falls to EUR 4 million, and three outgoing payments totalling EUR 60 million go into the queue. Treasury draws EUR 50 million of intraday credit against government bonds, the queue clears, and by 3 in the afternoon the delayed EUR 150 million arrives and the credit is repaid. Nothing failed, no customer noticed, but for one hour the bank was EUR 4 million away from being unable to pay. Liquidity management is where the payments team and the treasury team must work as one unit, and the analyst builds the bridge between them. The shared dashboard should show the live balance, the forecast for the rest of the day, and the value sitting in the queue, with agreed thresholds. An amber alert when the balance falls below EUR 20 million, a red alert below EUR 5 million, and a named person who acts on each. The escalation procedure should state who can draw intraday credit, how much collateral is available, and how long a top up takes. And the daily review should compare the forecast with what actually happened, because a forecast that is wrong by EUR 100 million every Tuesday has a cause, usually a regular large payment that nobody told treasury about, and finding it is cheaper than holding an extra EUR 100 million of idle balance every day.
