Sanctions and compliance in cross-border payments: screening and de-risking

Cross-border payments in depth, Lesson 8 of 8, Narration 4:24, D06 Cross-border payments

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain sanctions and compliance in cross-border payments and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. De-risking is the unintended consequence of tougher compliance, and the analyst must measure it.

Chapter objective: Learner explains cross-border payments in depth, traces correspondent banking chains, applies SWIFT gpi tracking and UETR, explains CBPR+ and the MT to MX migration, distinguishes serial from cover payments, prices FX, optimises nostro liquidity and applies sanctions screening across jurisdictions with correct terminology.

Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Exercise

  1. What to do. Work through the scenario and state the outcome with the reason.

Correct result. The bank compares the revenue against the screening cost and the sanctions risk, and if the cost is too high it exits the corridor, leaving the customers to find another route.

Why. De-risking is a commercial decision with a social cost, because it removes payment access from whole regions.

Cross-border payments are the hardest case for sanctions compliance, because a single payment can touch four or five jurisdictions, each with its own sanctions list. The United States enforces OFAC, the European Union maintains its own list, the United Nations has a consolidated list, and the United Kingdom enforces through OFSI. A name that is clear on one list can be a hit on another, and the lists change frequently, sometimes with no warning. A bank that misses a true hit can face a penalty running into hundreds of millions, so the incentive is to screen widely and to treat every hit seriously. Screening happens at every party and at every hop. The ordering customer, the beneficiary, the banks in the chain, and the words in the remittance information are all screened against the lists. Each bank in the chain screens with its own lists and its own matching rules, so a payment that passes the ordering bank's screen can still be stopped by an intermediary. The screening must happen before the payment leaves the bank, and the result must be recorded, because the bank has to prove to its regulator that every payment was screened and that the screening was effective. False positives are the daily reality of sanctions screening. A payment to a customer with a very common name can match hundreds of list entries, because the lists contain many people with similar names and limited identifying detail. Each hit must be reviewed by an analyst who decides whether it is a true match or a false positive. A large bank can see tens of thousands of screening alerts a day, and 95 percent or more of them are false positives. The cost is enormous: analysts, case management systems, and the delay to customers whose payments sit in a queue while an alert is reviewed. De-risking is what happens when the cost of compliance exceeds the revenue from a corridor. A bank that cannot make a corridor profitable, after the cost of screening, monitoring and reporting, exits the corridor. The number of active correspondent relationships has fallen sharply, and de-risking is the main driver. The cost falls on remittance customers and on small countries, whose payments become more expensive and slower because fewer banks serve them. Regulators and international bodies have warned about de-risking for years, but the economics have not changed, because the penalty for missing a hit is far larger than the revenue from the corridor. The cost can be put in numbers. A bank that processes 1 million cross-border payments a year spends on average about 3 dollars per payment on screening, alert triage and investigations, which is 3 million dollars a year. A single missed true hit that results in an enforcement action can cost hundreds of millions in penalties, plus the cost of the remediation programme that follows. That asymmetry is why banks screen aggressively and why they exit corridors. The business case for a corridor has to include the compliance cost, not just the payment revenue, and a corridor that looks profitable on fees alone can be loss-making once compliance is counted. Good screening is a combination of tuning, workflow and data. Fuzzy matching must be tuned to the corridor, because a name that is common in one region is rare in another, and the matching threshold should reflect that. The case management workflow must route alerts to the right analyst with the right context, and must enforce service levels so a payment does not sit for days. The UETR must be carried through the payment so an investigation can follow it across banks and across formats. And the screening must be fast enough not to break instant payment service levels, which means the engine must answer in under a second for an instant payment. For a business analyst, cross-border screening is a set of precise requirements. The payment hub must state which lists are screened, the matching thresholds per corridor, the alert workflow and its service levels, and the audit trail that proves every payment was screened. The de-risking decision must be recorded with its reasoning, so the bank can show its regulator why a corridor was exited. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment naming a sanctioned entity is blocked before it leaves the bank, and a test where a payment with a common name generates an alert that is reviewed and cleared within the service level, with the full decision recorded.
