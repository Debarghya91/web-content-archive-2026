What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain building a fraud prevention culture. Technology alone will not stop fraud, because criminals adapt faster than systems do.

Why it matters. Front line staff are often the first to spot something wrong, such as a customer who seems confused or pressured while making a large transfer, or a business customer whose invoice details have changed unexpectedly.

Chapter objective: Learner explains AML and KYC controls, including customer due diligence, transaction monitoring, and suspicious activity reporting, and identifies red flags in payment patterns. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key learning points

  • Technology alone failing against adapting criminals
  • Confirmation of Payee reducing fraud
  • Third line: internal audit testing the framework
  • Internal audit checking the first two lines
  • Testing the training: untested training unlearned
  • And the training completion rate

Key terms

AML: Anti money laundering, the set of laws, rules and controls that stop criminals from hiding the proceeds of crime through the financial system.

Exercise

Design the staff response when a customer looks pressured into a large transfer.

Front line staff are often the first to spot something wrong, such as a customer who seems confused or pressured while making a large transfer, or a business customer whose invoice details have changed unexpectedly. Training should be practical and refreshed regularly, using real cases from the bank own experience, not generic slides.

  1. What to do. State what the staff member should do, what training must cover, and how customers are part of the defence.

Correct result. The staff member should pause the transfer, ask calm and open questions about who the customer is paying and why, check the payee details against what the customer expects, and escalate the concern through a route that feels safe, with the authority to freeze the account and start recovery if fraud is suspected. Training must be practical and refreshed regularly, using real cases from the bank own experience, and it must cover the emotional side, because staff who handle fraud victims need to be calm, supportive and clear about what happens next. Customers are part of the defence too: banks invest in awareness campaigns that teach people to check payee details, to be suspicious of urgent payment requests, and to never share one time passcodes. Confirmation of payee, where the bank checks that the name on the account matches the name the customer typed, has reduced some types of fraud significantly.

Why. A strong fraud prevention culture starts with staff who understand the typologies, know what to look for, and feel safe escalating a concern.

Technology alone will not stop fraud, because criminals adapt faster than systems do. A strong fraud prevention culture starts with staff who understand the typologies, know what to look for, and feel safe escalating a concern. Front line staff are often the first to spot something wrong, such as a customer who seems confused or pressured while making a large transfer, or a business customer whose invoice details have changed unexpectedly. Training should be practical and refreshed regularly, using real cases from the bank's own experience, not generic slides. It should also cover the emotional side, because staff who handle fraud victims need to be calm, supportive and clear about what happens next. Customers are part of the defence too, and banks invest in awareness campaigns that teach people to check payee details, to be suspicious of urgent payment requests, and to never share one time passcodes. Confirmation of payee, where the bank checks that the name on the account matches the name the customer typed, has reduced some types of fraud significantly. Warning messages at the point of payment, sometimes called friction, are deliberately designed to make the customer pause and think before sending money to a new payee. The balance is delicate, because too much friction drives customers away, and too little lets fraud through, so the design of these warnings is a genuine product decision, not just a compliance checkbox. Governance ties all of this together through the three lines of defence. The first line, the business and operations teams, owns the day to day controls. The second line, compliance and risk, sets the policy, monitors effectiveness and challenges the first line. The third line, internal audit, independently tests whether the whole framework works. A BA will usually work in the first or second line, writing requirements, improving processes, or implementing new controls, and they need to understand who approves what and who is accountable when a control fails. The most important cultural point is that fraud and AML failures are treated as learning opportunities, not as occasions for blame, because a team that hides its mistakes will keep making them. The three lines of defence apply to fraud prevention as they do to every risk. The first line is the business and operations teams who process payments, apply the controls, and handle the exceptions. The second line is the risk and compliance teams who set the fraud policy, test the controls, and report to the board. The third line is internal audit, which checks independently whether the first two lines work. A fraud prevention culture is one where the first line owns the risk, the second line challenges it, and the third line verifies it, and where a failure in any line is reported, not hidden. A culture where the first line blames the second line, or where the second line is ignored, is a culture that will fail. The training programme is the mechanism that builds the culture. Every employee who touches payments must be trained on fraud: what it looks like, how to spot it, what to do when they see it, and who to tell. The training must be refreshed annually, and must include real cases, because a training that uses only theory will not change behaviour. The training must also be tested, because a training that is not tested is a training that may not have been understood. The test results must be recorded, and employees who fail must be retrained. A firm that cannot prove its employees were trained will be criticised in an examination. The metrics that measure a fraud prevention culture are the ones that show whether the controls work. The fraud loss rate, which is the fraud losses divided by the payment volume, shows whether fraud is rising or falling. The detection rate, which is the fraud detected before the money leaves divided by the total fraud attempted, shows whether the controls are effective. The time to detect, which is the time from the fraud to the detection, shows whether the monitoring is fast enough. And the training completion rate shows whether the culture is being built. The metrics must be reported to the board monthly, and the trend must be visible, because a metric that is reported once a year is a history lesson.
