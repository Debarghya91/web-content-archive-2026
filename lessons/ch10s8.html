Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Market practice: An agreed way of using a message standard in a particular market or community, covering field usage, codes and validation rules.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

The two largest US dollar payment systems have both completed their ISO 20022 migrations. CHIPS, the Clearing House Interbank Payments System, moved to ISO 20022 in April 2024. CHIPS processes around 500,000 payments a day with a value of around 1.8 trillion dollars, and all of those payments now use ISO 20022 messages. Fedwire, the Federal Reserve real-time gross settlement system, moved to ISO 20022 in July 2025. Fedwire processes around 800,000 payments a day with a value of around 4 trillion dollars, and all of those payments now use ISO 20022 as well. The migration changed the message format but not the settlement model. Fedwire is still a real-time gross settlement system. Each payment settles individually in central bank money, in real time, with finality. CHIPS is still a netting system. Payments are queued, netted multilaterally, and settled at the end of the day. What changed is the data. Both systems now carry structured party information, structured remittance data, and the same field definitions as the cross-border pacs.008 and pacs.009 messages. A payment that travels from a US bank through Fedwire and then cross-border through SWIFT now carries consistent data throughout. For banks, the migration required upgrading payment engines to parse and generate ISO 20022 XML, updating screening systems to use structured data, and updating reconciliation to match on the new references. The Federal Reserve and The Clearing House published implementation guides, test environments, and cutover plans, and banks had to certify their readiness before the migration date. The cutover was a single date for each system, not a phased coexistence, which meant banks had to be ready on the day or stop sending payments. For a business analyst, the practical impact is that US payment projects now assume ISO 20022. Your requirements should reference the Fedwire and CHIPS ISO 20022 usage guidelines, not the old proprietary formats. Your test cases should use ISO 20022 messages. Your exception handling should use the ISO 20022 status and return messages. And when a customer asks why a US payment carries more data than it used to, you can explain that the richer data improves screening, reduces false positives, and makes reconciliation faster.

Fedwire processes around 800,000 payments a day with a value of around 4 trillion dollars, and all of those payments now use ISO 20022 as well. Together the two migrations moved the majority of US dollar wholesale value onto the same message standard as the rest of the world.

The migration changed the message format but not the settlement model. Fedwire is still a real-time gross settlement system. Each payment settles individually in central bank money, in real time, with finality. CHIPS is still a netting system. Payments are queued, netted multilaterally, and settled at the end of the day. What changed is the data. Both systems now carry structured party information, structured remittance data, and the same field definitions as the cross-border pacs.008 and pacs.009 messages. A payment that travels from a US bank through Fedwire and then cross-border through SWIFT now carries consistent data throughout, which is the point of the migration: one data model from the domestic leg to the cross border leg.

What exactly changed in the messages is worth listing, because it drives the requirements. The old proprietary formats carried party names as free text with limited structure. The new ISO 20022 messages carry structured addresses with separate fields for street, town, country and postcode, purpose codes that say why the payment is being made, legal entity identifiers where available, and remittance information that can be structured rather than a single free text line. The debtor and creditor agents are identified with BICs, the end to end reference is carried consistently, and the message version is explicit in the name, for example pacs.008.001.08. An analyst who writes a requirement that says the payment carries the beneficiary name must now say which structured field carries it, because there is more than one.

For banks, the migration required upgrading payment engines to parse and generate ISO 20022 XML, updating screening systems to use structured data, and updating reconciliation to match on the new references. The Federal Reserve and The Clearing House published implementation guides, test environments, and cutover plans, and banks had to certify their readiness before the migration date. The cutover was a single date for each system, not a phased coexistence, which meant banks had to be ready on the day or stop sending payments. That is a hard cutover, and it is the riskiest kind: there is no fallback to the old format after the date, so the testing and the certification had to be complete before it.

Screening is where the richer data pays off first. Under the old format, a sanctions screen had to match against a free text name and address, which produced false positives whenever a name was similar to a sanctioned name. Under ISO 20022, the screen can match on structured fields, on the country code, on the purpose code and on the legal entity identifier, which reduces false positives and lets genuine payments through faster. The analyst should confirm with the compliance team which structured fields the screening engine now uses, and should make sure the payment initiation screens capture those fields, because a structured field left blank is a screening gap.

Reconciliation improves for the same reason. Under the old format, matching a payment to a trade or an invoice depended on a free text reference that could be truncated or mistyped. Under ISO 20022, the end to end reference, the instruction identification and the structured remittance data give reconciliation three separate keys to match on, and the pacs.002 status report carries the same references back, so the operations team can match a payment to its confirmation without manual work. The analyst should design the reconciliation around those three keys, and should specify what happens when one of them is missing: which key is the fallback, and who is told.

The cross border consistency is the strategic gain. A US dollar payment that leaves a US bank through Fedwire or CHIPS and then travels through SWIFT to a bank in Europe or Asia now uses ISO 20022 messages, but Fedwire and CHIPS each use their own ISO 20022 usage guidelines and implementation guides, not the SWIFT CBPR+ usage guidelines. The message names overlap, but field rules, versions and market practice differ by system, so data may still need to be translated at the boundary. Source: Federal Reserve Fedwire ISO 20022 implementation guide; The Clearing House CHIPS ISO 20022 usage guidelines. That means the data does not have to be translated at the boundary, the screening does not have to be repeated on a different format, and the tracking reference survives the whole journey. An analyst working on a cross border payment that starts in the US should trace the message from the Fedwire or CHIPS leg through the SWIFT leg and confirm the same references appear at each step.

For a business analyst, the practical impact is that US payment projects now assume ISO 20022. Your requirements should reference the Fedwire and CHIPS ISO 20022 usage guidelines, not the old proprietary formats. Your test cases should use ISO 20022 messages. Your exception handling should use the ISO 20022 status and return messages. And when a customer asks why a US payment carries more data than it used to, you can explain that the richer data improves screening, reduces false positives, and makes reconciliation faster. The migration is complete, the old formats are gone, and there is no reason to write a requirement against them.
