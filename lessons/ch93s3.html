What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain SDD in depth and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. SDD is the recurring payment scheme of Europe, and the refund rules are its core.

Chapter objective: Learner explains SEPA in depth, distinguishes SCT, SCT Inst, SDD Core and B2B, applies R-transaction reason codes, explains TIPS, Verification of Payee and the EPC rulebook change cycle, and determines reachability for banks across the SEPA area with correct terminology.

  • Acceptance criteria

Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Creditor: The party that is owed money and receives a payment, such as a supplier, a seller or a service provider.

The SEPA Direct Debit scheme, SDD, was launched on 2 November 2009, and it is the pull payment of the SEPA world. Instead of the payer pushing money, the creditor pulls it from the debtor account on the basis of a mandate, a signed authorisation from the debtor. There are two schemes. The Core scheme is for consumers and businesses, and it gives the debtor a refund right. The B2B scheme is for business debtors only, and it gives no refund right, which is why it is used for business-to-business collections where the debtor wants certainty. Both schemes run on the pacs.003 message for the collection and the pain.008 for the customer instruction, and both depend entirely on the quality of the mandate. The mandate is the legal foundation of every collection, and a weak mandate is the most common cause of a failed or refunded direct debit. The debtor signs the mandate, the creditor stores it, and the mandate carries a Unique Mandate Reference and the creditor identifier. The mandate can be paper or electronic, and an electronic mandate must still prove who signed it and when. A mandate amendment, for example a change of debtor account or a change of creditor identifier, must be recorded and carried in the collection, because the debtor bank checks the mandate data on the first collection. A mandate that cannot be produced when the debtor disputes a collection is a mandate that does not exist, and the collection will be refunded. The difference between Core and B2B is the refund right, and it changes the risk completely. Under the Core scheme, the debtor can claim a refund of an authorised collection within eight weeks of the debit date, no reason required, and can claim a refund of an unauthorised collection within 13 months. Under the B2B scheme, there is no refund right at all, which gives the creditor certainty but means the debtor bank must verify the mandate before the first collection and must check that the debtor is a business, not a consumer. A consumer account collected under the B2B scheme is a serious error, and the debtor bank that allowed it is exposed. A business analyst must be able to state, for any direct debit product, which scheme it uses and what refund right the debtor has. The timelines are fixed by the rulebook and they are the part most often got wrong. The creditor must pre-notify the debtor at least 14 calendar days before the collection, unless the debtor has agreed a shorter period, and the pre-notification can be the invoice itself. For the Core scheme, the first collection in a series must reach the debtor bank five interbank business days before the due date, and recurrent collections two days before. For the B2B scheme, the deadline is one interbank business day before the due date. The sequence types tell the debtor bank where a collection sits in a series: FRST for the first, RCUR for recurrent, FNAL for the final, and OOFF for a one-off. A first collection sent with the RCUR code will be rejected, and a recurrent collection sent as FRST will confuse the debtor bank mandate check. The R-transactions for direct debits are more numerous than for credit transfers, and each has its own deadline. A reject happens before settlement, when the debtor bank refuses the collection, for example because the account is closed or the mandate data is wrong. A refusal is the debtor telling their bank not to pay before the debit happens. A return happens after settlement, when the debtor bank sends the money back, and it must do so within five interbank business days of the settlement date for technical reasons such as insufficient funds. A refund is the debtor claim after the debit, eight weeks for an authorised Core collection and 13 months for an unauthorised one. A reversal is the creditor bank taking the money back, for example because the collection was duplicated. The reason codes make the cause machine-readable: MD01 means no mandate, AC01 means the account is closed, AM04 means insufficient funds, and the operations team must be able to read them without guessing. Take a worked example. A utility collects 86.40 euro a month from 40,000 customers. Each month it sends a pain.008 file with 40,000 collections, each marked RCUR, each carrying the mandate reference and the creditor identifier. The first collection for a new customer is marked FRST and is sent five interbank business days before the due date. On the due date, 38,900 collections settle. Around 700 return with AM04 for insufficient funds, 120 return with AC01 because the account was closed, and 30 are refunded within the eight-week window because the customer disputes the amount. The utility re-presents the insufficient funds collections once, charges no fee to the customer for the return, and writes off the closed accounts to its collections team. The whole operation depends on the mandate file being accurate, because a single wrong mandate reference can invalidate a whole batch. For a business analyst, direct debit work is mandate work. Every requirement should state the mandate capture method, the storage and retrieval procedure, the pre-notification period, the sequence type logic, and the R-transaction handling with its deadline. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a first collection is sent five days before the due date, a test where a recurrent collection is sent two days before, a test where a return arrives within five interbank business days, a test where an authorised Core refund is honoured inside eight weeks, and a test where a B2B collection from a consumer account is blocked.
