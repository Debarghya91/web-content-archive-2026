Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Show transcript

A commercial bank is the institution that holds money for people and businesses and moves it when they ask it to. A customer opens an account, deposits money, and the bank records a balance that the customer can draw on at any time. The bank lends part of the money it holds to borrowers, who pay interest, which is one of the ways the bank pays for its services. The third job, alongside holding deposits and making loans, is payments: moving money from one account to another, inside the same bank or to an account at a different bank. Almost every payment a beginner meets starts and ends at a commercial bank, because the payer and the payee each hold their account there.

When a customer instructs a payment, the bank checks the instruction before it moves anything. It confirms that the account exists, that the customer is entitled to use it, and that the balance or the agreed overdraft covers the amount. It screens the payment against sanctions lists and fraud rules, because moving money for a sanctioned party or a fraudster carries serious penalties. Only then does it debit the customer account, which means reducing the balance it owes the customer, and send the payment onward. The balance in an account is best understood as money the bank owes the account holder, and a payment is the bank settling that obligation by moving the claim somewhere else.

A central bank is the bank for the banks, and it does a different set of jobs from a commercial bank. It issues the currency of the country, in notes, coins, and electronic form, and it is the institution whose money every other bank trusts without question. It holds reserve accounts for commercial banks, which are accounts that banks use to settle payments with each other, in the same way that customers hold accounts at commercial banks. It operates the settlement system in which those reserve accounts are debited and credited, so that payments between banks become final in central bank money. It also sets the main policy interest rate, which influences what commercial banks charge borrowers and pay depositors, and it oversees the stability of the financial system, setting rules for banks and stepping in when the system itself is at risk.

The two kinds of bank meet in every payment that crosses from one commercial bank to another. The debtor bank, the bank that holds the payer account, debits its customer and asks for the value to move, and the settlement happens when the central bank debits the debtor bank reserve account and credits the creditor bank reserve account. The creditor bank, the bank that holds the payee account, then credits its customer, knowing the value has already arrived in final form. This is why a payment has two layers: the customer layer, where accounts at commercial banks are debited and credited, and the settlement layer, where reserve accounts at the central bank move. A payment is only complete when both layers have happened, and the time between them is one of the reasons payments can appear to leave one account before they arrive in another.

The distinction matters because the two institutions carry different risks and serve different customers. A commercial bank can fail, and depositors rely on regulation and deposit protection schemes for safety, while a central bank cannot run out of its own currency and its money is treated as the safest form of settlement. A commercial bank serves the public, businesses, and other banks, while a central bank normally does not offer accounts to the public and deals mainly with banks and with the government. For a business analyst, the practical point is that every payment design must name both layers: which commercial banks hold the customer accounts, and which settlement system and central bank accounts carry the final movement of value. Requirements that describe only the customer instruction will miss cut off times, liquidity needs, and the exact point of finality.

A single supplier payment shows both institutions doing their jobs. A manufacturer in London instructs its bank to pay a supplier in Manchester, and the manufacturer bank checks the instruction, debits the manufacturer account, and sends the payment into the settlement system. The central bank moves the value between the two banks' reserve accounts, the supplier bank credits the supplier, and the supplier can rely on the money because settlement is final. Remember the pattern and most of payments becomes easier: commercial banks hold customer money, lend it, and carry out instructions, while the central bank issues the currency, holds the banks' reserves, settles between banks, sets policy, and guards stability. Every later lesson builds on that pattern, from clearing to correspondent banking.
