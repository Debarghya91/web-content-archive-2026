Risks, UCP 600 and ISP98 Rules, and a Worked Example

Standby Letter of Credit, Lesson 7 of 7, Narration 3:35, D06 Cross-border payments

Note saved.

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain risks, ucp 600 and isp98 rules. The applicant bears the risk that the beneficiary will draw even when the applicant believes it has performed.

Why it matters. This matters because cross-border payments involve more banks, more charges, more time and more ways to fail than any domestic payment. Getting the chain right is the job.

Chapter objective: Learner explains what a standby letter of credit is, distinguishes it from a commercial letter of credit, and describes issuance, notification, drawing and expiry. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Applicant risk: beneficiary drawing despite performance
  • Beneficiary risk: non-compliant documents or expiry before drawing
  • Issuing bank bears credit risk
  • Standby letters of credit
  • Worked example: buyer in Chicago
  • Bank examining the documents on 21 and 22 April

Key terms

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

pacs.009: An ISO 20022 message for a financial institution to financial institution credit transfer with no customer beneficiary.

UCP 600: Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, revision 600, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs documentary credits.

Show transcript

The applicant bears the risk that the beneficiary will draw even when the applicant believes it has performed. Because the bank deals in documents, a beneficiary who presents a complying demand will be paid, and the applicant must then argue its case in a separate dispute. An applicant should therefore agree the standby wording with care, and should require a document that is hard to produce without a real default, such as a copy of a court order or an independent engineer certificate. A vague demand that needs only a signed statement gives the beneficiary a very wide right to draw. The beneficiary bears the risk that the documents will not comply, or that the standby will expire before a drawing is made. A beneficiary who presents documents one day after the expiry date loses the right to payment, even if the default is real. A beneficiary who omits a required document, or who claims an amount above the available balance, will face a refusal. The beneficiary must therefore diary the expiry date, keep a copy of the standby, and prepare the drawing documents in advance, so that a presentation can be made quickly when a default occurs. The issuing bank bears credit risk on the applicant, because it must pay the beneficiary and then recover the money from the applicant. If the applicant is insolvent, the bank may lose the amount paid. The bank also bears operational risk, because a wrong decision to pay or to refuse can lead to a claim. A bank that pays on discrepant documents may not be able to recover from the applicant, while a bank that wrongly refuses a complying presentation may be sued by the beneficiary. Careful examination and clear refusal notices are therefore essential controls. Most standby letters of credit are governed by ISP98, the International Standby Practices, which was written for standbys and covers demands, presentations, examination, and the honour or dishonour of a drawing. ISP98 states that a standby is independent, that the bank examines documents for apparent compliance, and that the bank has a reasonable time, not exceeding seven banking days, to examine and decide. Some standbys are governed by UCP 600, the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, which also covers commercial credits. The standby must state which set of rules applies, and the parties should not assume that one set applies by default. The worked example starts with a buyer in Chicago, Apex Retail Group, which asks Bank of America to issue a standby for 500,000 dollars in favour of Formosa Electronics in Taiwan, to guarantee payment for goods on 60 day terms. The standby is issued on 10 January 2026, expires on 10 July 2026, is governed by ISP98, and requires a signed demand and an unpaid invoice copy. Formosa delivers goods worth 210,000 dollars on 15 February 2026, and Apex fails to pay by 16 April 2026. On 20 April 2026 Formosa presents a demand for 210,000 dollars and invoice FE 4421 in Taipei. The bank examines the documents on 21 and 22 April, finds them complying, and pays 210,000 dollars on 23 April 2026 by pacs.009, the ISO 20022 financial institution transfer that replaced MT202 for cross-border payments when coexistence ended on 22 November 2025. The available balance falls to 290,000 dollars, and Bank of America debits Apex for 210,000 dollars plus fees that day.

The risks in a standby letter of credit fall on the applicant, the beneficiary, and the issuing bank, and each party must understand its own risk. The applicant risk is that the beneficiary draws the standby wrongfully, or draws it for an amount that is not due. The bank will pay on a complying demand, without investigating whether the applicant really defaulted. The applicant must therefore trust the beneficiary, or must have a legal remedy against the beneficiary if the drawing is wrongful. The applicant risk is reduced by stating the drawing conditions clearly, so that the beneficiary must present a specific document, such as a certificate of default, that is hard to fabricate. The applicant risk is increased by a standby that requires only a simple demand, with no supporting document.

The beneficiary risk is that the standby expires before it can draw, that the demand is refused for a discrepancy, or that the issuing bank fails to pay. The beneficiary reduces its risk by diarying the expiry date, by preparing the demand carefully, and by asking for confirmation if the issuing bank is weak. The beneficiary risk is increased by a standby with complex drawing conditions, a short expiry, or an issuing bank in a country with transfer risk. The beneficiary should examine the standby on receipt, and should not rely on a standby with terms it does not understand.

The issuing bank risk is that the applicant does not reimburse it after it pays the beneficiary. The bank reduces its risk by taking collateral from the applicant, by assessing the applicant creditworthiness, and by charging a fee that reflects the risk. The bank risk is increased if the collateral is inadequate, if the applicant credit deteriorates after the standby is issued, or if the bank fails to examine the demand within the five banking day period and is forced to pay a discrepant demand. The bank should monitor the applicant credit position for the life of the standby, and should act if the position deteriorates, for example by asking for additional collateral or by giving notice of non-extension under an evergreen clause.

The governing rules are UCP 600 and ISP98, and the standby should state which applies. UCP 600 is the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, published by the International Chamber of Commerce, and it applies to all letters of credit, including standby letters of credit, when the standby states that it is subject to UCP 600. ISP98 is the International Standby Practices, also published by the International Chamber of Commerce, and it is written specifically for standby letters of credit. ISP98 is the preferred rule set for standbys in the United States, and it is increasingly used internationally. The two rule sets differ in their treatment of the examination period, the expiry, the transfer, and the assignment of proceeds. An SME must know the differences, because they affect how the bank examines the demand and how the beneficiary can transfer the standby.

Under UCP 600, the examination period is a maximum of five banking days following the day of presentation. Under ISP98, the examination period is also five banking days, but ISP98 gives the bank more flexibility in the examination, and it allows the bank to seek clarification from the presenter. Under UCP 600, a standby that does not state an expiry date is valid for one year from the date of issue. Under ISP98, a standby that does not state an expiry date is valid until the issuing bank gives notice of termination. Under UCP 600, a standby is transferable only if it states that it is transferable. Under ISP98, a standby is transferable only if it states that it is transferable, but ISP98 gives more detail on the transfer process. The differences are subtle, but they matter in a dispute, and the standby should state the rule set explicitly.

Here is a worked example that brings the chapter together. A contractor, BuildWell Ltd, in London, wins a contract to build a warehouse for a project owner, StoreCo Ltd, for GBP 5,000,000. The contract requires BuildWell to give a performance standby for 10 percent of the contract value, GBP 500,000, as security for completion. BuildWell applies to its bank, Barclays, for a standby for GBP 500,000, in favour of StoreCo, expiring 31 December 2026, subject to ISP98, requiring a written demand stating that BuildWell has failed to complete the work. Barclays approves, takes a lien on BuildWell assets as collateral, charges a fee of 1 percent per annum, GBP 5,000 per year, and issues the standby by MT700 to StoreCo bank. StoreCo receives the advice, examines it, and accepts it.

BuildWell completes the work on time, and the standby expires on 31 December 2026 without being drawn. Barclays releases the lien, restores the BuildWell credit limit, and the standby is closed. Now consider the alternative. BuildWell fails to complete the work, and StoreCo presents a written demand to Barclays on 15 November 2026, stating that BuildWell has failed to complete the work, and demanding GBP 500,000. Barclays examines the demand, finds that it complies with the standby terms, and pays StoreCo GBP 500,000 within five banking days. Barclays then debits the BuildWell account, or enforces the lien on its assets, to reimburse itself. StoreCo uses the GBP 500,000 to pay another contractor to complete the work. The standby has done its job, it protected StoreCo against the BuildWell default, and it cost BuildWell GBP 5,000 per year in fees, plus the collateral. A business analyst who can walk through this example, with the amount, the expiry, the drawing condition, and the rule set, can explain a standby letter of credit to any client.
