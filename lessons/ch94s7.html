What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain cross-border instant payments and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Cross-border instant is the future of the market, and the links are the new infrastructure.

Chapter objective: Learner explains cross-border payments in depth, traces correspondent banking chains, applies SWIFT gpi tracking and UETR, explains CBPR+ and the MT to MX migration, distinguishes serial from cover payments, prices FX, optimises nostro liquidity and applies sanctions screening across jurisdictions with correct terminology.

  • It also changes the payment
  • Acceptance criteria

Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Domestic instant payment systems have changed what customers expect. Faster Payments in the UK, SEPA Instant in Europe, FedNow in the United States and UPI in India all settle in seconds inside one country, and customers now assume a payment to another country should be just as fast. It is not. A cross-border payment still travels through the correspondent chain and still takes hours or days. The gap between domestic instant and cross-border slow is now the most visible failure in the payments experience, and closing it is the main frontier in cross-border innovation. The simplest way to close the gap is to link two domestic instant systems directly, with a foreign exchange and compliance layer in between. Singapore's PayNow is linked to India's UPI, so a payment from a Singapore bank account to an Indian bank account settles in seconds, using a mobile number or a virtual payment address instead of an account number. PayNow is also linked to Thailand's PromptPay. The model works because both sides already have instant settlement, and the link only has to solve three problems: converting the currency, screening the payment, and agreeing the rules for errors and returns. The multilateral model goes further. The BIS Innovation Hub's Project Nexus is a design for a single hub that links domestic instant systems, so each country connects once to the hub instead of building a separate bilateral link with every other country. A country that joins Nexus can reach every other member through the hub, which standardises the message format, the foreign exchange quotation, and the compliance checks. Nexus is a design and a pilot, not a live network, but it shows the direction: the future is a network of networks, not a single global system. The hard problems are not the settlement. They are foreign exchange in real time, sanctions screening in seconds, and different rules. The exchange rate must be quoted and locked in seconds, because an instant payment cannot wait for a dealer to price it. The sanctions screen must answer in under a second, because the payment cannot sit in a queue while an analyst reviews a hit. Operating hours differ, message formats differ, and consumer protection rules differ: one country may guarantee a refund for a mistaken payment while the other does not. Each of these has to be agreed corridor by corridor, which is why progress is corridor by corridor rather than global. The live corridors today are mostly remittance-heavy. Singapore to India, Singapore to Thailand, Malaysia to Indonesia, and similar pairs carry the early volume. The volumes are small compared with correspondent banking, but they are growing fast, and they set the expectation for everything else. A customer who has sent money instantly to family abroad will not accept a three-day wait for a business payment on the same corridor. The pressure is now on the banks to extend instant links to business payments, which are larger, need richer remittance data, and face stricter screening. For the banks, instant cross-border changes the funding model. Correspondent banking settles on nostro accounts with end-of-day netting, so a bank can fund the day's payments in arrears. Instant settlement needs prefunded liquidity in the destination currency, available at the moment the payment is made. That means holding balances in more places, or using a liquidity provider that can fund on demand. It also changes the payment hub: the hub must route a payment to the instant link when the corridor supports it, fall back to the correspondent route when it does not, and tell the customer the expected arrival time before they confirm. For a business analyst, instant cross-border routing is a set of requirements. The hub needs a corridor capability table that states, for each corridor, whether instant is available, the maximum amount, the operating hours, and the expected arrival time. It needs a fallback rule for when instant is unavailable. It needs a real-time foreign exchange quote with a lock and an expiry. It needs a screening time budget that guarantees the payment is screened within the instant SLA. And it needs a customer disclosure that states the expected arrival time before the customer confirms. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment on a live instant corridor arrives in under a minute, and a test where the instant link is down and the payment routes through the correspondent chain with the customer told the new arrival time.
