Key terms

Execution date: The date on which the bank executes a payment instruction, debiting the debtor account and sending the message onward.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

UTC, or Coordinated Universal Time, is the world reference time, and every ISO 20022 timestamp is written as a UTC time plus an offset that shows the local time difference. A timestamp such as 2026-11-05T14:30:00+01:00 means half past two in the afternoon in a zone one hour ahead of UTC, which is half past one in the afternoon UTC. A timestamp such as 2026-11-05T09:15:00Z means a quarter past nine in the morning UTC exactly, because Z stands for zero offset. The offset matters, because the same local time can mean two different UTC times in different zones, and a payment that is dated half past two in the afternoon in Paris is not the same moment as half past two in the afternoon in New York.

Without the offset, the receiving system cannot know when the payment was created or when it should be executed. The rule exists because payments cross time zones, and a payment created in Tokyo, cleared in London, and settled in New York must have one unambiguous time. If each system recorded only its local time, the audit trail would be a puzzle, and a dispute about whether a payment met a cut off could not be resolved. With UTC and offset, every system can convert to its own local time and to UTC, and the sequence of events is clear.

The creation date and time in the group header is the anchor for this, because it records when the message was built, and the requested execution date records when the debtor wants the payment to happen. Both must be consistent, or the receiving bank will reject the instruction. Daylight saving makes the offset change during the year, and that change catches many systems out. Paris is UTC plus one hour in winter and UTC plus two hours in summer, so a timestamp written as plus one hour in July is wrong, even though the local time looks right.

The system must derive the offset from the date, not from a fixed setting, and it must handle the days when the clocks change, when a local time may occur twice or not at all. A business analyst should include daylight saving cases in the test pack, including a payment created on the day the clocks go forward and one on the day they go back, because those are the days when timestamps fail in production. Cut off times, value dates and settlement dates all depend on timestamps, and a wrong time can move a payment by a day.

A payment received after the cut off is executed on the next business day, so a timestamp that is one hour wrong can turn a same day payment into a next day payment. The value date is when the funds are available to the creditor, and it comes from the execution date and the clearing cycle. The settlement date is the date the banks settle between themselves, and it must match the value date rules of the scheme. A tester should check a payment just before the cut off, one just after it, and one on a bank holiday.

The screen must show the local time and value date and warn when a payment will execute on the next business day.
