Key terms

Suspense account: A temporary account used to hold funds or entries that cannot yet be posted to their final account. Banks use suspense accounts for unidentified receipts, pending investigations and items awaiting...

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Reject: A payment instruction that is refused before settlement because it fails validation, screening or account checks.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Every exception has a timeline, and the timeline is set by the rules of the payment system, the agreements between banks, and the law of the countries involved. A reject should be notified to the customer within one business day, because the payment never left the sending bank. A return should be completed within five business days, because the funds must travel back through the chain. A recall should be acknowledged within one business day and resolved within fifteen business days, although the actual recovery depends on whether the funds are still available.

An investigation should have a first response within two business days and a resolution within fifteen business days, with regular updates to the customer in between. The cost of an exception is borne by different parties depending on the cause. If the sender made an error, such as a wrong account number, the sender bears the cost of the return charges and the recall fee. If the bank made an error, such as sending the payment to the wrong bank, the bank bears the cost and should refund any charges to the customer.

If the beneficiary bank made an error, such as crediting the wrong account, the beneficiary bank bears the cost. If the payment was stopped for a compliance query and the query finds nothing wrong, the cost is usually borne by the sender, because the compliance check is a cost of doing cross border business, although some banks will waive the fee as a goodwill gesture. The analyst must include the cost allocation in the requirements, must ensure that the charges are applied correctly, and must ensure that the customer is told upfront what an exception will cost.

A customer who is surprised by a charge is a customer who will complain, and a complaint about a charge is harder to resolve than the exception itself. The analyst must also think about the cost that is not a fee, the cost of the customer's time, the cost of the delayed business, and the cost of the lost trust. A supplier who is paid late may charge a late fee, may refuse to ship the goods, or may refuse to do business again. An employee who is paid late may not be able to pay rent. A company that misses a payment deadline may breach a contract.

These costs are real, even though they do not appear on the bank's invoice, and they are the reason why exception handling matters. The analyst who understands the true cost of an exception will prioritise prevention, will design the handling to be fast, and will communicate with the customer honestly about what happened and when it will be fixed.
