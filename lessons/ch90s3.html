Key terms

Suspense account: A temporary account used to hold funds or entries that cannot yet be posted to their final account. Banks use suspense accounts for unidentified receipts, pending investigations and items awaiting...

Transit account: An internal account used to hold funds temporarily while they move between systems or accounts. Transit accounts record the movement of money during settlement, currency conversion or internal...

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

A suspense account is a parking place for money that cannot yet be posted to its final destination, and it exists because double entry gives the bank no choice. Every payment must be posted somewhere, and if the correct account is not yet known, the payment is posted to suspense rather than left unrecorded. A payment arrives with a beneficiary account number that matches no customer. A payment is returned by the clearing system with no reason code. A fee is charged but the product code is missing. A corporate customer sends a bulk file with one line that fails validation. In each case the money is real, it must sit on the balance sheet, and suspense is where it sits until operations can identify it.

Transit accounts are the close cousins of suspense accounts, but they hold money that is in flight rather than money that is lost. A Bacs file submitted on day one is processed on day two and settles on day three, and between the customer debit and the settlement the funds sit in a Bacs transit account. A payment is sent from the payment hub to the card system for clearing, and the funds sit in a card transit account until the clearing file arrives. A payment moves between two branches of the same bank, and the interbranch transit account holds it until both sides have posted. Transit balances should clear within a day or two. A transit balance that is still there after a week is not in transit any more, it is stuck, and it needs investigation.

The difference between suspense and transit matters for risk. A transit balance has a known destination and a known clearing time, so the risk is operational: the clearing file might be late, or the interface might fail. A suspense balance has an unknown destination, so the risk is higher: the money might belong to a customer who is waiting for it, it might be the proceeds of fraud, or it might be a duplicate payment that should be returned. This is why auditors and regulators treat aged suspense as a red flag. A suspense item over 30 days old must be explained, and a suspense balance that keeps growing suggests a systemic problem in the payment flow rather than a handful of one-off errors.

Clearing a suspense item means finding its true destination and posting a correcting entry, never deleting the original. An incoming SEPA credit transfer of 5,000 euros arrives with an IBAN that has one digit transposed. The bank posts it to suspense: debit the settlement account 5,000 euros, credit suspense 5,000 euros. Operations contact the sending bank, confirm the intended beneficiary, and then post the correction: debit suspense 5,000 euros, credit the customer's account 5,000 euros. The suspense account is now back to zero for that item, and the audit trail shows both entries and the reason. If the beneficiary cannot be identified after investigation, the funds are returned to the sender, again by an entry, not by deleting anything.

Ageing and ownership are the controls that keep suspense from becoming a dumping ground. Every suspense account has a named owner, usually in operations or finance, and a daily report that lists every item by age: 1 day, 5 days, 10 days, 30 days and over. Items over 10 days old are escalated to a team leader. Items over 30 days old are escalated to management and reported to the audit committee. Some banks set a hard rule that no suspense item may be older than 60 days without a written explanation signed by a senior manager. Unallocated funds can also be a conduct issue: if the bank holds a customer's money in suspense and does not tell them, it is failing its duty to the customer, and if the funds are the proceeds of crime the bank may be breaching anti-money-laundering law by holding them without acting.

For a business analyst, suspense and transit accounts must be designed into every new payment flow, not discovered afterwards. The requirements must state which suspense account each failure mode uses, what information is captured with the item so it can be identified later, who owns it, and how quickly it must clear. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment with an invalid beneficiary is posted to suspense, a test where it is identified and cleared, and a test where a suspense item over 30 days old appears on the ageing report. A flow with no suspense design is a flow that will fail the first time a real payment goes wrong. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: the Basel Committee guidance on internal controls and suspense accounts; the Financial Conduct Authority expectations on client money and unallocated funds; the European Banking Authority guidelines on payment account reconciliation.
