What Is Real and What Is Hype

CBDCs, Stablecoins and AI in Payments, Lesson 6 of 9, Narration 4:13, D15 Advanced and emerging payments

Note saved.

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain is real and what. Payments attract more hype than most industries, because money is universal and every new technology claims it will transform how money moves.

Why it matters. This matters because the next payment product you work on may use one of these technologies. Knowing what is real and what is still a proposal is what separates a good analyst from a confused one.

Chapter objective: Learner explains central bank digital currencies, stablecoins, and AI in payments, comparing them with existing rails on speed, settlement, risk, and regulation, and assesses a realistic use case. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key learning points

  • Why payments attract more hype than most industries
  • Real time payments pass
  • Confusing a good demonstration with a working product
  • Real time payments versus decentralised finance
  • Pilot: usually in pilot
  • Written evaluation with a recommendation, not a verbal opinion

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Open banking: A system where banks share customer account data and payment services with licensed third parties through secure APIs, with customer consent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Payments attract more hype than most industries, because money is universal and every new technology claims it will transform how money moves. The skill of separating what is real from what is hype is therefore a professional one, and it can be learned. A useful test is to ask three questions about any new payment idea. First, does it solve a real problem for a real user, in a way that is better than what exists today? Second, can it work at scale, with real volumes, real compliance requirements and real customer support? Third, does it have a sustainable business model, where someone pays for the service and the economics work without subsidies? An idea that passes all three is worth taking seriously. An idea that fails the first is a solution looking for a problem, and an idea that fails the second or third will stay a pilot forever. Real time payments pass the test clearly. They solve the problem of slow settlement, they work at national scale in dozens of countries, and they are funded by banks and central banks as core infrastructure. Open banking passes too, more slowly, because it solves the problem of fragmented financial data and it has regulatory backing, even if the business models are still maturing. Stablecoins partially pass, because they solve a real cross border problem for some users, but their regulatory status and reserve models are still being settled. Fully decentralised finance, where lending and payments happen without any regulated institution, has so far failed the scale and compliance tests, because it cannot handle customer protection, fraud recovery or anti money laundering in the way regulators require. The most common mistake is to confuse a good demonstration with a working product. A pilot that moves a few thousand dollars between friendly participants proves that the technology works, but it does not prove that the product works, because the hard parts, compliance, customer support, reconciliation, dispute handling and regulatory approval, are usually not in the pilot. A BA should always ask what the pilot did not test, and what would have to change for the product to handle a million payments a day. The payments industry changes more slowly than the headlines suggest, but it does change, and the professionals who thrive are the ones who learn the new ideas early, test them honestly, and adopt the ones that genuinely make payments faster, cheaper and safer for the people who use them. Take real time payments and decentralised finance as a comparison. Real time payments solve the problem of slow settlement, they work at national scale in dozens of countries, and they are funded by banks and central banks as core infrastructure. They pass all three tests: real problem, real scale, sustainable funding. Fully decentralised finance, where lending and payments happen without any regulated institution, has so far failed the scale and compliance tests, because it cannot handle customer protection, fraud recovery or anti money laundering in the way regulators require. It may pass those tests one day, but as of 2026 it has not, and a BA who recommends it for a real customer payment today is making a mistake. The most common mistake is to confuse a good demonstration with a working product. A pilot that moves a few thousand dollars between friendly participants proves that the technology works, but it does not prove that the product works, because the hard parts, compliance, customer support, reconciliation, dispute handling and regulatory approval, are usually not in the pilot. A BA should always ask what the pilot did not test, and what would have to change for the product to handle a million payments a day. If the answer is that everything would have to change, the pilot proved nothing about the product. The BA's job is to be the honest evaluator in the room. When a vendor or a stakeholder presents a new technology, the BA should ask for the evidence: the volumes it has handled, the compliance requirements it meets, the business model that funds it, and the failure modes that have been tested. The BA should write the evaluation as a short document with a recommendation, because a verbal opinion is forgotten and a written evaluation is accountable.
