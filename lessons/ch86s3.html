The EU Instant Payments Regulation 2024/886: the 10-second rule and verification of payee

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain the EU Instant Payments Regulation 2024/886 and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. The 10-second rule changes every part of the payment chain, from screening to liquidity to error handling.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the regulatory framework that binds payments in Europe, the UK, the US and India, distinguishes PSD2 from PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation, and applies DORA, the Instant Payments Regulation, sanctions regimes, AML rules, RBI directions and FFIEC guidance to realistic payment scenarios with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Regulation (EU) 2024/886
  • Fraud scoring deciding in well under a second, in memory
  • Receiving: 24 hours day
  • Regulated too
  • Payee bank
  • Last reviewed 8 November 2026

Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Fraud scoring: A risk score assigned to a payment based on factors such as amount, location, device, velocity and customer history.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Regulation (EU) 2024/886, the Instant Payments Regulation, amends the SEPA Regulation 260/2012 and changes what every euro payment service provider must do. It was adopted in March 2024. The headline rule is the 10-second rule: an instant credit transfer must be executed within 10 seconds of the time of receipt, measured from the moment the payer payment service provider receives the payment order to the moment the payee account is credited. Ten seconds is not a target, it is a maximum, and it includes the time spent on screening, fraud checks, and ledger posting. A payment that is still sitting in a queue after 10 seconds has breached the regulation. The 10-second budget changes how a payment hub is built. Screening, which used to run as a batch or as a slow synchronous call, must now complete inside the budget or be redesigned. Many firms moved sanctions screening of their own customer base to a daily batch, which the regulation expressly permits, so that the per-transaction check only covers the counterparty and the payment details. Fraud scoring must return a decision in well under a second, which means the scoring model has to run in memory with precomputed features, not as a database lookup at payment time. The ledger posting must be atomic with the payment execution, so that a payment cannot be marked as sent while the ledger still shows the money in the account. A hub that was built for batch processing cannot meet the 10-second rule by tuning alone, it needs a redesign of the hot path. The receiving obligation is the part of the regulation that catches firms out. Every payment service provider in the euro area must be able to receive instant payments, with the obligation applying from 9 January 2025, and every provider must be able to send them from 9 October 2025, with a later date for providers outside the euro area. A bank that can send instant payments but cannot receive them is in breach, and the gap is usually in the core banking system, which was built to receive batch files and cannot process a single instant credit at any hour. Receiving also means 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, including public holidays, so the operations rota and the incident response must cover nights and weekends. Verification of payee, known as VoP, becomes mandatory from 9 October 2025, and it is the most important fraud control in the regulation. Before a payer sends an instant payment, the payer bank checks the payee name against the payee IBAN. The result is a match, a close match, or no match. On a close match the payer is shown the name the bank holds and asked to confirm. On no match the payer is warned and must actively confirm they want to proceed. The liability follows the warning: if the payer proceeds after a no-match warning, the payer bears the loss; if the bank failed to perform the check, the bank bears it. For a business analyst the requirements are precise: the VoP check must run before the payment is released, the result must be shown to the payer in plain language, the payer decision must be logged, and the log must be retrievable when a fraud claim arrives. Charges are regulated too. An instant payment must not cost the payer more than a regular SEPA credit transfer, and the regulation requires the price to be the same or lower. This removes the premium that some banks charged for instant payments and makes the service a standard feature rather than a paid extra. The pricing change has a commercial consequence: banks can no longer recover the cost of instant infrastructure from a per-payment fee, so the business case must rest on customer retention and on the volume that moves from cards and cash to instant payments. Failure modes are where the regulation meets real operations. When a payment times out at 10 seconds, the payer bank must determine the status: was the payment executed, rejected, or is it unknown? If the status is unknown, the bank must investigate and must not simply retry, because a retry can double-pay. If the payment was executed late, the bank must still credit the payee and must tell the payer what happened. If the payee bank is unreachable, the payment must be returned with a reason code and the payer must be told. The SCT Inst scheme provides the reason codes, and the operations team needs a procedure for each one, because the 10-second clock does not stop while the team investigates. The 2025 SCT Inst rulebook also removed the 100,000 euro maximum amount for instant payments, which means a high-value payment can now travel as an instant payment, with the same 10-second execution rule and the same verification of payee duty. That changes the risk profile, because a fraudulent instant payment of 500,000 euros is gone in 10 seconds and cannot be recalled through the scheme. The controls that matter are therefore the ones that run before release: the verification of payee check, the fraud score, the per-customer limits, and the dual authorisation for high values. For a business analyst, the acceptance criteria must include a test where an instant payment completes inside 10 seconds, a test where a verification of payee mismatch warns and logs, a test where a timeout is resolved without a duplicate, and a test where a high-value payment requires a second authoriser. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: Regulation (EU) 2024/886; Regulation (EU) 260/2012 as amended; the European Payments Council SCT Inst rulebook 2025; the European Banking Authority guidelines on verification of payee.
