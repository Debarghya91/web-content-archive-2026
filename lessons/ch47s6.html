  • Management keeps every partner informed
  • Emergency: allowed
  • Team monitors the service closely

Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Scheme: A card or payment network that sets the rules, standards and fees for transactions between issuers and acquirers.

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Onboarding starts with a discovery workshop, where the bank and the partner agree on the business need, the volumes, and the technical approach. A bank onboarding a new corporate customer that will send 30,000 payments a month needs to know the file format, the cutoff times, the settlement account, and the support contacts. The workshop produces a short onboarding document that names the systems, the interfaces, the test plan, and the go-live date. Skipping this step is a common mistake, because the two sides discover their misunderstandings only during testing, when fixing them is expensive and the date is already fixed. Certification is the formal proof that the integration works, and it is usually required by schemes and by large partners. The partner provides a test environment and a set of test cases, such as a valid payment of USD 1,000, a payment with an invalid account, a payment above the limit, and a duplicate payment. The bank must pass every case and provide evidence, such as message logs and screenshots, before it is allowed into production. Certification can take several weeks, because each failure requires a fix and a retest. A business analyst should plan for at least two rounds of testing and should never promise a go-live date before the first round is complete. Test environments must mirror production closely, or certification proves nothing. The test core banking system should behave like the live one, the test scheme simulator should return realistic responses, and the test data should include edge cases such as leap years, public holidays, and foreign characters in names. A bank that tests only with clean data will be surprised by the first real payment with a special character in the beneficiary name. Test data must also be refreshed regularly, because a stale environment hides the changes that have been made to production since the last refresh. Change management keeps every partner informed when something changes, because a change on one side can break the other side without warning. If a bank changes the format of its payment file, every corporate customer that sends that file must be told, given a test window, and given a date for the change. If a scheme changes its message version from one release to another, every participant must upgrade by a deadline. A change calendar lists all planned changes, their owners, and their impact, and a change advisory board reviews the high-risk ones. Emergency changes are allowed, but they need a named approver and a rollback plan, because an untested emergency fix can cause more damage than the problem it solves. Go-live is a controlled event, not a single moment, and it needs a runbook that everyone can follow. The runbook lists the deployment steps, the verification checks, the rollback trigger, and the contact numbers for every team involved. The first live payments are usually limited in volume and value, such as ten payments of GBP 100, so that any problem affects a small number of customers. The team monitors the service closely for the first few hours, and the rollback decision is made by a named incident manager, not by a committee. A successful go-live is often quiet, because the preparation was thorough, and the team celebrates the lack of drama rather than the heroics of a recovery.
