What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain transaction-level matching: references, amounts, currencies and value dates. Chapter objective: Learner performs payment reconciliation, matching bank statements to internal records, identifying breaks, and resolving differences with clear actions and evidence. Learner applies this skill in realistic banking scenarios, with clear examples and correct terminology throughout.

  • Payment reference

  • Value date

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Exercise

Investigate the reconciliation break described in this lesson and find its root cause.

Use the three-way matching rule and the break categories in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Compare the instruction, the settlement record and the statement, name the break type, and state the corrective action.

Correct result. A break is found by matching amount, currency, date and reference across the three sources. The root cause is a timing difference, an amount difference, a missing entry, or a duplicate, and the corrective action follows the cause.

Why. Reconciliation is the last line of defence. A break that is not investigated is money that cannot be proved.

The payment reference is the primary key that links an internal record to an external statement line, and it must survive the whole payment journey unchanged. A bank typically assigns an internal reference such as PAY2026110500147 and also carries the customer reference, for example INV-8841, and the end-to-end reference that travels in the pacs.008 message. If any system truncates the reference, adds a prefix, or drops a character, the reconciliation engine cannot find the match, even though the payment itself succeeded. For example, a reference of sixteen characters sent through a system that allows only fifteen will lose its final digit, and the statement line will sit unmatched until an analyst manually links it by amount and date. The amount is the field most people check first, and it is also the field most often changed by fees, charges, and foreign exchange. A customer may instruct a payment of GBP 80,000 with charges to be borne by the sender, known as OUR, and expect the beneficiary to receive the full GBP 80,000. If an intermediary bank deducts GBP 25 in transit, the beneficiary receives GBP 79,975 and the statement shows a debit of GBP 80,000 but a credit of GBP 79,975 at the far end. The reconciliation engine must know the charging arrangement and match on the expected net amount, posting the GBP 25 difference to a charges account. Without that tolerance rule, every payment with an intermediary fee would appear as a break. Currency matching seems obvious, yet it causes real breaks whenever a payment is converted or reported in a different currency from the one instructed. A customer may instruct a payment of USD 100,000 to a beneficiary in Tokyo, and the beneficiary bank may convert it to JPY 15,200,000 before crediting the beneficiary account. The nostro statement will show a USD debit, while the beneficiary confirmation may show a JPY credit, and the two cannot be matched by amount alone. The reconciliation engine must therefore match on the original currency and amount, and use the exchange rate applied on the value date to explain the converted figure, rather than treating the JPY amount as a different payment. The value date is the date on which the money is considered to have moved for interest and accounting purposes, and it is frequently different from the processing date. A payment instructed on Friday 6 November 2026 for value Monday 9 November 2026 will appear in the bank's ledger on Friday but on the correspondent statement on Monday, creating a timing break over the weekend. That break is expected and should clear automatically on Monday, but the reconciliation system must be configured to recognise value date differences as timing rather than as errors. If it is not, the operations team will waste hours each Monday investigating breaks that were never real. Good matching rules combine all four fields into a hierarchy, trying an exact match first and then falling back to looser rules only when the exact match fails. A typical hierarchy starts with reference plus amount plus currency plus value date, then tries reference plus amount, then amount plus value date plus beneficiary account, and finally amount alone within a narrow window. Each looser rule carries a higher risk of a false match, so the engine should require human approval before it matches two items on amount alone. Analysts who understand that hierarchy can explain why a payment matched automatically, why another needed manual matching, and why a third could not be matched at all.
