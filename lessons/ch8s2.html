Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Charge bearer: A code that states who pays the charges for a payment. In ISO 20022 the values are DEBT for debtor, CRED for creditor, SHAR for shared and SLEV for service level.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Show transcript

Translation between MT and MX is governed by mapping rules defined by SWIFT and by the CBPR Plus usage guidelines. The rules specify, for each MT field, which MX element or elements it maps to, and for each MX element, which MT field or fields it maps from. Some mappings are straightforward, for example field 32A in an MT103, which contains value date, currency, and amount, maps to the Interbank Settlement Date, the Instructed Amount, and the currency in a pacs.008. Other mappings are more complex, because the data models are different. An MT field that contains free text may need to be split across several MX elements, or several MT fields may need to be combined into a single MX element.

Translation is not just a technical mapping, it is a business decision about what data is preserved and what data is lost. When an MX message with a full structured address is translated to MT, the address must be truncated to fit the 35 character lines of MT, and some detail will be lost. When an MT message with a 140 character remittance field is translated to MX, the remittance information can be carried as unstructured text, but it will not have the structured invoice details that a native MX message would have. The translation rules therefore include rules for truncation, for abbreviation, and for what to do when data cannot be mapped. Those rules must be documented and tested, because they determine what the beneficiary will actually see.

Analysts must also understand the difference between translation and transformation. Translation is the mapping of one format to another, while transformation is the change in business meaning that can occur during translation. For example, translating a charge bearer code from MT to MX should preserve the business meaning, but if the code lists are different, the translation may need to use a different code that has a slightly different meaning. That is a transformation risk, and it must be identified and mitigated. Your requirements should list every field that is translated, state the mapping rule, and state the expected behaviour when data cannot be mapped exactly.

Walk through the main mappings one by one. An MT103 becomes a pacs.008. Field 20, the transaction reference, maps to the instruction identification. Field 32A splits into the interbank settlement date, the instructed amount, and the currency. Field 50K becomes the debtor, field 59 becomes the creditor, field 70 becomes the remittance information, and field 71A becomes the charge bearer. An MT202 becomes a pacs.009, with field 20 mapping to the instruction identification and field 32A mapping to the settlement date and amount.

Translation in the other direction was harder, because MX carries more data than MT can hold. A pacs.008 with a full structured address had to be squeezed into four lines of 35 characters in MT field 50K or 59. Structured remittance information with several invoices had to be squeezed into 140 characters in MT field 70. Purpose codes, structured charges, and the UETR had no natural home in MT, so they were dropped or carried in free text fields where the receiving bank might not look for them. Every one of those squeezes was a place where data could be lost, and the translation rules had to say exactly what to drop and what to keep.

Validation after translation was a separate step that teams often underestimated. A translated message had to pass the validation rules of the target format, not just the source format. A pacs.008 produced by translating an MT103 had to pass CBPR Plus validation, and if it failed, the sending bank received a pacs.002 status report with a reject reason code. An MT produced by translating a pacs.008 had to pass SWIFT network validation, and if it failed, the network returned a negative acknowledgement.

For a business analyst, the translation deliverables were concrete. The mapping document listed every field, the mapping rule, the mapping type, and the behaviour when data could not be mapped exactly. The test plan covered each mapping with realistic data, including long names, long addresses, and long remittance blocks. Operations and compliance signed off on the truncation behaviour before go live, because they owned the consequences. Those documents are still useful today, because they explain why a historical payment looks the way it does, and because the same discipline applies to any future migration, such as a domestic scheme moving to ISO 20022.
