Key terms

Indirect participant: A bank or institution that uses a payment system through a direct participant, without holding its own settlement account.

Direct participant: A bank or institution that holds an account in a payment system and sends and receives payments directly.

Irrevocable: A payment or instruction that cannot be cancelled or reversed once it has been made, except by agreement of all parties or by a court order.

Show transcript

Faster Payments, usually called FPS, is the UK instant payment system. It was launched in 2008, and it now carries the majority of UK electronic retail payments by volume. A single immediate payment is credited to the beneficiary within 15 seconds of the payer confirming it, and in practice it usually arrives in under five seconds. Standing orders and forward-dated payments also travel by FPS. The service runs 24 hours a day, seven days a week, including weekends and bank holidays, which is the fundamental difference from BACS and CHAPS. A payment sent at 23:00 on a Sunday arrives at 23:00 on a Sunday.

The scheme limit for a single Faster Payment is 1 million pounds, raised from 250,000 pounds in 2022. That is the scheme maximum, not the limit a customer will actually get. Each bank sets its own per-transaction and daily limits, typically between 10,000 and 100,000 pounds for retail customers, and higher for corporate customers who have been through additional controls. A customer who needs to move 500,000 pounds in one payment will usually find their bank's limit is lower, and the payment must be split, or sent by CHAPS. The limit is a risk control as much as a technical one: instant and irrevocable is a dangerous combination for a fraudster, so banks cap the exposure.

Access to FPS comes in two forms. Direct participants are members of the scheme, hold settlement accounts, and send payments directly into the central infrastructure. There are around 40 direct participants. Indirect access is how most smaller banks, building societies and payment institutions connect: they use an aggregator or a sponsor bank that is a direct participant, and the sponsor settles on their behalf. The indirect participant's customers see the same 15-second service, but the sponsor bears the settlement risk and the technical burden. A firm choosing between direct and indirect access is choosing between control and cost, and most new entrants start indirect.

Settlement in FPS is not real-time gross settlement. Payments are exchanged continuously through the day, but the net settlement between participants happens at the Bank of England three times a day, through the FPS settlement cycles. Each participant prefunds its settlement account, and the prefunding must cover its net debit position. If a participant's outgoing payments exceed its prefunding, its payments queue. The Bank of England provides liquidity to the settlement account, and the scheme monitors prefunding levels continuously. This is why a bank can have a technical outage in FPS even when the central infrastructure is healthy: its own prefunding ran out.

The payment types matter for the requirements. A single immediate payment is the basic case: the payer confirms, the payment is sent, the beneficiary is credited within 15 seconds. A standing order is a recurring Faster Payment, set up once and paid automatically on the due date. A forward-dated payment is a single payment scheduled for a future date. Each type has its own failure mode. A standing order fails when the payer's balance is insufficient on the due date, and FPS does not retry it automatically, so the payer must set it up again or make a manual payment. A forward-dated payment can be cancelled before the due date, but not after it is sent.

What goes wrong in FPS is usually a limit, a sort code, or a fraud hold. A payment that exceeds the bank's limit is rejected with a clear error, and the customer must split it or use CHAPS. A payment to a sort code that is not reachable by FPS is rejected, and the customer must use BACS or CHAPS instead. A payment that trips the fraud engine is held for review, and the 15-second promise does not apply, because the bank is allowed to delay a payment it suspects is fraudulent. The most painful failure is a payment sent to the wrong account: it arrives in seconds, it is irrevocable, and recovery depends on the receiving bank and the beneficiary. This is why Confirmation of Payee exists.

For a business analyst, Faster Payments requirements are about speed, limits and irrevocability. The acceptance criteria must state the 15-second target, the bank's own transaction limit, and the daily limit. A test must prove that a payment sent at 02:00 on a Sunday is credited within 15 seconds. The requirements must cover the three payment types and their failure modes, including what happens when a standing order fails for insufficient funds. The design must handle the prefunding position, and the operations team must have a procedure for a payment that is held by the fraud engine, including how long it can be held and who can release it. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
