Why it matters. An investigation begins when a customer reports that a payment has not arrived, or when reconciliation shows a payment with no matching settlement, and the analyst must reconstruct the payment journey from the available evidence.

Chapter objective: Learner investigates payment exceptions, including non-receipt, wrong amount, duplicate, and delayed payments, and runs a structured investigation from first report to resolution. Learner demonstrates this skill using realistic banking examples, explaining each step clearly and using correct terminology throughout.

Key learning points

  • Investigation beginning with a customer report
  • Investigation finding the hold and releasing the payment
  • Timeout: no acknowledgement in expected time
  • Status check
  • Pending list growing silently without daily review
  • Settlement reference

Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

An investigation begins when a customer reports that a payment has not arrived, or when reconciliation shows a payment with no matching settlement, and the analyst must reconstruct the payment journey from the available evidence. The analyst starts with the internal payment record, checks the pacs.008 that was sent, looks for a pacs.002 acknowledgement, checks the nostro statement for the debit, and contacts the correspondent or beneficiary bank if the trail stops. For example, a USD 120,000 payment to a supplier in Hong Kong that has not arrived after three days may have been held by an intermediary bank for additional information. The investigation must find that hold, obtain the missing information, and get the payment released. Duplicate payments are investigated differently, because the question is not where the money went but how to get the second payment back. The analyst must first prove that a duplicate exists, by showing two settlement debits for one customer instruction, with the same amount, currency, beneficiary, and value date. Once the duplicate is proven, the bank contacts the beneficiary bank, explains the error, and requests a return of the duplicate amount through a pacs.004 message. If the beneficiary has already spent the money, recovery becomes a commercial negotiation rather than a technical return, and the bank may need to involve its legal team. Prevention matters more than cure, which is why duplicate detection checks run before a payment is sent. A timeout happens when a system sends a payment and does not receive an acknowledgement within the expected time, so the sender does not know whether the payment succeeded. This is common when a clearing system is slow, when a network link fails, or when a correspondent bank's system is temporarily unavailable. The worst response to a timeout is to resend the payment immediately, because the first attempt may have succeeded and the resend would create a duplicate. The correct response is to check the payment status through the clearing system or the correspondent bank, confirm whether the original payment was processed, and only then decide whether to resend. That status check is a formal step, not an informal guess. A pending payment is one that has been accepted for processing but has not yet reached a final state of settled or rejected, and it can remain pending for hours or even days. A payment may be pending because it is waiting for a future value date, because it is held for sanctions screening, because it is waiting for funds in the customer account, or because the beneficiary bank has asked for more information. The analyst must know why each pending payment is pending, how long it has been pending, and what action will move it forward. A pending list that is not reviewed daily will grow silently, and customers will discover the delay before the bank does. The closing discipline for all four types is the same. Every investigation must end with a written conclusion, a customer notification, and a ledger entry that proves the outcome. An investigation that ends with a phone call and no record is not closed, because the next analyst who looks at the payment will have to start again. A duplicate that is recovered must show the return credit on the customer account. A timeout that is resolved must show whether the original payment succeeded or was safely resent. A pending payment that settles must show the settlement reference. That standard turns investigations from open-ended worry into accountable work.
