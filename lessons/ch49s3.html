What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain parties involved and their roles. The applicant is the customer who asks the issuing bank to create the standby.

Why it matters. The applicant is the customer who asks the issuing bank to create the standby.

Chapter objective: Learner explains what a standby letter of credit is, distinguishes it from a commercial letter of credit, and describes issuance, notification, drawing and expiry. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Applicant, customer
  • Applicant and issuance fee
  • Act in good faith
  • Late presentation or a missing document
  • Own independent undertaking
  • Confirmation and one bank promise

Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

MT700: A SWIFT message a bank sends to issue a documentary credit, stating the terms under which it will pay the beneficiary on presentation of complying documents.

Exercise

HSBC in Hong Kong issues a standby for 750,000 dollars at the request of a customer, in favour of a beneficiary. A landlord in Singapore is the beneficiary of a 100,000 dollar standby from a tenant, so unpaid rent can be recovered by a demand. Name each party and state who the beneficiary looks to for payment.

Use the party roles in this lesson: applicant, beneficiary, issuing bank, advising bank and confirming bank.

  1. What to do. List the applicant, the beneficiary and the issuing bank in each example, and state who pays when a complying demand is presented.

Correct result. The applicant is the customer who asks the issuing bank to create the standby. The beneficiary is the party in whose favour it is issued and who can draw it. The issuing bank, HSBC in the first example, is the bank the beneficiary looks to for payment. A confirming bank, if one is added, adds its own payment undertaking alongside the issuing bank.

Why. Every standby workflow names each party and the message that connects them. An analyst who mixes up applicant and beneficiary will route a drawing to the wrong party.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

The applicant is the customer who asks the issuing bank to create the standby. The applicant is usually the buyer, the contractor, the borrower, or the party whose performance is being guaranteed. A textile exporter in Dhaka may be the applicant for a standby that guarantees it will repay a 400,000 dollar advance from a buyer in New York. The applicant signs an application and a reimbursement agreement, promising to repay the bank immediately if the bank pays under the standby. The applicant also pays the issuance fee and provides any collateral the bank requires. The issuing bank is the bank that creates the standby and gives the payment undertaking to the beneficiary. It is the bank whose credit the beneficiary relies on. If HSBC in Hong Kong issues a standby for 750,000 dollars, the beneficiary looks to HSBC for payment, not to the applicant. The issuing bank must examine any documents presented under the standby, decide whether they comply, and pay or refuse within the time allowed by the rules. It must act in good faith and with reasonable care, but it does not investigate the underlying dispute. The beneficiary is the party in whose favour the standby is issued, and the only party who can draw on it. The beneficiary may be a seller, a project owner, a landlord, a lender, or a government authority. A landlord in Singapore may be the beneficiary of a 100,000 dollar standby from a tenant, so that unpaid rent can be recovered by a demand. The beneficiary must present the documents exactly as the standby requires, before the expiry date and at the place specified for presentation. A late presentation or a missing document can lose the right to be paid. The advising bank is the bank, usually in the beneficiary country, that delivers the standby to the beneficiary on behalf of the issuing bank. It authenticates the message, checks that it appears genuine, and forwards it without adding its own payment undertaking. A beneficiary in Germany may receive a standby issued by a bank in India through Deutsche Bank as advising bank. The advising bank is not obliged to pay, and its role is limited to transmission and authentication, unless it also agrees to confirm the standby. A confirming bank adds its own independent undertaking to pay, on top of the issuing bank undertaking. Confirmation is common when the beneficiary doubts the credit standing of the issuing bank or the country risk of the issuing bank location. A supplier in Brazil may ask for confirmation by Citibank in New York on a standby issued by a smaller bank in Turkey, for 600,000 dollars. The confirming bank charges a fee for taking that risk, and the beneficiary can then present documents to the confirming bank and be paid locally. Confirmation turns one bank promise into two.

The applicant is the party that asks the issuing bank to issue the standby letter of credit, and on whose behalf the standby is issued. The applicant is normally the party that owes the obligation, for example the contractor who must complete the work, the tenant who must pay the rent, or the borrower who must repay the loan. The applicant applies to the bank, provides collateral or a credit facility, pays the issuance fee, and bears the risk that the beneficiary will draw the standby. The applicant is the party whose credit the bank relies on, and the bank will assess the applicant creditworthiness before issuing the standby.

The beneficiary is the party in whose favour the standby is issued, and the party that can draw it. The beneficiary is normally the party to whom the obligation is owed, for example the project owner, the landlord, or the lender. The beneficiary holds the standby as security, and it draws on it by presenting a demand to the bank, stating that the applicant has defaulted. The beneficiary must present the demand before the expiry date, and must present the documents the standby requires. The beneficiary is the party the standby protects, and it is the party that decides whether to draw.

The issuing bank is the bank that issues the standby, at the request of the applicant, in favour of the beneficiary. The issuing bank gives the payment undertaking, and it pays the beneficiary on presentation of a complying demand. The issuing bank is liable to the beneficiary, independently of the applicant, and it must pay even if the applicant disputes the default. The issuing bank charges the applicant an issuance fee, and it holds collateral or a credit facility from the applicant as security for the reimbursement. If the issuing bank pays the beneficiary, it will debit the applicant account or call on the collateral to reimburse itself.

The advising bank is the bank that advises the standby to the beneficiary, on behalf of the issuing bank. The advising bank authenticates the standby, confirms that it is genuine, and delivers it to the beneficiary. The advising bank does not give a payment undertaking, unless it also confirms the standby. Its role is to transmit and to authenticate. The advising bank is normally a bank in the beneficiary country, and it is chosen by the issuing bank or by the beneficiary. The advising bank charges a fee for advising, and it is not liable to pay the beneficiary unless it confirms the standby.

The confirming bank is a bank that adds its own payment undertaking to the standby, in addition to the issuing bank undertaking. A beneficiary asks for confirmation when it does not trust the issuing bank, or when the issuing bank is in a country with political or transfer risk. The confirming bank examines the demand and pays the beneficiary, and then claims reimbursement from the issuing bank. Confirmation costs a fee, and it is not automatic. The issuing bank must authorise the confirmation, or the confirming bank must agree to confirm without authorisation, which is called silent confirmation. A standby that is confirmed gives the beneficiary two banks to rely on, and it is safer than a standby that is only issued.

The reimbursement flow is as follows. The beneficiary presents a complying demand to the issuing bank, or to the confirming bank if the standby is confirmed. The bank examines the demand, and if it complies, it pays the beneficiary. The issuing bank then debits the applicant account, or calls on the collateral, to reimburse itself. If the applicant account has insufficient funds, the bank enforces the collateral, which may be a cash deposit, a lien on assets, or a guarantee from the applicant parent company. The applicant bears the ultimate cost, and the bank is only an intermediary that pays on the strength of the documents.

For a business analyst, the parties requirement must name each party, each bank, and the message that connects them. The requirement should state that the applicant applies to the issuing bank, that the issuing bank issues the standby by MT700, that the advising bank advises it to the beneficiary, that the beneficiary presents a demand to the issuing bank or the confirming bank, and that the issuing bank reimburses itself from the applicant account or the collateral. A system that cannot distinguish the applicant from the beneficiary, or the issuing bank from the advising bank, cannot process a standby correctly.
