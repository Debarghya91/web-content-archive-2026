Key terms

Exercise

HSBC in Hong Kong issues a standby for 750,000 dollars at the request of a customer, in favour of a beneficiary. A landlord in Singapore is the beneficiary of a 100,000 dollar standby from a tenant, so unpaid rent can be recovered by a demand. Name each party and state who the beneficiary looks to for payment.

Use the party roles in this lesson: applicant, beneficiary, issuing bank, advising bank and confirming bank.

  1. What to do. List the applicant, the beneficiary and the issuing bank in each example, and state who pays when a complying demand is presented.

Correct result. The applicant is the customer who asks the issuing bank to create the standby. The beneficiary is the party in whose favour it is issued and who can draw it. The issuing bank, HSBC in the first example, is the bank the beneficiary looks to for payment. A confirming bank, if one is added, adds its own payment undertaking alongside the issuing bank.

Why. Every standby workflow names each party and the message that connects them. An analyst who mixes up applicant and beneficiary will route a drawing to the wrong party.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

The applicant is the customer who asks the issuing bank to create the standby. The applicant is usually the buyer, the contractor, the borrower, or the party whose performance is being guaranteed. A textile exporter in Dhaka may be the applicant for a standby that guarantees it will repay a 400,000 dollar advance from a buyer in New York. The applicant signs an application and a reimbursement agreement, promising to repay the bank immediately if the bank pays under the standby. The applicant also pays the issuance fee and provides any collateral the bank requires.

The issuing bank is the bank that creates the standby and gives the payment undertaking to the beneficiary. It is the bank whose credit the beneficiary relies on. If HSBC in Hong Kong issues a standby for 750,000 dollars, the beneficiary looks to HSBC for payment, not to the applicant. The issuing bank must examine any documents presented under the standby, decide whether they comply, and pay or refuse within the time allowed by the rules. It must act in good faith and with reasonable care, but it does not investigate the underlying dispute.

The beneficiary is the party in whose favour the standby is issued, and the only party who can draw on it. The beneficiary may be a seller, a project owner, a landlord, a lender, or a government authority. A landlord in Singapore may be the beneficiary of a 100,000 dollar standby from a tenant, so that unpaid rent can be recovered by a demand. The beneficiary must present the documents exactly as the standby requires, before the expiry date and at the place specified for presentation. A late presentation or a missing document can lose the right to be paid.

The advising bank is the bank, usually in the beneficiary country, that delivers the standby to the beneficiary on behalf of the issuing bank. It authenticates the message, checks that it appears genuine, and forwards it without adding its own payment undertaking. A beneficiary in Germany may receive a standby issued by a bank in India through Deutsche Bank as advising bank. The advising bank is not obliged to pay, and its role is limited to transmission and authentication, unless it also agrees to confirm the standby.

A confirming bank adds its own independent undertaking to pay, on top of the issuing bank undertaking. Confirmation is common when the beneficiary doubts the credit standing of the issuing bank or the country risk of the issuing bank location. A supplier in Brazil may ask for confirmation by Citibank in New York on a standby issued by a smaller bank in Turkey, for 600,000 dollars. The confirming bank charges a fee for taking that risk, and the beneficiary can then present documents to the confirming bank and be paid locally.
