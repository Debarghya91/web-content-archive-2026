Key terms

Cover payment: A payment method where the customer instruction travels one route while the funds travel separately as a bank to bank cover message.

Show transcript

The Unique End to end Transaction Reference, known as the UETR, is the identifier that makes gpi tracking possible, and every analyst who works on cross border payments must understand it. The UETR is a thirty six character string in the format of a universally unique identifier, for example a string of letters and numbers separated by hyphens, and it is generated by the sending bank when the payment is created. The UETR stays with the payment through every bank in the chain, through every conversion, and through every return or recall, so that the payment can be identified at any point by a single reference.

Unlike the sender's own reference, which can change as the payment passes through different systems, the UETR is never changed, and every bank must carry it forward. The UETR is carried in the SWIFT message, in the ISO 20022 pacs.008 message, and in the gpi tracker, so that all three views of the payment can be linked together. When a customer asks where their payment is, the operations team searches the tracker by UETR and sees the full journey. When a bank needs to investigate a payment, it quotes the UETR, and every other bank can find the same payment in its own system.

When a payment is returned, the return message carries the same UETR, so that the return can be matched to the original payment without ambiguity. The UETR is also used in the cover payment, where the pacs.009 COV carries the UETR of the customer payment it covers, so that the funds leg and the instruction leg can be matched. A business analyst must ensure that the system generates a UETR for every cross border payment, stores it, shows it to the customer, and includes it in every message and every report.

The UETR is a simple idea with a large impact, because it solves the oldest problem in cross border payments. The payment changes its identity as it moves. Before the UETR, a payment could have one reference at the sending bank, a different reference at the intermediary bank, and a third reference at the receiving bank, and tracing it meant matching amounts and dates by hand. With the UETR, the payment has one identity from start to finish, and tracing it is a lookup, not a detective exercise. The analyst should treat the UETR as a first class field in the data model, not as an optional extra, and should test that it survives every transformation in the payment flow, including currency conversion, charge deduction, and repair.
