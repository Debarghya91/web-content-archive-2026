What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain audit trails and record keeping and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. The audit trail is the evidence that every other control worked, and it must survive for years.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the control framework that keeps payments safe, distinguishes fraud typologies, transaction monitoring, SAR and STR filing, KYC and CDD, access controls, audit trails and scheme rulebooks, and applies each control to realistic payment operations with correct terminology.

Key terms

Chargeback: A dispute raised by a cardholder through the issuer to reverse a card payment, for example for fraud, non delivery or a duplicate charge.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

An audit trail is the record that proves what happened, and in payments it must prove it to a standard that survives a court, a regulator, and an internal investigation. Every state change in a payment must be recorded: when it was created, when it was screened, when it was approved, when it was released, when it was settled, and when its status changed. Each record must say who made the change, when they made it, what the value was before, and what it is after. A record that says a payment was approved, without saying who approved it and what they approved, is not an audit trail, it is a note. Retention periods are set by law and by scheme, and they are longer than most people expect. Anti-money-laundering law in the European Union requires transaction records to be kept for five years after the end of the business relationship, extendable by a further five years. The United States Bank Secrecy Act requires records to be kept for five years. Tax law has its own periods. Scheme rules add their own: card scheme records, including chargeback evidence, must be kept for the scheme retention period, which can run beyond the legal minimum. The retention schedule must list every record type, its retention period, and its legal basis, and the system must enforce it. A record that is deleted early is a breach, and a record that is kept forever is a data protection breach, so the schedule must be exact. Tamper evidence is what makes an audit trail trustworthy. The trail must be append-only, so that a record once written cannot be changed or deleted without leaving evidence. The practical methods are hashing, where each record contains a hash of the previous record so that any change breaks the chain, and write-once storage, where the storage medium itself prevents modification. Time synchronisation matters too: every system in the payment chain must use the same time source, synchronised by network time protocol, because an audit trail with clocks that disagree by minutes cannot reconstruct the order of events. A payment that was approved at 10:00 in one system and released at 09:58 in another is either a clock problem or a fraud, and the investigation must be able to tell which. Reconstruction is the test of an audit trail. An auditor or an investigator must be able to take a payment reference and rebuild its entire life: the instruction, the authentication, the screening decision and the score, the approval and the approver, the release, the message sent, the settlement, and every status change in between. The reconstruction must be possible from the trail alone, without asking the operations team to remember what happened. A trail that records the payment but not the screening decision cannot prove the payment was screened. A trail that records the approval but not the limit in force at the time cannot prove the approval was within authority. The gaps are found in investigations, and by then it is too late to fill them. System logs and audit trails are different things, and confusing them is a common design error. System logs record what the software did: the database queries, the network calls, the errors. Audit trails record what the business did: who approved what, who released what, who changed a limit. The audit trail must be written in business terms, with the payment reference, the customer, and the decision, so that a non-technical investigator can follow it. The system log is for the engineer. The audit trail is for the auditor, the regulator, and the court. Both must exist, and neither can substitute for the other. The failure modes are predictable. Clock skew, where two systems disagree by minutes and the order of events cannot be established. Missing events, where a state change was made through a back-door procedure that bypassed the audit trail. Truncated logs, where the retention period expired and the evidence is gone. A real investigation scenario shows the cost: a customer claims a payment was made without their authority, the bank must prove the authentication, the approval and the release, and the trail is missing the authentication record because it was held in a separate system that was decommissioned. The bank cannot prove its case, and it pays the claim. For a business analyst, the audit trail work is a set of event definitions. Every business event must be defined: what it is, when it fires, what fields it records, and how long it is kept. The definitions must cover the whole payment lifecycle, including the exceptions, because an exception that is not recorded is an exception that cannot be investigated. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment is reconstructed from the trail alone, a test where a tampered record is detected by the hash chain, a test where a record is retrieved after the retention period has started, and a test where two systems with synchronised clocks agree on the order of events. The evidence must be kept, because the audit trail is the control that proves all the other controls worked. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
