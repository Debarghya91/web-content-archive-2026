Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Show transcript

In a prefunded settlement model, participants deposit funds in advance into a settlement account, and payments are settled only to the extent that prefunded balance allows. A participant cannot send more than it has prefunded, which means settlement is guaranteed as long as the prefund is in place. This model is used by many fast payment systems, where the promise to the customer is immediate availability of funds, and by some card and mobile money systems where the settlement risk must be kept to zero.

Prefunding works well when speed and certainty matter more than liquidity efficiency. In a fast payment system, the receiving customer expects to see the money in seconds, not hours, and the receiving bank credits the customer immediately. That credit is only safe if the sending bank has already funded it. Prefunding provides that safety. The cost is that funds sit idle in the settlement account, earning little or no return, and participants must forecast how much they will need. If they prefund too little, payments are rejected or delayed. If they prefund too much, capital is tied up unnecessarily.

Many systems combine prefunding with other features to reduce the cost. Participants can top up their prefund during the day, and some systems allow a prefund to be used across multiple payment types. Some central banks pay interest on prefunded balances, or allow the prefund to count towards reserve requirements, which softens the cost. A business analyst designing a prefunded system needs to model the prefund sizing problem carefully, because getting it wrong directly affects customer experience, either through rejected payments or through wasted liquidity.

Follow the prefund through a day. Bank A starts at 8 in the morning with EUR 20 million prefunded in its fast payment settlement account. By 1 in the afternoon it has sent EUR 17.5 million and received EUR 6 million, so its balance is EUR 8.5 million. At one twenty in the afternoon a corporate customer sends a EUR 9 million payment, which is more than the balance, so the payment cannot settle from the prefund. The system either rejects it or holds it while treasury tops the prefund up by EUR 10 million, and the payment goes through minutes later. Now take the forecasting failure. Bank A sizes its prefund on an average day, EUR 12 million, but today is month end, when salary payments push its outflows to EUR 19 million by midday. The prefund runs out at eleven forty in the morning, and 400 customer payments are rejected before treasury notices and tops up. The customers see failed payments, the bank sees complaints, and the cause is not a system fault but a prefund sized for the wrong day.

Sizing the prefund is the analyst's core modelling task for this model. The model should take the peak day outflow, not the average, add a buffer for unexpected large payments, and subtract the expected inflows for the same period. The requirements should state the auto top up rules, for example topping up automatically when the balance falls below 20 percent, and the alert thresholds, for example an amber alert at 80 percent usage and a red alert at 95 percent. The operations runbook should name who can approve an emergency top up and how fast it can be done. And the cost of the prefund should be stated honestly in the business case. Funds sitting in the settlement account earn little or no return, so every extra million of buffer has a price.
