Transcript and full text

Show transcript

The MOD-97 check is the arithmetic test that catches most typing errors in an IBAN before a payment is sent, and every analyst who works on European payments must be able to work it by hand. An IBAN carries two check digits near its start, calculated so the whole IBAN passes one division test, which means a mistyped character usually breaks the test and exposes the error at entry. Valid check digits always fall in the range 02 to 98, so a pair outside that range fails at once and never needs the full division. Catching a bad IBAN at the payment screen is far cheaper than handling a returned payment days later.

The worked example uses the IBAN GB29 NWBK 6016 1331 9268 19, a valid IBAN used in the ISO 13616 standard as its example, and no other IBAN is needed to learn the check. The first two letters, GB, are the country code, the next two digits, 29, are the check digits, and the remaining characters identify the bank and the account within the country. The spaces exist only to help the eye, so validation starts by removing the spaces and working with the string GB29NWBK60161331926819, written without spaces and in upper case.

Step one and step two rearrange the IBAN and turn its letters into numbers, so the whole string becomes one long number that can be divided. Step one moves the first four characters to the end, so GB29NWBK60161331926819 becomes NWBK60161331926819GB29, with the country code and check digits now at the end. Step two converts every letter to a number, with A equal to 10, B equal to 11, and the values rising by one through the alphabet until Z equals 35, so N becomes 23, W becomes 32, B becomes 11, K becomes 20, and G becomes 16. Reading the converted string in groups gives 23 32 11 20 6016 1331 9268 19 16 11 29, and joining those groups gives the long number 2332112060161331926819161129, ready for step three.

Step three divides the long number by 97 and requires the remainder to be 1 for a valid IBAN, with no other remainder accepted. Dividing 2332112060161331926819161129 by 97 leaves remainder 1, spoken as remainder one, so the IBAN GB29 NWBK 6016 1331 9268 19 passes the check and may proceed to the next validation. A person checking by hand works through the long number from left to right, carrying the remainder forward at each stage. A remainder of one is the only passing result, and any other remainder means the IBAN as typed cannot be valid and must be corrected before the payment is submitted.

The failure mode shows the value of the check, because changing a single digit breaks the remainder and exposes an error that the eye might miss. Changing only the final digit, from 9 to 8, gives GB29 NWBK 6016 1331 9268 18, and running the same three steps on that mistyped IBAN leaves remainder 71, spoken as remainder seventy one, which is not remainder 1, so the check fails. The payment screen must reject that IBAN at once with a clear message, and the payment must never be submitted with an IBAN that fails the check. Passing the check does not prove the account exists or belongs to the expected payee, because MOD-97 tests only the typing of the IBAN, while verification of payee answers the name question separately.

The business analyst designs IBAN validation into every payment screen so a mistyped IBAN never reaches the payment system or the receiving bank. The design checks the country code, checks the length for that country, strips spaces, converts letters to upper case, and runs the MOD-97 check on the normalised form before the payment is submitted. A failure shows a clear error that names the IBAN as invalid and asks the payer to check it, rather than a generic message that leaves the payer guessing. Storing the IBAN without spaces and in upper case, while displaying it in groups of four characters, keeps the stored and displayed forms consistent.
