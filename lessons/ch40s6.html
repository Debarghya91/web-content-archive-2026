Key learning points

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Correct result. A break is found by matching amount, currency, date and reference across the three sources. The root cause is a timing difference, an amount difference, a missing entry, or a duplicate, and the corrective action follows the cause.

Why. Reconciliation is the last line of defence. A break that is not investigated is money that cannot be proved.

Automated reconciliation uses rules to match internal records against external statements at high speed and at high volume, and it is the only practical way to reconcile a large bank. A modern engine can load a camt.053 statement with fifty thousand lines, match ninety-eight per cent of them in minutes, and produce a report listing only the two per cent that need human attention. The rules cover exact matches on reference and amount, tolerance matches for fees, and value date windows for timing differences. Automation therefore removes the routine work and leaves the exceptions, which is exactly where skilled analysts add the most value. Manual reconciliation still matters for the items that automation cannot resolve, and those items are usually the most interesting and the most risky. An analyst manually matching a payment must read the statement narrative, compare it with the internal payment record, check the customer instruction, and sometimes telephone the correspondent bank to confirm what happened. For example, a USD 42,000 credit with a truncated reference and no customer name cannot be matched by any rule, but an analyst who recognises the amount and the value date from a corporate customer's regular salary run can link it correctly. That judgement cannot be automated, and it is why reconciliation teams still employ experienced people. End-of-day processing is the sequence that closes the payment day, and reconciliation is its final gate before the books are declared closed. The sequence typically starts with a cut-off time, after which no new payments are accepted for that value date. The payment engine then completes all pending sends, the core banking system posts all ledger entries, and the settlement system confirms which payments settled. Only then does the reconciliation engine run, comparing the day's internal records against the statements and reports that have arrived. If the reconciliation shows material breaks, the day cannot be closed until those breaks are explained or escalated. The control totals that run through end-of-day processing are the simplest and most powerful check in the whole sequence. Before the day starts, the bank knows how many payments it expects to process and their total value, and after the day ends, it compares those expected totals against the actual totals in each system. If the bank expected to send 8,000 payments worth EUR 96 million and the payment engine shows 8,000 payments worth EUR 96 million, but the settlement report shows 7,990 payments worth EUR 95.8 million, then ten payments worth EUR 200,000 did not settle, and the reconciliation report must list them. Control totals catch missing files and dropped batches that transaction-level matching might miss. The trend in large banks is toward intraday reconciliation, where matching runs several times during the day rather than once at night, because finding a problem at noon is cheaper than finding it at midnight. Intraday runs let the operations team fix a duplicate or a missing file while the payment day is still open and the beneficiary bank can still be contacted. End-of-day reconciliation remains the formal control, but intraday reconciliation is the operational safety net. Analysts should expect both in a modern bank, and should understand that the end-of-day report is the one auditors will ask to see, while the intraday reports are the ones that prevent the problems the auditors would find.
