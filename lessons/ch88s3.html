What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain national CSMs across Europe and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. CSM choice is a cost and reach decision, and it changes as volumes and corridors change.

Chapter objective: Learner explains what a clearing and settlement mechanism is, how STEP2, national CSMs and TIPS clear and settle payments, how a bank chooses a CSM, and how payments route when debtor and creditor banks use different CSMs, with correct terminology.

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Transcript and full text

Every SEPA country has its own clearing history, and most still run a national CSM alongside the pan-European ones. France has STET, which runs the CORE clearing system for French domestic and SEPA payments. Germany has the Bundesbank retail payment system, known as EMZ, which clears SEPA and domestic payments. Italy has BI-COMP, operated by the Bank of Italy. Spain has Iberpay, which runs the SNCE clearing system. The Netherlands clears through the Dutch CSM operated with the banking community. Belgium has the Centre for Exchange and Clearing. These national CSMs all clear SEPA payments under the same rulebooks, but they differ in ways that matter to a bank choosing between them. The first difference is the instrument mix. A national CSM usually clears domestic instruments as well as SEPA ones. In France, STET clears cheques and domestic direct debits alongside SEPA credit transfers. In Germany, EMZ handles domestic direct debits in the German legacy format as well as SEPA direct debits. A bank that still has domestic volume needs a CSM that handles it, and STEP2 alone does not. The second difference is the cycle pattern and the cut-off times. A national CSM is tuned to its domestic market: its cycles match the domestic business day, its cut-offs match domestic customer expectations, and its settlement times match the domestic working hours. A bank whose customers expect a payment made at 15:00 to reach the creditor the same day needs a CSM with a late cycle, and the national CSM is often the one that provides it. The third difference is pricing. National CSMs usually charge per transaction with volume tiers, and the per-item price for domestic volume is often lower than the pan-European price, because the national CSM has lower costs and no cross-border handling. A bank must compare the total monthly cost, not the headline per-item price, because the fixed membership fee, the file fees, and the charges for R-transactions all add up. The fourth difference is value-added services. Some national CSMs manage direct debit mandates, run the national direct debit database, or provide the creditor reference checks that a particular market expects. A bank that uses a national CSM for direct debits may get mandate storage and mandate amendment handling included, while STEP2 expects the bank to manage mandates itself. Banks use more than one CSM for four practical reasons. The first is instrument split: domestic payments through the national CSM, cross-border payments through STEP2. The second is resilience: if one CSM has an outage, the payment hub routes through the other, and the bank can still meet its customer cut-offs. The third is geography: a banking group with subsidiaries in Germany, France and Italy joins the CSM in each country where it has volume, because each national CSM gives it local reach and local cut-offs. The fourth is commercial: a bank negotiates volume across CSMs, or moves volume when one CSM raises its prices. A worked example shows the routing. A German bank sends 100,000 SEPA credit transfers a day. 70,000 go to German creditor banks and are sent through EMZ, because EMZ is cheaper for domestic volume and its last cycle is at 16:30. 30,000 go to creditor banks in other SEPA countries and are sent through STEP2, because STEP2 reaches all of them from one connection. The payment hub decides the route from the creditor IBAN country code and the BIC, and it must never send the same payment to both CSMs. Edge cases are where the work is. A payment routed to the wrong CSM can be rejected, or worse, can be processed and then need to be returned, which costs a day. A duplicate submission to two CSMs double-pays the creditor and creates a recovery case. A bank that changes its routing must update its reachability tables on the same day, or payments will be sent to a CSM that no longer accepts them. For a business analyst, multiple CSMs mean a routing table, a cut-off table, and a reconciliation per CSM. The requirements must state the routing rule for each instrument and each creditor country, the cut-off for each CSM, and the fallback route when a CSM is unavailable. The acceptance criteria must include a test where the primary CSM is unavailable and the payment is routed through the fallback without being duplicated. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
