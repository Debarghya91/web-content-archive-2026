Key terms

Direct participant: A bank or institution that holds an account in a payment system and sends and receives payments directly.

Intraday liquidity: Cash available to a bank during the business day to settle payments as they fall due. Intraday liquidity comes from account balances, incoming payments and central bank facilities.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Show transcript

CHAPS is the UK system for high-value, same-day, irrevocable sterling payments. It is used for house purchases, large corporate payments, and interbank settlement. A CHAPS payment is settled in central bank money across accounts at the Bank of England, in real time, gross, one payment at a time. That is what RTGS means: real-time gross settlement. There is no netting, no waiting for the end of the day, and once the payment is settled it cannot be recalled. The finality is the point. A solicitor who receives a CHAPS payment for a house purchase knows the money is theirs, in full, immediately.

CHAPS operates between 06:00 and 18:00 on working days. Most banks cut off customer CHAPS payments around 17:00, and interbank payments run until 18:00. A payment submitted after the cut-off waits until the next working day, and there is no CHAPS on weekends or bank holidays. The cost reflects the service: a bank typically charges a retail customer between 20 and 35 pounds for a CHAPS payment, and the fee is charged whether the payment succeeds or fails, so a CHAPS payment with a wrong sort code is an expensive mistake.

The Bank of England operates the RTGS infrastructure that CHAPS runs on, and the Bank is also the settlement agent. Direct participants hold settlement accounts at the Bank and send payments directly. Indirect participants access CHAPS through a direct participant, usually a larger bank, which settles on their behalf. The direct participant bears the liquidity risk for its indirect participants, and charges for the service. A firm that wants direct access must meet the Bank's access criteria, hold a settlement account, and prove it can manage its intraday liquidity.

Intraday liquidity is the core operational problem in CHAPS. A bank must have enough central bank money in its settlement account to fund each payment as it goes out, and incoming payments replenish it during the day. The Bank of England provides intraday liquidity against eligible collateral, and the auto-collateralisation mechanism lets a bank borrow automatically against securities it holds. A bank that runs out of liquidity queues its payments, and a queue can cascade: if one bank holds back payments, other banks do not receive the funds they expected, and their payments queue too. The Bank monitors throughput, and expects direct participants to settle a proportion of their payments early in the day, not all of them at 17:30.

CHAPS migrated from SWIFT MT messages to ISO 20022 in June 2023, with a like-for-like migration first and enriched data following. The MT103 became the pacs.008, and the MT202 became the pacs.009. The migration matters because ISO 20022 carries structured remittance data, a legal entity identifier, and a purpose code, which improve screening and reconciliation. Banks that had hard-coded MT field parsing had to rebuild their payment engines, and the coexistence period, when some banks sent MT and others sent MX, required translation in the middle. A payment that arrives as a pacs.008 with a purpose code of SUPP, for supplier payment, can be auto-reconciled in a way an MT103 never could.

What goes wrong in CHAPS is usually liquidity or data, not the network. A payment with an invalid IBAN or a missing beneficiary name is rejected before settlement, and the payer must resubmit. A payment that passes validation but exceeds the bank's available liquidity sits in the queue, and the operations team must decide whether to inject liquidity, hold the payment, or contact the customer. A duplicate CHAPS payment is rare but serious, because the payment is irrevocable, and recovery depends on the beneficiary agreeing to return the funds. The most common operational failure is a cut-off miss: a customer who instructs a house purchase payment at 17:15 finds it cannot be made until the next day, and the completion is delayed.

For a business analyst, CHAPS requirements are about finality, liquidity and cut-offs. The acceptance criteria must state the cut-off time for each customer type, the fee, and what happens when a payment is submitted after the cut-off. A test must prove that a payment settles gross in central bank money and cannot be recalled. The requirements must cover the ISO 20022 message set, including the pacs.008 for customer payments, the pacs.009 for bank to bank payments, and the pacs.002 for status reports. The design must handle the liquidity queue, and the operations team must have a procedure for a payment that is queued at the end of the day. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
