Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

FATCA, GATCA and the Common Reporting Standard are three names that describe two regimes and one nickname, and keeping them apart is the starting point for the whole topic. Enacted in 2010, the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act, FATCA, is United States legislation that obliges financial institutions based outside the United States to find accounts belonging to US persons and pass details of them to the Internal Revenue Service. The Common Reporting Standard, shortened to CRS, is a standard developed by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development in 2014 for automatic exchange of financial account information. GATCA is an informal nickname for the global version of the FATCA idea, and it is not a separate law or a separate reporting regime of its own.

FATCA looks for US persons, and its test reaches further than tax residence alone. A US person includes a US citizen wherever they live, a US tax resident, and certain US entities, and the bank must also look through foreign entities to substantial US owners. Reporting flows to the Internal Revenue Service, either directly or through the bank's own tax authority under an intergovernmental agreement with the United States. The enforcement mechanism is distinctive: a non compliant institution, and certain payments to undocumented accounts, face a 30 percent withholding tax on US source income such as interest and dividends, deducted before the money reaches the recipient.

CRS looks for tax residence, and its scope is far broader in geography but narrower in its test. A bank in a participating jurisdiction must identify accounts held by tax residents of any other participating jurisdiction, and more than one hundred jurisdictions now take part in the automatic exchange. Citizenship plays no part in the CRS test: a person is reportable because of where they are tax resident, not because of the passport they hold. The bank reports to its own tax authority, which exchanges the information once a year with the tax authority of each jurisdiction where the account holder is resident. There is no withholding mechanism under CRS; each participating jurisdiction sets its own penalties for failure to report. The United States does not participate in CRS, which is why the two regimes run side by side.

Identification starts at onboarding with a self certification. A new customer signs a declaration of the countries where they are tax resident and provides a taxpayer identification number for each country. The bank does not accept the declaration without checking it against the information it already holds, such as its address and place of birth records. A conflicting signal, known as an indicium, must be resolved with further evidence before the account can be classified. US persons document their status with a W-9 form, while non US persons use a form from the W-8 series, which certifies that the holder is not a US person. For entity accounts the bank must classify the entity and, where the entity earns mainly investment income, look through to the natural persons who control it, and establish the tax residence of each controlling person.

Once an account is classified as reportable, the bank collects a defined set of information and reports it every year: the name, address, tax residence and taxpayer identification number of the holder, the date of birth for an individual holder, the account number, the balance at the year end, and income paid during the year, split by type. The exchange is automatic, and the bank must also refresh its records when circumstances change, such as a move to another country. These rules also reach into payment operations: an account cannot be opened properly until the tax information is collected, a change of address or a new power of attorney can trigger a fresh review, and a payment linked to expired documentation can be held at screening until the form is renewed. Where withholding applies, the paying bank must deduct it, remit it, and record the amount withheld.

Getting these regimes wrong carries consequences for the bank, for its relationships and for the customer. A bank that fails to identify and report can face penalties, regulatory action, and remediation across its whole book of accounts. Beyond withholding, a FATCA failure can cost an institution its correspondent banking relationships, because other banks may refuse to take on the added risk of dealing with it. A customer who refuses to provide information can face restriction or closure of the account, and the account may be reported as undocumented. For a payments professional, the working lesson is that onboarding, screening, exception handling and account maintenance must all carry the same tax data, so that the right accounts are identified, the right information is collected, and the right report reaches the right authority on time.
