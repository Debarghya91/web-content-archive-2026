The Business Requirements Document, or BRD, records what the business needs and why, without describing how the system will be built. It opens with the background and business objectives, then states the scope, including what is in scope and what is explicitly out of scope. It lists the stakeholders, the business requirements grouped by priority, the business rules, and the success criteria that will prove the project delivered value. The BRD is owned by the business and signed off by the business sponsor, and it is the document that settles arguments about what was agreed. The Functional Specification Document, or FSD, is the technical companion to the BRD. It describes how the system must behave to meet each business requirement, and it is written for developers and testers. The FSD covers the functional flows step by step, the screen layouts, the field definitions, the validation rules, the error messages, the interface mappings, and the expected system behaviour in every scenario including failures. A single business requirement in the BRD can expand into several pages of functional detail in the FSD. A worked outline makes the difference clear. Consider a project to integrate UPI collect requests into a bank's corporate banking portal, so that a merchant can raise a collect request of 2,500 rupees against a customer who then approves it in their UPI app. The BRD states the objective, which is to let 5,000 merchants collect payments without sharing account details, and the scope, which covers collect requests only and excludes UPI person-to-person transfers. It lists the success criteria, including 50,000 collect requests per month within six months of launch. The BRD for this UPI project then lists the business requirements with priorities. A must-have requirement is that a merchant can raise a collect request with the customer's virtual payment address, an amount and a validity period of 30 minutes. Another must-have is that the merchant receives a real-time notification when the customer approves or rejects the request. A should-have requirement is a dashboard showing the status of all collect requests raised in the last 30 days. The BRD also records the business rules, such as a maximum collect amount of 100,000 rupees per request and a daily limit of 200,000 rupees per merchant. The FSD for the same project turns these requirements into system behaviour. It specifies the collect request screen field by field. The virtual payment address field must accept up to 50 characters and validate the format, the amount field must accept values from 1 rupee to 100,000 rupees, and the expiry field must default to 30 minutes. It defines the API call to the UPI switch, the request and response fields, the timeout of 30 seconds, and the error handling if the switch does not respond. It also defines the status flow. A request moves from Initiated to Pending to Success, Failed or Expired, and each transition is logged with a timestamp. Both documents are versioned and signed off, and changes after sign-off go through a formal change request. The analyst keeps a traceability matrix that links each business requirement in the BRD to the functional sections in the FSD, the user stories in Jira, and the test cases in the test plan. When a requirement changes, the matrix shows exactly which documents, stories and tests are affected, so nothing is missed. This discipline is what separates a professional payments BA from someone who writes requirements in an email thread.
