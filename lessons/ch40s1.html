Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

A payment can pass every upfront check and still end up in the wrong place, which is why reconciliation exists as an independent control after processing. Validation confirms that an instruction is well formed, sanctions screening confirms that it is permitted, and the payment engine confirms that it was sent, but none of those steps confirms that the money actually arrived where it was supposed to arrive. Reconciliation compares the bank's own records against independent external evidence, such as a correspondent bank statement or a clearing system report, and it flags any difference. That comparison is the last chance to catch an error before it becomes a loss, a customer complaint, or a regulatory breach.

Consider a corporate customer in London that instructs its bank to pay USD 250,000 to a supplier in Singapore through a pacs.008 message. The payment engine records the debit to the customer's account, the core banking system records the movement in the nostro account, and the correspondent bank in New York records the credit. If the correspondent bank credits USD 249,950 because it deducted a USD 50 fee, the three records no longer agree, and only reconciliation will reveal the difference. Without that check, the bank would believe it had paid USD 250,000 in full while the supplier received less, and the shortfall might remain hidden for weeks until the supplier complains.

Reconciliation also protects the bank against fraud, duplicate processing, and system failure in ways that no single system can protect itself. If an internal fault sends the same EUR 40,000 payment to a beneficiary in Frankfurt twice, the payment engine may show two successful sends while the customer expected only one. A transaction-level match against the clearing report will show one expected settlement and two actual settlements, and the duplicate becomes visible on the same day. Similarly, if a file interface drops ten payments out of a batch of five hundred, the control totals will not agree, and the missing payments can be investigated before their value dates pass.

Auditors and regulators treat reconciliation as a core financial control because it proves that the money a bank says it moved is the money that actually moved. Under most internal control frameworks, nostro accounts and suspense accounts must be reconciled daily, and any break older than a defined age must be escalated to senior management. A bank that cannot reconcile its accounts cannot prove its balance sheet is correct, because an unreconciled nostro balance may hide a payment that was debited but never sent, or sent but never debited. That uncertainty is why reconciliation failures feature so often in audit findings and regulatory enforcement actions.

For a business analyst, reconciliation is also where business knowledge meets operational reality, because every break tells a story about a process gap. A repeated break caused by value date differences points to a cut-off problem, a repeated break caused by missing references points to an enrichment problem, and a repeated break caused by fee deductions points to a charging problem. The analyst who can read a reconciliation break, trace it to its root cause, and propose a fix is doing work that directly reduces operational risk and customer harm. That is why this chapter treats reconciliation as a discipline to master, not as a back-office chore to ignore.
