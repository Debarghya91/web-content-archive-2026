Internal Ledger, Clearing and Settlement Reconciliation

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain internal ledger, clearing and settlement reconciliation. Every payment creates at least two ledger entries inside the bank, a debit and a credit, and the discipline of double-entry bookkeeping requires those entries to balance exactly.

Why it matters. The practical lesson for analysts is that internal reconciliation is not one check but a chain of checks, and a break in any link can hide a break further down the chain.

Chapter objective: Learner performs payment reconciliation, matching bank statements to internal records, identifying breaks, and resolving differences with clear actions and evidence. Learner applies this skill in realistic banking scenarios, with clear examples and correct terminology throughout.

Key learning points

  • Accounting treatment behind
  • Break investigation
  • Each settlement entry against bank's own settlement ledger

  • Sequenced reconciliations: the right order matters

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Exercise

Investigate the reconciliation break described in this lesson and find its root cause.

Use the three-way matching rule and the break categories in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Compare the instruction, the settlement record and the statement, name the break type, and state the corrective action.

Correct result. A break is found by matching amount, currency, date and reference across the three sources. The root cause is a timing difference, an amount difference, a missing entry, or a duplicate, and the corrective action follows the cause.

Why. Reconciliation is the last line of defence. A break that is not investigated is money that cannot be proved.

An unexplained balance in a clearing account is a classic audit finding, because it may hide a payment that was taken from a customer and never sent onward.

Advanced depth

Worked at message level

Worked example. When a customer in Bengaluru instructs a payment of INR 500,000 to a supplier, the bank debits the customer's current account by INR 500,000 and credits an internal payment clearing account by the same amount.

When it goes wrong. A settlement entry on the central bank report with no internal match may mean the bank received funds it did not expect, while an internal entry with no report match may mean a settlement instruction was rejected by the central bank. If a payment of EUR 120,000 was processed correctly in the payment engine but posted to the wrong general ledger account, the customer and the beneficiary may both be satisfied while the financial statements are wrong.

The accounting treatment behind these checks, double-entry bookkeeping, posting rules, and the movement from sub-ledger to general ledger, is taught in ch71 Ledger, posting and reconciliation, in D02 Accounts, ledger and accounting. Read ch71s1 Double-entry bookkeeping, ch71s2 Posting rules and ch71s6 Sub-ledger to general ledger for the full treatment, including the exact debit and credit for pacs.008, pacs.004 and camt.056. This lesson stays with the reconciliation method: how the bank proves its ledger, clearing and settlement accounts agree, and how it investigates the breaks. Settlement reconciliation compares the bank's settlement account movements against the report issued by the clearing system or the central bank. For example, after a day of processing through the local real-time gross settlement system, the central bank issues a statement showing every settlement debit and credit for that bank. The reconciliation team matches each settlement entry against the bank's own settlement ledger, checking the amount, the value date, and the settlement reference. A settlement entry on the central bank report with no internal match may mean the bank received funds it did not expect, while an internal entry with no report match may mean a settlement instruction was rejected by the central bank. The practical lesson for analysts is that internal reconciliation is not one check but a chain of checks, and a break in any link can hide a break further down the chain. A payment that fails internal ledger reconciliation should never be sent onward until the ledger break is resolved, because sending it would create a second break in the nostro or settlement reconciliation. Good banks therefore sequence their reconciliations, starting with the customer ledger, then the clearing account, then the settlement account, and finally the nostro account, so that each stage confirms the previous one. That sequence turns reconciliation from a detective control into a preventive one.
