Key terms

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Most retail clearing systems settle in batches: payments accumulate, they are netted or grouped, and settlement happens at fixed times during the day. STEP2-T works differently. It is the continuous gross settlement service of the STEP2 platform, operated by EBA CLEARING, and it settles SEPA payments individually, one by one, continuously through the day, in central bank money. Each payment settles as soon as it is processed, rather than waiting for the next batch cycle, and each payment settles for its full value, rather than being netted against other payments. The result is faster finality for each payment, at the cost of higher liquidity needs, because every payment needs its full value available at the moment it settles.

The contrast with the batch service, STEP2-S, makes the design clear. STEP2-S collects payments into cycles, calculates the net position of each participant for the cycle, and settles the net amounts at defined settlement times. Netting reduces the liquidity needed, because only the net difference moves, but it delays finality until the cycle settles, and a payment sent just after a cycle closes waits for the next one. STEP2-T settles each payment gross, as it arrives, so finality is immediate, and a payment sent at any time during the operating day settles at once, provided the sending participant has the funds. The choice between the two services is a choice between liquidity efficiency and settlement speed, and participants choose based on their payment mix and their liquidity position.

Settlement in STEP2-T is prefunded. Each participant holds a settlement account, funded in advance, and each payment debits the sending participant settlement account and credits the receiving participant settlement account, in central bank money, at the moment of processing. The prefunding means the system never creates credit exposure between participants: a payment settles only if the funds are there, and if they are not, the payment waits in a queue until funds arrive or the payment is rejected. The participant must manage its prefunded balance actively through the day, topping it up as payments flow out and drawing it down as payments flow in, and the analyst must design the liquidity monitoring that keeps the balance above the level the payment flow needs.

The service handles SEPA credit transfers, and its continuous model suits payments that need same day value with immediate finality. A corporate payment that must reach the beneficiary bank today, with certainty about when it settles, benefits from gross settlement, because the settlement time does not depend on a batch cycle. A high volume of small payments benefits more from batch netting, because the liquidity saving outweighs the delay. Participants can use both services, routing each payment to the service that fits its needs, and the routing decision is part of the payment hub design: the hub must know which service each payment type uses, what the cut offs are, and what happens when the prefunded balance runs low.

Queue management is the operational heart of continuous gross settlement. When a payment arrives and the funds are not available, the payment enters a queue, and the system retries it as funds arrive, in an order defined by the queue rules. The participant can see its queued payments, prioritise them, and fund the account to release them. A payment that stays queued past the end of the operating day is rejected, and the sending bank must tell its customer and handle the payment again the next day. The analyst must design the queue visibility, the funding alerts, and the end of day handling, because a queue that nobody watches will hold payments until they fail, and the customer will experience the failure as a payment that vanished.

A worked example shows the difference. A bank sends 100 SEPA credit transfers during the morning. Through the batch service, the payments accumulate, the net position is calculated at the midday cycle, and settlement happens at the cycle time, with one net movement per participant. Through STEP2-T, each payment settles individually as it is processed, the beneficiary banks receive final funds within moments of each payment being sent, and the sending bank settlement account falls by the full value of each payment as it goes. By midday, both services have settled the same 100 payments, but the gross service settled each one immediately and needed more liquidity during the morning, while the batch service settled them together and needed less. The trade off is liquidity against speed, and it is visible in every settlement account.
