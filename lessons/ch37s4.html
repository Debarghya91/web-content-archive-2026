Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Deutsche Bank runs its payment operations from Frankfurt, with a payment hub that connects directly to T2, the real-time gross settlement service of the Eurosystem, and to STEP2, the pan-European clearing system for low value euro payments. The bank is one of the largest euro clearers in the world, settling more than 1 trillion euros a day through its own accounts and on behalf of correspondent banks. A bank in South Africa that wants to send euros to a supplier in Poland will often route the payment through Deutsche Bank, which holds a euro nostro account for the South African bank and forwards the payment through T2. Deutsche Bank earns fees on every such payment and interest on the balances held in those nostro accounts.

BNP Paribas has a similar hub in Paris, with direct membership of T2 and STEP2 and a strong presence in the domestic clearing systems of France, Belgium, Italy, and Luxembourg. The bank strength is its network across Europe. It operates retail banks in France, Belgium, Italy, and Luxembourg, so it can often move a euro payment between two BNP Paribas entities without using an external clearing system at all. A company in Milan that pays a supplier in Brussels can have the payment settled inside BNP Paribas in seconds, while a payment to a bank outside the group goes through T2 or STEP2. This internal network is a major selling point for multinational companies that operate in several European countries.

SEPA, the Single Euro Payments Area, is the system that lets a euro payment move between any two countries in the SEPA zone as easily as a domestic payment. Both Deutsche Bank and BNP Paribas were founding participants in SEPA, and both process millions of SEPA credit transfers and SEPA direct debits every day. A SEPA credit transfer must arrive at the beneficiary bank by the next business day, and an instant SEPA payment must arrive within ten seconds. The payment hubs at both banks are built to meet these deadlines, with automated validation, automated sanctions screening, and straight-through processing rates in the high 90s percent range, which is typical for SEPA payments across the industry.

The correspondent networks of both banks extend well beyond Europe. Deutsche Bank holds nostro accounts in more than 40 currencies and acts as a correspondent for banks in Asia, Africa, and the Americas that need to send euros or dollars. BNP Paribas has a similar network, with a particular strength in Africa, where it operates in more than 15 countries through its subsidiary banks. A bank in Senegal that needs to pay a supplier in China in US dollars may route the payment through BNP Paribas in Paris, which converts the funds and forwards them through its New York correspondent. This network business is a major source of income for both banks, and it depends on trust: correspondent banks choose Deutsche Bank and BNP Paribas because they are large, well capitalised, and well regulated.

The correspondent business model is built on three revenue streams and one risk. The revenue streams are transaction fees on each payment, interest earned on the nostro balances that respondent banks must hold, and foreign exchange spreads when a payment is converted. A respondent bank that sends 500 million euros a month through Deutsche Bank might keep an average nostro balance of 50 million euros, and Deutsche Bank earns interest on that balance while charging a fee per payment. The risk is that the respondent bank fails, or is found to be facilitating illicit flows, and the correspondent bank is left with the loss and the regulatory consequences. That is why correspondent banks run rigorous due diligence and cut relationships when the risk no longer justifies the revenue.

Both banks have modernised their payment hubs in the last ten years, moving from separate systems for each payment type to a single platform that handles high value, SEPA, and cross-border payments together. Deutsche Bank consolidated its payment processing onto a single hub that serves its branches in more than 50 countries, while BNP Paribas built a shared platform for its European entities that now processes payments for France, Belgium, Italy, and Luxembourg from one centre. The consolidation reduced cost and improved straight-through processing. Consolidation is a long project, often taking five years or more, and it succeeds only when the business, operations, and technology teams agree on a single target design before the migration starts.

When a business analyst works with either bank, the practical questions are about cut-offs, standing settlement instructions, and charges. Each bank publishes cut-off times for each currency and each clearing system: a euro payment that misses the T2 cut-off waits until the next business day, and a dollar payment that misses the New York correspondent cut-off waits too. Standing settlement instructions, the SSI, tell the bank which correspondent to use for each currency, and a wrong SSI is one of the most common causes of a delayed payment. Charges follow the OUR, SHA and BEN rules, and a corporate treasurer who does not understand them will be surprised when the beneficiary receives less than the amount sent.

Both banks face the same failure modes, and a payments SME should know them. De-risking is the first: if a respondent bank is judged too risky, the correspondent may close the account with 30 to 90 days notice, and the respondent must find a new route or lose the ability to pay in that currency. Sanctions are the second: a payment that hits a sanctions list is blocked, and the bank must hold the funds and report to the regulator. Liquidity is the third: a correspondent that runs low on its nostro balance cannot settle payments until it is funded, and a payment can queue for hours. The operational lesson is that a payment route is never just a technical connection; it is a commercial relationship, a compliance judgement, and a liquidity commitment, and all three can fail independently.
