Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. Compare the instruction, the settlement record and the statement, name the break type, and state the corrective action.

Correct result. A break is found by matching amount, currency, date and reference across the three sources. The root cause is a timing difference, an amount difference, a missing entry, or a duplicate, and the corrective action follows the cause.

Why. Reconciliation is the last line of defence. A break that is not investigated is money that cannot be proved.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Cash scattered across a hundred accounts in twenty countries is cash the group cannot use. Cash concentration gathers it: surplus cash is swept from operating accounts into a central account, where it funds the group needs, earns better returns, and gives the treasury a single view of liquidity. The in-house bank takes the idea further: the group runs an internal bank for its own entities, with internal accounts, internal settlement, and centralised external banking. Concentration and the in-house bank are the two great centralisation tools of corporate treasury, and they are usually built together.

Cash concentration works by sweeping. In physical sweeping, funds actually move: at the end of the day, or at agreed times, surplus balances are transferred from operating accounts to a master account. Zero balancing accounts end the day at zero: every cent is swept to the master, and the operating account is funded again the next morning. Target balancing sweeps to a target balance: the operating account keeps an agreed buffer, and only the excess moves. In notional pooling, funds do not move: the bank offsets the balances of several accounts for interest calculation, so a surplus in one account offsets an overdraft in another, without physical transfers. The choice between physical and notional depends on regulation, tax, and the bank capability in each country.

The in-house bank is an internal bank for the group. Entities hold accounts with the in-house bank, denominated in the currencies they use. Payments between entities settle internally, on the in-house bank books, with no external bank movement: an intercompany payment is a debit to one internal account and a credit to another. Only net external payments leave the group, through the in-house bank external accounts. The in-house bank manages foreign exchange centrally, converting at group rates, manages liquidity centrally, funding the internal accounts from the external pool, and manages bank relationships centrally, so the group deals with a small number of banks instead of dozens.

The benefits are substantial. Fewer external transactions: intercompany payments never touch the banking system, saving fees and time. Netting: external payments are netted, so the group moves less money across borders. Better foreign exchange: conversions happen at group volumes and group rates. Centralised liquidity: the treasury sees the whole group position and funds it from one pool. Reduced bank fees: fewer accounts, fewer transactions, and stronger negotiating power. And visibility: the group cash position is a single report, not a hundred statements.

Concentration and in-house banking carry considerations that must be designed for. Regulation: some jurisdictions restrict cross border pooling, cash sweeping, or intercompany lending, and the structure must comply in every country it touches. Tax and transfer pricing: internal interest, internal foreign exchange rates, and intercompany balances must be set at arm length and documented. Systems: the in-house bank needs a ledger, internal accounts, internal statements, and reconciliation to the external bank accounts. And cut offs: the internal day must align with the external banking day, so that internal settlement and external funding stay in step.

For a business analyst, the design covers the account structures, the sweep rules, the internal payment flows, and the statements. The requirements name the accounts, the sweep types and timings, the internal account rules, the foreign exchange model, and the reconciliation between the in-house bank ledger and the external bank accounts. The test cases cover a sweep that fails, an internal payment between entities in different currencies, and a reconciliation break. Concentration, the in-house bank, and pay on behalf of structures form the treasury toolkit, and the analyst who understands all three can design the centralised treasury the group actually needs.
