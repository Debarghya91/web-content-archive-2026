Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Multilateral netting: Netting across many participants in a clearing system, where each participant settles only its net position against the system.

Liquidity transfer: A movement of funds between accounts held by the same participant to manage liquidity, for example from a main cash account to a dedicated cash account.

Correct result. Clearing validates, sorts, matches and nets the instructions, then produces a net position per bank. Only the net position settles, which needs far less liquidity than settling every payment individually.

Why. Netting is what makes high volume payments possible. Without it, every payment would need full liquidity at the moment it is sent.

Clearing House Interbank Payments System, or CHIPS, is the private sector system operated by The Clearing House for large value US dollar payments. It is the main alternative to Fedwire for wholesale payments, and it is used heavily for international payments, because many cross border US dollar payments are settled through CHIPS after arriving in the United States through correspondent banking. It is also used for domestic large value payments between banks, for securities settlement, and for corporate treasury movements.

While Fedwire settles each payment individually in central bank money, CHIPS settles payments through netting that is designed to save liquidity. The happy flow starts with a payment instruction and a prefunded position. A bank that wants to send a CHIPS payment first makes sure its CHIPS prefunded account at the Federal Reserve holds enough funds, because settlement draws on that prefunded balance. The sending bank then sends the payment instruction to CHIPS as an ISO 20022 message, and CHIPS places the payment in its queue.

CHIPS continuously looks for payments that can be offset against each other, and when a match is found, it releases the payments. The receiving bank is notified, credits the beneficiary, and the payment status moves from queued to settled, with final settlement in central bank money through the prefunded accounts. The netting model is what makes CHIPS different. During the operating day, CHIPS collects payment instructions and holds them in a queue, and it continuously looks for payments that can be offset against each other.

If Bank A owes Bank B ten million dollars and Bank B owes Bank A eight million dollars, the system can settle the net two million dollars rather than moving eighteen million dollars in two separate gross payments. This multilateral netting reduces the liquidity banks need to hold, which is a real saving when trillions of dollars move every day. At the end of the day, any payments not yet netted are settled through the participants' prefunded accounts at the Federal Reserve, so the final settlement is still in central bank money, and no payment is left unsettled overnight.

Operating hours, cutoffs and prefunding decide whether a payment settles today. CHIPS operates on business days with published hours and cutoffs, and participants must prefund their CHIPS accounts and manage their queues, because a payment that stays in the queue because the sending bank has not funded its position will not settle. The analyst must show the cutoff in the product and in the operations manual in Eastern Time, and must design the queue view so operations can see which payments are waiting and what funding is needed to release them.

A payment released to CHIPS is not the same as a payment that has settled, and the customer communication must reflect that difference, because a payment can sit in the queue for hours while the banks wait for offsetting payments or funding. Exceptions in CHIPS follow the queue and the funding position. A reject happens before the payment enters the queue, when the instruction fails validation or screening, for example an invalid account, a sanctions hit, or a missing mandatory field, and it is reported with a status message carrying a reject code.

A return happens after receipt, when the receiving bank cannot apply the payment, and the funds come back through a return message. A recall is a request to cancel a payment still in the queue, and it can succeed while the payment has not yet been released for settlement. A payment that stays in the queue past the cutoff because the sending bank never funded its position will not settle that day, and operations must decide whether to fund it or reroute it through Fedwire. The analyst working on CHIPS needs to understand the queue, the prefunding requirement, the cutoff times, and the difference between a payment released to CHIPS and a payment that has actually settled.

CHIPS and Fedwire are complementary, not competing: a bank may use Fedwire for a payment that must settle immediately and CHIPS for a payment where netting saves liquidity and a short delay is acceptable, and choosing between them is a treasury decision as much as a payments decision. The analyst checklist is therefore practical: confirm the prefunding process and the intraday funding alerts, confirm the cutoff in Eastern Time, design the queue view with funding and release status, and map the reject, return and recall paths.
