Key terms

Nostro: An account that a bank holds with another bank, usually in a foreign currency, to settle payments in that currency.

UETR: A unique end to end transaction reference assigned to each cross border payment for tracking through the whole chain.

PSD2: Payment Services Directive 2, a European Union law that opened banking to third party providers, required strong customer authentication, and set rules for payment services.

Practise this lesson

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a $10,000 payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full amount. Under SHA the sender pays the sending charges and the beneficiary pays the receiving charges. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives the amount less all fees.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Transparency is the direction in which cross border payments are moving, driven by regulation, by competition from fintech companies, and by customer expectation. In the past, a customer who sent money abroad was told the amount to be sent and the charge of the sending bank, but was not told the exchange rate, the spread, the charges of the intermediary banks, or the time the payment would take. The customer discovered the true cost only when the beneficiary received less than expected, or when the payment arrived days later than promised.

Regulators in Europe, the United Kingdom, India and elsewhere have responded by requiring banks and payment providers to disclose the full cost upfront, including the exchange rate, the spread, the charges, and the expected delivery time, before the customer confirms the payment. The transparency rules change the design of the payment journey in practical ways. The payment screen must show the amount to be sent, the exchange rate, the amount the beneficiary will receive, the charges, and the expected delivery time, all before the customer confirms.

The confirmation screen must repeat that information, so that the customer has a record of what was agreed. The receipt must show the same information, plus the UETR, so that the customer can track the payment. If the payment is delayed or the amount changes, the customer must be informed. A business analyst who designs a cross border payment product must build these disclosures into the requirements, must test that the amounts shown are accurate, and must ensure that the rate shown is the rate applied, not a rate that changes after confirmation.

Fintech companies have pushed transparency further by offering a single upfront fee and a rate close to the mid market rate, and by showing the delivery time as a range with a high degree of accuracy. Banks have responded by improving their own disclosures and by adopting gpi, which gives the transparency on the journey that fintechs offer on the price. The analyst should study both models, the fintech model of a simple upfront price, and the bank model of a gpi tracked journey, and should design a product that combines the best of both.

The customer does not need to understand the correspondent chain or the nostro accounts, but the customer does need to know what they are paying, what the beneficiary will receive, and when it will arrive. That is the transparency standard, and it is the standard this chapter teaches.
