Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A SEPA Direct Debit collection is a pull payment: the creditor asks for money from the debtor account, and the settlement model must move the money from the debtor bank to the creditor bank on the due date, while keeping the return and refund paths open for weeks afterwards. The SDD settlement model does this in two stages that must not be confused. The first stage is the interbank collection: the creditor bank presents the collection to the debtor bank through a clearing and settlement mechanism, and on the due date the debtor bank debits the debtor account and the creditor bank credits the creditor account. The second stage is the settlement between the banks: the clearing mechanism settles the net or gross positions between the participating banks in central bank money, on the due date, through their settlement accounts.

Presentation follows a timetable that the creditor must respect. The creditor bank must present the collection to the debtor bank ahead of the due date, and under the current rulebook the presentation deadline is one business day before the due date for both first and recurrent collections. The creditor must also have pre-notified the debtor at least 14 calendar days before the due date, unless a shorter period has been agreed. The collection carries the mandate reference, the creditor identifier, the sequence type, and the due date, and the debtor bank validates it on receipt: the mandate reference must be present, the account must exist, and the collection must arrive by the deadline. A collection that arrives late, or with invalid data, is rejected before the due date, and no money moves.

On the due date, the debtor bank debits the debtor account and the creditor bank credits the creditor account, and the interbank settlement happens through the clearing mechanism. The mechanism can be a batch system such as STEP2, which nets the positions of all participants for the settlement cycle, or another clearing system agreed between the banks. Settlement is in central bank money on the due date, so the creditor bank is funded the same day it credits the creditor. The creditor must not treat the credit as final on the due date, because the collection can still be returned or refunded, and the settlement model keeps that possibility open by design.

The return and refund paths settle separately from the original collection, and each has its own timing. A reject happens before settlement: the debtor bank refuses the collection, and no money moves. A return happens after settlement: the debtor bank returns the funds because the account had insufficient funds, the account was closed, or another return reason applied, and the return settles as a separate movement back through the clearing mechanism, normally within five business days after the due date. A refund happens when the debtor claims the collection back: for an authorised collection, within eight weeks of the debit, with no reason needed, and for an unauthorised collection, within 13 months. A reversal happens when the creditor itself cancels a collection that should not have been made. Each of these is a separate settlement movement, and the creditor bank must be able to match it to the original collection.

The creditor liquidity position must account for returns and refunds. A creditor that collects 1 million euros on the due date and immediately spends it will be caught short if 50,000 euros comes back as returns over the following days. Prudent creditors hold a buffer against returns, monitor the return rate, and reconcile every return to its original collection before re-presenting or writing it off. The debtor bank must also manage its position: it debits the debtor on the due date, but if the debtor claims a refund weeks later, the debtor bank must pay the refund and recover from the creditor bank through the clearing mechanism. The analyst must design the reconciliation to stay open for the full window.

A worked example shows the timeline. A utility presents a collection for 120 euros with a due date of Friday. The creditor bank presents it to the debtor bank on Thursday, one business day before. On Friday the debtor account is debited 120 euros, the creditor account is credited 120 euros, and the interbank settlement moves the funds through the clearing mechanism. On Monday the debtor bank returns the collection because the account had insufficient funds, and 120 euros moves back through the clearing mechanism to the creditor bank, which debits the utility. The utility sees the return, contacts the customer, and either re-presents the collection or arranges another way to pay.
