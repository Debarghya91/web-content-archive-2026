Key terms

Open banking: A system where banks share customer account data and payment services with licensed third parties through secure APIs, with customer consent.

Refund: A repayment of funds to a customer after a payment fails, is cancelled or is disputed. In card payments a refund returns money to the cardholder account.

Reject: A payment instruction that is refused before settlement because it fails validation, screening or account checks.

Show transcript

Open banking is a regulated way for a customer to give a licensed third party access to a bank account, either to read information or to start a payment. The third party connects to the bank through an application programming interface, known as an API, and the customer gives explicit consent for each access. The third party never sees the customer bank password, and the bank checks the third party licence before it shares anything. A customer might let a budgeting app read transactions from three accounts in one view, or let a retailer start a payment straight from a bank account at checkout.

The bank stays the holder of the account, and the third party acts only within the consent the customer gave. Paying by bank can be cheaper for a merchant than a card and can settle faster. Before open banking, customers who wanted a combined view of their money often gave their bank login details to an app, a practice called screen scraping. The app logged in as the customer and copied the screen contents, which meant the customer password sat outside the bank and the bank could not tell the app from the real customer.

If the app was breached, the password could be stolen, and if the bank changed its website the app broke. Regulators saw this as unsafe and unfair, because it pushed risk onto customers while banks kept control of the data. Open banking replaced that fragile workaround with a standard, auditable API route. In Europe, the Second Payment Services Directive, known as PSD2, forced banks to open their accounts to licensed third parties from 2019. PSD2 said that a bank could not block a licensed provider, that access had to be free of charge for the provider, and that the customer consent and strong authentication rules had to be followed.

The aim was competition, new firms could build payment and account services without becoming a bank, and customers could choose services that suited them. Banks had to publish API interfaces, run a testing environment, and support the new providers, which turned account access from a bank privilege into a regulated right. The third payment services directive, PSD3, together with the Payment Services Regulation, is the agreed successor to PSD2 and would fix its weak points. PSD3 is not law yet: political agreement was reached on 27 November 2025, final compromise texts were published on 23 April 2026, and formal adoption and Official Journal publication are expected in December 2026 or January 2027, with most PSR obligations applying about 21 months later, around the second half of 2028.

Until then PSD2 remains the law in force. As agreed, PSD3 would tighten fraud rules, improve the way banks and third parties share fraud information, strengthen refund rights when fraud happens, and clarifies how payment verification should work. It also improves accessibility, so authentication must work for customers who cannot use a phone app or a fingerprint. For a business analyst, the point is that PSD3 is not a new idea, it is PSD2 made stronger, clearer and fairer, once it becomes law, and the work it creates is in fraud controls, verification, consent screens and accessible authentication journeys.

For example, a customer who banks with one bank and holds a savings account with another can let a budgeting app read both accounts, so the app shows a single balance of EUR 5,000 across the two accounts and lists the monthly spending in one place. The customer gives consent for 90 days, the app reads the transactions through the bank APIs, and the customer can revoke the consent at any time. No password changes hands, and the banks check the app licence on every call. A worked example shows open banking in practice.

A customer of Bank A wants to use a budgeting app to see all their accounts in one place. The customer selects Bank A in the app, is redirected to Bank A to authenticate with strong customer authentication, and consents to the app reading their account information. The app, acting as an account information service provider, calls Bank A's account information API and receives the customer's balances and transactions. The customer never gives their Bank A password to the app, and the app cannot move money, because it only has a read scope.

The customer can revoke the consent at any time from Bank A's app, and the budgeting app immediately loses access. For a business analyst, the requirement is to define the consent flow, the scopes, the revocation behaviour, and the liability if the app accesses data after the consent is revoked. A failure scenario tests the consent. A budgeting app continues to call Bank A's API after the customer has revoked its consent, because the app cached the access token and did not check whether it was still valid.

Bank A's API must reject the call with a 401 Unauthorized response, because the token was revoked when the consent was revoked. The bank must also log the attempt and may suspend the app's access if it repeatedly calls with a revoked token. The consent is not a one time check; it is enforced on every call, and a token that was valid yesterday is invalid today if the consent has been revoked. PSD3, which is agreed but not yet law as of 7 November 2026, strengthens these controls, but PSD2 remains the law in force.
