Key terms

Direct participant: A bank or institution that holds an account in a payment system and sends and receives payments directly.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

The SEPA model only works if any bank can reach any other bank, and that is why CSM interoperability is a requirement, not a convenience. The European Payments Council framework says that CSMs must exchange payments with each other, so that a payment sent through one CSM can be delivered to a bank that participates in a different CSM. Without interoperability, every bank would have to join every CSM, which would be expensive and pointless. With it, a bank joins one CSM and reaches the whole SEPA area.

The mechanism is straightforward in design and demanding in practice. Each CSM holds a reachability table that lists, for every BIC, which CSM can deliver to it. When a debtor bank submits a payment, its CSM checks the creditor BIC against the table. If the creditor bank is a direct participant in the same CSM, the payment stays inside it. If the creditor bank participates in a different CSM, the debtor bank's CSM forwards the payment to that CSM, using the same pacs.008 message, and the receiving CSM delivers it to the creditor bank. The two CSMs settle the resulting obligation between themselves, usually through their settlement accounts in TARGET2, now T2, at the end of the cycle. The debtor bank sees nothing of this. It submits one file to one CSM, and the CSMs handle the rest.

The payment hub's routing logic is where this becomes a system design problem. The hub must decide, for each payment, which CSM to use. The decision uses the creditor BIC, the creditor country, the instrument, and the value date. The hub holds a routing table that maps BIC ranges to CSMs, and it must update that table whenever a bank changes its CSM or a CSM changes its reachability. The rule is simple and absolute: a payment is sent to exactly one CSM. Sending it to two CSMs double-pays the creditor, and the recovery is a manual process that takes days.

A worked example shows the flow. A German bank sends a SEPA credit transfer to a French bank. The German bank participates in EMZ. The French bank participates in STET. The German bank sends the pacs.008 to EMZ. EMZ checks its reachability table, sees the creditor BIC is reachable through STET, and forwards the payment to STET. STET delivers it to the French bank. At settlement, EMZ owes STET the net amount for all payments exchanged between them in that cycle, and that net amount settles in T2. Alternatively, the German bank could have sent the payment through STEP2, which reaches both banks directly, and no forwarding would have been needed. The choice between the direct route and the forwarded route is a routing decision the bank makes in advance, and it is usually driven by cost and cut-off.

Edge cases are frequent and they are the reason interoperability needs constant attention. An interoperability link can fail, and when it does the sending CSM must either queue the payments until the link recovers or route them through a different CSM. A CSM may not support a particular instrument, for example it clears SCT but not SDD, so a direct debit to a bank reachable only through that CSM cannot be delivered that way and needs a different route. A bank that changes its CSM must be removed from the old CSM's reachability table and added to the new one on the same day, or payments will be sent to the wrong place. A duplicate delivery across CSMs, where the same payment is forwarded twice, must be detected by the message identification, or the creditor will be paid twice. Value dates can differ between CSMs: a payment that leaves one CSM in the morning cycle may arrive at the other CSM in time for its afternoon cycle, or it may miss it and settle the next day, and the debtor bank must know which it is before it promises the customer a value date.

For a business analyst, interoperability is a set of requirements about routing tables, fallback routes, and reconciliation. The requirements must state the routing rule for each instrument and corridor, the fallback route when an interoperability link fails, and the reconciliation that proves every payment sent to a CSM was either delivered or returned. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment is routed through an interoperability link and settles on the expected value date, a test where the link fails and the fallback route is used without duplication, and a test where a bank changes its CSM and the routing table is updated on the same day.
