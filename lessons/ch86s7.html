Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Show transcript

The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council, the FFIEC, is the body that coordinates examination standards across the US federal banking regulators, and its IT Examination Handbook is the document that shapes how a US bank builds and runs its payment systems. The Retail Payment Systems booklet in that handbook covers the examination expectations for ACH, wire transfer, card, and check systems, including the controls over origination, processing, settlement and reconciliation. An examiner using the booklet will ask for the risk assessment, the control inventory, the test results, and the incident history, and will test whether the controls work, not whether they exist on paper. A payment firm that sells into the US market must be able to answer those questions, even if it is not itself a bank.

The FFIEC BSA and AML Examination Manual is the companion document for the anti-money-laundering side, and it sets out what examiners test in a bank payment operation. The manual covers the customer identification programme, customer due diligence, beneficial ownership, transaction monitoring, suspicious activity reporting, and the independent testing of the programme. The examination is risk based, so the examiner focuses on the highest-risk areas: the correspondent banking relationships, the high-risk customers, the products with the most cash, and the geographies with the weakest controls. A bank that cannot show its risk assessment, its monitoring rules, and the disposition of its alerts will fail the examination, and the failure is published in the enforcement action.

Beyond the examination manuals, the substantive US payment law that a business analyst must know starts with Regulation E, which implements the Electronic Fund Transfer Act. Regulation E governs error resolution for electronic fund transfers: a customer who reports an error has 60 days from the statement date, the bank must investigate within 10 business days, and must provisionally credit the account if the investigation takes longer. It also governs the liability limits for unauthorised transfers and the disclosure duties. For a payment product the requirements are concrete: the error report workflow, the investigation clock, the provisional credit, and the final determination letter. A product that cannot meet the 10-day clock has a compliance gap.

The Uniform Commercial Code Article 4A governs wholesale wire transfers in the US, and it allocates loss in a way that surprises people from other jurisdictions. Under Article 4A, a payment order that is accepted in good faith and in compliance with a commercially reasonable security procedure is effective as the order of the customer, even if the customer did not actually authorise it. The security procedure is therefore the centre of the regime: the bank and the customer must agree a procedure, the procedure must be commercially reasonable, and the bank must follow it. If the bank follows the procedure, the customer bears the loss from a fraud that the procedure did not catch. The requirements that follow are about the security procedure itself: what it is, how it is agreed, how it is tested, and how exceptions are handled.

The Nacha rules govern the ACH network, and they are a contract that every participant signs. The rules set out the warranties each party gives: the originator warrants that the entry is authorised, the originating bank warrants that it has a valid authorisation, and the receiving bank warrants that it will post the entry. Returns are governed by strict codes and deadlines. An unauthorised consumer debit can be returned within 60 days of the settlement date, while most other returns must be made within 2 banking days. A firm that originates ACH debits must have the authorisation on file, must be able to produce it when a return arrives, and must monitor its return rates, because a return rate above the Nacha threshold triggers enforcement. The rules also cover same-day ACH, with its three daily windows and its per-transaction limit.

State money transmitter licensing is the part of the US regime that catches foreign firms by surprise. There is no single federal money transmitter licence. A firm that transmits money in the United States needs a licence in each state where it has customers, and each state has its own application, its own capital requirement, and its own examination. The Nationwide Multistate Licensing System, NMLS, provides a single application portal, and the states have agreed a common examination framework, but the licences themselves remain state by state. A firm that launches in 20 states must hold 20 licences, and must maintain 20 sets of filings. The Conference of State Bank Supervisors has worked to harmonise the regime, and federal legislation on stablecoins and payment stablecoins continues to be debated, but for now the state patchwork is the reality.

Fedwire and CHIPS sit at the top of the US payment system, and their oversight is different from the retail systems. Fedwire is operated by the Federal Reserve, which is both the operator and the supervisor, and its Policy on Payment System Risk sets the rules for intraday credit, for daylight overdrafts, and for the monitoring of participants. CHIPS is a private system owned by its participants and overseen by the Federal Reserve, and it settles in real time on a prefunded basis. For a business analyst the point is that the wholesale systems have their own control expectations: the daylight overdraft limit, the collateral requirement, the monitoring of participant credit, and the contingency arrangements. The acceptance criteria must include a test where an ACH authorisation is produced on demand, a test where a Regulation E error is resolved inside the 10-day clock, a test where a wire transfer follows the agreed security procedure, and a test where a money transmitter licence is verified for each state of operation.
