Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Open banking: A system where banks share customer account data and payment services with licensed third parties through secure APIs, with customer consent.

Refund: A repayment of funds to a customer after a payment fails, is cancelled or is disputed. In card payments a refund returns money to the cardholder account.

Show transcript

Open Banking in the UK began with the Competition and Markets Authority order of 2017, which required the nine largest banks to open their data and payment functions to third parties through standard APIs. The framework was built on the EU Second Payment Services Directive, PSD2, and is now governed in the UK by the Payment Services Regulations 2017 and the Financial Conduct Authority. The Open Banking Implementation Entity, OBIE, wrote the API standards. The result is that a regulated third party can, with the customer's consent, initiate a payment from the customer's bank account without the customer ever logging into the third party's system to move the money.

Payment initiation works through a payment initiation service provider, a PISP. The customer is shopping or paying a bill on the PISP's app or website, and chooses to pay by bank transfer. The PISP creates a payment initiation request and redirects the customer to their bank, where the customer authenticates, usually with strong customer authentication, and confirms the payment. The bank then executes the payment, normally as a Faster Payment, and redirects the customer back to the PISP with a confirmation. The PISP never sees the customer's bank credentials, and never holds the customer's money. The money moves directly from the customer's bank to the merchant's bank.

Consent is the legal core. The customer consents to a single payment, or to a recurring payment arrangement, and the consent is specific: the amount, the payee, and the date are part of it. The bank must show the customer exactly what they are consenting to, and the customer can revoke consent at any time. Consent for a single payment expires once the payment is made. Consent for a recurring payment lasts until the customer revokes it or the arrangement ends. A PISP that initiates a payment outside the consent it holds is in breach, and the bank must refuse it. The consent record is the audit trail, and it must be kept.

Variable recurring payments, VRPs, are the most important development in Open Banking payments. A VRP lets a customer authorise a third party to take payments of varying amounts, within limits the customer sets, without the customer approving each payment. Sweeping VRPs move money between a customer's own accounts, for example moving surplus funds into a savings account. Commercial VRPs take payments from a customer to a business, for example a utility bill or a subscription, and are being rolled out under commercial agreements between banks and third parties. The difference from a Direct Debit is control: the customer sets the limits, and the bank enforces them.

The regulatory framework has three layers. The FCA authorises and supervises PISPs and account information service providers, AISPs, and a firm cannot offer payment initiation without FCA authorisation or registration. The OBIE standards define the API messages, the security profile, and the customer experience, so that a payment initiation works the same way across banks. The Payment Services Regulations set the liability rules: if a payment is executed incorrectly, the payer's bank must refund it, and if a payment is unauthorised, the payer's bank must refund it immediately unless it can prove fraud by the payer. The liability follows the money, not the app.

What goes wrong in Open Banking payments is usually consent, authentication, or a bank API failure. A consent that has expired produces a failed initiation, and the customer must start again. Strong customer authentication that fails, because the customer cannot receive the one-time passcode or the biometric check fails, blocks the payment at the bank. A bank API that is down means the PISP cannot initiate at all, and the customer must use another payment method. A payment that is initiated but not executed, because the bank's Faster Payments limit is exceeded, leaves the customer in limbo, and the PISP must show the failure clearly rather than leaving the customer to guess.

For a business analyst, Open Banking payment requirements are about the consent journey and the failure states. The acceptance criteria must state the exact screens the customer sees at the bank, the consent record that is created, and the confirmation the customer receives. A test must prove that a payment cannot be initiated outside the consent, and that a revoked consent blocks any further payment. The requirements must cover VRP limits, including the maximum amount per payment, the maximum per month, and what happens when a payment would exceed the limit. The design must log every API call, because the log is the evidence in a dispute about whether the payment was authorised. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
