Key terms

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Creditor: The party that is owed money and receives a payment, such as a supplier, a seller or a service provider.

Every cross border payment that changes currency passes through the foreign exchange market, and the analyst who designs payments must understand how that market works, because the rate, the value date and the settlement of the currency conversion decide when the beneficiary is paid and how much they receive. The foreign exchange market is the largest financial market in the world, and it is decentralised: there is no single exchange, and currencies are traded over the counter between banks, brokers, corporates and platforms, around the clock, in every time zone. A payment that converts euros to dollars is a trade in that market, and the payment system and the trading system must work together for the beneficiary to be paid correctly.

The basic trade is the spot transaction, an agreement to exchange two currencies at an agreed rate for settlement on the spot value date, normally two business days after the trade date. The rate quoted for a spot trade has two sides, the bid at which the dealer buys the base currency and the offer at which the dealer sells it, and the difference between them is the spread, which is the dealer margin. A payment that needs same day conversion uses a value today trade, and a payment that needs conversion tomorrow uses a value tomorrow trade, and both carry different rates from spot, because the dealer must fund the position for a different period.

Forward and swap transactions let a payer fix a rate for a future date. A forward is an agreement to exchange currencies at a rate fixed today for settlement on a future date, and it protects the payer against rate movements between now and the payment date. A swap is a combination of a spot trade and a forward trade in opposite directions, used to move a position from one value date to another, and it is the tool banks use to manage the funding of their currency positions day by day. A corporate that must pay dollars in three months can buy the dollars forward today, fixing the cost in its own currency, and the bank that sells the forward hedges its own exposure in the market. The payment instruction and the forward contract are separate, and the analyst must make sure the payment is linked to the correct contract, so that the right rate is applied to the right payment.

Settlement of the currency trade is where payment risk lives. In a spot trade, each side must deliver one currency and receive the other, and if one side delivers and the other fails, the delivering side loses the full principal. This is Herstatt risk, named after the bank failure that made it famous, and it is why the Continuous Linked Settlement system exists: CLS settles both legs of a currency trade simultaneously, so that neither side delivers without the other. Not every currency pair settles through CLS, and payments in currencies outside CLS settle through correspondent accounts, with the timing gap managed by limits and cut offs. The analyst must know whether a currency pair settles through CLS or through correspondents, because the timing and the risk controls are different.

For payments, the conversion can happen at three points, and the choice affects the customer experience. The debtor agent can convert before sending, so that the payment travels in the beneficiary currency and the beneficiary receives the full amount. An intermediary can convert in the middle of the chain, which is less transparent, because the beneficiary bank receives a currency it did not expect. The creditor agent can convert on arrival, so that the payment travels in the sending currency and the beneficiary is credited in local currency at the receiving bank rate. Each model has different charge and transparency consequences, and the scheme rules and the customer agreement decide which model applies. The analyst must document the conversion point for each corridor, because a payment that converts twice, once at each end, loses value to two spreads.

A worked example shows the pieces. A company in the euro area must pay 100,000 dollars to a supplier in the United States in two days. The company asks its bank for a spot rate, the bank quotes a rate with a spread, and the company agrees. The bank buys 100,000 dollars in the market for spot settlement, and the trade settles through CLS, with euros delivered and dollars received simultaneously. The bank then sends a payment in dollars through its correspondent to the supplier bank, and the supplier is credited 100,000 dollars. The company euro account is debited at the agreed rate, the supplier receives the full dollar amount, and the conversion risk sat with the bank between the rate agreement and the CLS settlement.
