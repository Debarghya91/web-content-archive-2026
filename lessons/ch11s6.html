Key terms

Liquidity transfer: A movement of funds between accounts held by the same participant to manage liquidity, for example from a main cash account to a dedicated cash account.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Irrevocable: A payment or instruction that cannot be cancelled or reversed once it has been made, except by agreement of all parties or by a court order.

TARGET2, now replaced by its successor system known as T2, is the real time gross settlement system operated by the Eurosystem, which is the European Central Bank together with the national central banks of the euro area. T2 is the system through which banks settle large value euro payments in central bank money, and it is also the system through which the retail clearing systems settle their net positions. When a bank sends a payment through T2, the European Central Bank debits the sending bank's account and credits the receiving bank's account immediately, and the payment is final. T2 is the foundation on which the rest of the euro payment system rests. T2 is used for interbank payments, monetary policy operations, securities settlement, and high value customer payments that need immediate finality. It operates on business days during extended hours, with published opening and closing times and cut offs for customer payments and for interbank payments. A payment submitted after the customer cut off will be processed on the next business day, so the cut off is a hard deadline that corporate treasurers and operations teams plan around. T2 uses the ISO 20022 message format, and its messages carry the same rich information as other modern RTGS systems, including the debtor, the creditor, the purpose, and the end to end reference that allows straight through processing and reconciliation. The relationship between SEPA and T2 is the key to understanding the European system as a whole. SEPA is the set of schemes and rules for retail and commercial euro payments, and T2 is the settlement system that moves the money between banks in central bank money. A retail SCT payment is cleared through a clearing house and the net amount between banks is settled in T2. A high value payment can go directly through T2 without going through the retail clearing at all. This layered design, where retail systems net and the central bank settles the net amounts, is the same pattern seen in India and the United States, and it is the standard architecture of a modern payment system. The analyst who understands the layers can explain why a retail payment is cheap and a T2 payment is expensive, and why both are needed. T2 also includes a service called TIPS, the TARGET Instant Payment Settlement service, which settles instant payments in central bank money in real time. When a customer sends an SCT Inst payment, the clearing can happen through TIPS, which means the interbank settlement is also instant, not just the customer experience. This is an important distinction. In some instant systems the customer sees instant credit while the banks settle later, but with TIPS the settlement itself is instant. A business analyst who works on European instant payments should know whether the settlement in their flow happens through TIPS or through a deferred net settlement, because it affects the liquidity, the risk and the reconciliation design.

Follow one payment end to end. A German bank needs to settle a 50 million euro interbank payment with a French bank, and the payment must be final today. The German bank sends a pacs.009 financial institution credit transfer message to T2, giving the French bank's BIC, the amount and the reference. T2 checks the German bank's balance in its RTGS dedicated cash account, and if it is enough, it debits the German bank and credits the French bank in the same instant, and the payment status moves to settled. The French bank returns a pacs.002 confirming settlement. The whole flow takes seconds, and once T2 has moved the money the payment is final and irrevocable in central bank money.

T2 is built from components, and an analyst must know them. The RTGS component settles the large value payments. The CLM component, central liquidity management, holds the main cash accounts and lets banks move liquidity between their accounts and their dedicated cash accounts. TIPS, the TARGET Instant Payment Settlement service, settles instant payments in central bank money in real time. T2S settles securities transactions. A bank holds a main cash account in CLM and dedicated cash accounts for RTGS, TIPS and T2S, and it moves liquidity between them with camt.050 liquidity transfer messages. The analyst who designs a T2 integration must map which account each payment type uses, because a payment sent from the wrong account will be rejected.

T2 is used for interbank payments, monetary policy operations, securities settlement, and high value customer payments that need immediate finality. A payment submitted after the customer cut off will be processed on the next business day, so the cut off is a hard deadline that corporate treasurers and operations teams plan around. T2 uses the ISO 20022 message format, and its messages carry the same rich information as other modern RTGS systems, including the debtor, the creditor, the purpose, and the end to end reference that allows straight through processing and reconciliation.

Liquidity management is the daily discipline of T2, and it has tools the analyst must design for. Banks set reservations, which ring fence liquidity for urgent payments, and limits, which cap how much can be used for lower priority payments. Payments that cannot settle immediately go into a queue, and T2 runs optimisation algorithms that look for payments that can offset each other, releasing them together when a match is found. A payment that sits in the queue because the sending bank has not funded its account will not settle, and the operations team must see the queue, the position of each payment, and the funding shortfall. The analyst must design the queue view and the liquidity alerts with treasury, because a T2 queue is where a 50 million euro payment waits when the funding is short.

The relationship between SEPA and T2 is the key to understanding the European system as a whole. SEPA is the set of schemes and rules for retail and commercial euro payments, and T2 is the settlement system that moves the money between banks in central bank money. A retail SCT payment is cleared through a clearing house such as STEP2 and the net amount between banks is settled in T2. A high value payment can go directly through T2 without going through the retail clearing at all. This layered design, where retail systems net and the central bank settles the net amounts, is the same pattern seen in India and the United States, and it is the standard architecture of a modern payment system. The analyst who understands the layers can explain why a retail payment is cheap and a T2 payment is expensive, and why both are needed.

A worked example shows the net settlement cycle. During the day, STEP2 clears SCT payments between a German bank and a French bank. The German bank's customers sent 120 million euro to the French bank's customers, and the French bank's customers sent 95 million euro the other way. At the settlement cycle, STEP2 sends a single net settlement instruction to T2 for 25 million euro from the German bank to the French bank, rather than settling thousands of individual payments. T2 debits the German bank's account and credits the French bank's account for 25 million euro, and the cycle is complete. The netting saved 95 million euro of liquidity, and the final settlement is in central bank money, so there is no settlement risk between the two banks.

TIPS deserves a closer look, because it is the settlement engine for instant payments. When a customer sends an SCT Inst payment, the clearing can happen through TIPS, which means the interbank settlement is also instant, not just the customer experience. This is an important distinction. In some instant systems the customer sees instant credit while the banks settle later, but with TIPS the settlement itself is instant, in central bank money, in real time. A business analyst who works on European instant payments should know whether the settlement in their flow happens through TIPS or through a deferred net settlement, because it affects the liquidity, the risk and the reconciliation design. With TIPS the sending bank needs central bank liquidity at that moment; with deferred net settlement it needs to fund the net position at the cycle.

The analyst checklist for T2 has five items. First, the account structure, stating which dedicated cash account each payment type uses and how liquidity moves between them. Second, the cut off times, for customer payments and for interbank payments, stated in Central European Time. Third, the queue and liquidity tools, so operations can see a queued payment, its position, and the funding shortfall. Fourth, the message set, including pacs.008, pacs.009, pacs.002 and camt.050, with the fields validated. Fifth, the ancillary system settlement, stating how the STEP2 and other clearing net positions are settled in T2 and at what times. A T2 design that answers all five is a design treasury and operations can both sign off.
