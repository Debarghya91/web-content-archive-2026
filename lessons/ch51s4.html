The Collection Process Step by Step

Documentary Collections and SWIFT Category 4, Lesson 4 of 7, Narration 3:25, D06 Cross-border payments

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain collection process. The process begins with the sales contract between exporter and importer, which must state the collection terms clearly.

Why it matters. This matters because cross-border payments involve more banks, more charges, more time and more ways to fail than any domestic payment. Getting the chain right is the job.

Chapter objective: Learner explains documentary collections, the roles of remitting and collecting banks, and the SWIFT Category 4 messages used at each stage. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Process beginning with the sales contract
  • Exporter shipping the goods and obtaining the transport document
  • Documents matching the contract on amount, currency, quantity and consignee
  • Collecting bank
  • Final steps close

Key terms

Bill of exchange: A written order by one party instructing another party to pay a stated amount to a named person on a stated date.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Why. A collection is a sequence of handoffs, and a skipped step, such as remitting proceeds without an MT400, leaves the exporter unable to reconcile. An analyst must be able to walk the sequence in order.

The process begins with the sales contract between exporter and importer, which must state the collection terms clearly. The contract should name the payment term as D/P or D/A, state the amount and currency, name the remitting bank and the collecting bank if known, and state who pays the bank charges. For example, a contract for EUR 250,000 of pharmaceutical packaging from Lyon to Madrid might state D/P at sight, charges for the account of the importer, documents to be presented through Banco Santander. If the contract is silent on these points, the collection instruction must fill the gap, and disputes become more likely. The exporter then ships the goods and obtains the transport document from the carrier. The carrier issues the bill of lading, or an air waybill for air freight, showing the shipper, the consignee, the port of loading, the port of discharge and the description of the goods. The exporter prepares the commercial invoice, the packing list, the certificate of origin and any inspection certificate required by the contract. The exporter also draws the draft if the collection is D/A, or a sight draft if the instruction requires one. Every document must match the contract on amount, currency, quantity and consignee name. The exporter delivers the full document set and a signed collection instruction to the remitting bank. The instruction lists the documents enclosed, states D/P or D/A, gives the amount and currency, names the drawee and the collecting bank, and states what to do if the importer refuses to pay or accept. It may instruct the bank to warehouse the goods, to return them, or to contact a named agent. The remitting bank logs the collection, assigns a reference such as COLL 2026 0847, and forwards the documents and instruction to the collecting bank by courier or by secure electronic channel, with a SWIFT message to alert the collecting bank. The collecting bank receives the documents, checks that the instruction is complete, and presents them to the importer. Presentation means the bank contacts the importer, shows the collection schedule, and asks for payment under D/P or acceptance under D/A. The importer may examine the documents at the bank counter before deciding. If the terms are D/P, the importer pays EUR 250,000 into the collecting bank and the bank releases the documents. If the terms are D/A, the importer signs the draft for EUR 250,000 payable in 90 days, and the bank releases the documents against that signed acceptance. The final steps close the collection and move the money. Under D/P the collecting bank sends the proceeds to the remitting bank, less its own charges, and sends an MT400 Advice of Payment to confirm the collection. The remitting bank credits the exporter account for EUR 250,000 less charges, and closes the collection file. Under D/A the collecting bank holds the accepted draft until maturity, presents it for payment on the due date, and then remits the proceeds in the same way. If the importer refuses to pay or accept, the collecting bank notifies the remitting bank promptly, holds the documents, and waits for fresh instructions from the exporter.

The collection process has a clear sequence, and an SME should be able to walk through it step by step. Step 1, the exporter and the importer agree the sale contract, including the payment terms, D/P or D/A, the documents required, and the banks involved. Step 2, the exporter ships the goods and obtains the shipping documents, including the bill of lading, the commercial invoice, the certificate of origin, and the insurance certificate. Step 3, the exporter draws the bill of exchange on the importer, for the invoice amount, and prepares the collection instruction for its bank.

Step 4, the exporter gives the documents, the bill of exchange, and the collection instruction to the remitting bank. The instruction states the documents enclosed, the amount, the terms of release, D/P or D/A, the charges arrangement, and what to do if the drawee refuses. The remitting bank checks that the instruction is complete, and that the documents listed in the instruction are present. It does not examine the documents for compliance with the sale contract, because that is not its role under URC 522.

Step 5, the remitting bank sends the documents to the collecting bank by courier, and sends a SWIFT message, normally an MT410 acknowledgement is returned, and the collection is advised by an MT400 series message or by a covering letter. The remitting bank retains a copy of the instruction and the documents. The collecting bank receives the documents, checks that they match the instruction, and registers the collection in its system. It sends an MT410 acknowledgement of the collection to the remitting bank, confirming receipt.

Step 6, the collecting bank presents the documents to the drawee. It notifies the drawee that documents have arrived, and it asks the drawee to pay or to accept, as the instruction states. The drawee examines the documents, checks that they match the goods it ordered, and decides whether to pay or accept. The collecting bank should present the documents without delay, and should not release them to the drawee before payment or acceptance, unless the instruction authorises it to do so.

Step 7, the drawee pays or accepts. In a D/P collection, the drawee pays the amount of the bill, and the collecting bank releases the documents. In a D/A collection, the drawee accepts the bill, promising to pay on the maturity date, and the collecting bank releases the documents. The drawee takes the documents to the carrier, presents the bill of lading, and takes delivery of the goods. The collecting bank holds the payment, or holds the accepted bill, as the case may be.

Step 8, the collecting bank remits the proceeds to the remitting bank. In a D/P collection, it sends the money, less its charges, and it sends an MT400 advice of payment to the remitting bank, stating the amount, the value date, and the charges deducted. In a D/A collection, it holds the accepted bill until maturity, and on maturity it presents the bill for payment, collects the money, and then remits the proceeds with an MT400. The remitting bank receives the proceeds and credits the exporter account, less its own charges. The exporter is paid, and the collection is closed.

Step 9, if the drawee refuses, the collecting bank follows the instruction. It may hold the documents and await further instruction, it may protest the bill, it may return the documents to the remitting bank, or it may store the goods. It advises the remitting bank of the refusal by SWIFT, normally with an MT416, and it does not release the documents to the drawee. The remitting bank advises the exporter, and the exporter decides whether to find another buyer, to ship the goods back, or to take legal action. A collection that ends in refusal is not a failure of the bank, it is a commercial risk the exporter accepted when it chose a collection instead of a letter of credit.

For a business analyst, the process requirement must cover the documents, the presentation, the release condition, and the remittance. The requirement should state that the system records each step with a date and a status, that it prevents the release of documents before payment or acceptance, that it diaries the maturity date for a D/A collection, and that it generates the MT400 when the proceeds are remitted. A system that cannot enforce the release condition, or that cannot diary a maturity date, cannot process a collection safely.
