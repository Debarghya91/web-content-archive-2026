Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Tokenisation: Replacing a sensitive value, such as a card number, with a random token that has no meaning outside the system that created it.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Show transcript

A scheme rulebook is a contract, and it is the contract that makes a payment system work. When a bank joins Visa, Mastercard, or a SEPA scheme, it signs an agreement to follow the rulebook, and the rulebook then binds it in the same way a law does. The rulebook sets out who can join, what they must do, how payments are processed, how disputes are resolved, and what happens when a member breaks the rules. A bank that does not follow the rulebook can be fined, suspended, or expelled, and expulsion means it can no longer process payments on that scheme. The rulebook is therefore a control in its own right, and the bank must treat it as one.

Visa and Mastercard each publish operating regulations that run to thousands of pages, and they are updated on a regular cycle with mandatory compliance dates. The regulations cover authorisation, clearing, settlement, chargebacks, and the technical standards for messages and files. They also contain mandates: specific changes that every member must implement by a date. Recent mandates have covered 3D Secure, tokenisation, the display of the merchant name, and the handling of recurring payments. A mandate is not optional, and the scheme monitors compliance and fines members that miss the date. The compliance team must therefore track the mandate calendar, assess the impact of each mandate on the bank systems, and deliver the change before the deadline.

The SEPA rulebooks work differently but with the same force. The European Payments Council publishes a rulebook for each scheme: the SEPA Credit Transfer, the SEPA Instant Credit Transfer, the SEPA Direct Debit Core, and the SEPA Direct Debit Business to Business. Each rulebook sets out the scheme rules, the message standards, the timelines, and the exception handling. Adherence is through a formal process, and a payment service provider that does not adhere cannot offer the scheme. The rulebooks are updated on a regular cycle, and the changes flow into the bank requirements: a new rulebook version means a gap analysis, an impact assessment, and a change programme, with a deadline set by the scheme.

Compliance monitoring is how the schemes enforce the rulebook. The schemes audit their members, either directly or through a qualified assessor, and they run compliance programmes that test specific areas, such as chargeback handling, authorisation rates, or data quality. A member that fails an audit is given a remediation plan with a deadline, and failure to meet the deadline leads to fines. The fines are not trivial: they are calculated per case and can run into millions for a systemic failure. The bank must therefore have a compliance monitoring programme of its own, which tests its own adherence before the scheme does, and which tracks the remediation of any finding to completion.

Chargeback rules are the part of the rulebook that most directly affects the customer, and they are a control in themselves. A chargeback is a dispute raised by the cardholder bank against the merchant bank, and the rules set out the reason codes, the time limits, the evidence required, and the liability. The reason codes cover fraud, processing errors, and authorisation problems, and each one has a specific evidence requirement. Representment is the merchant defence, and it must be filed with the evidence the rulebook requires, inside the time limit the rulebook sets. A bank that misses the time limit loses the case automatically, whatever the merits. The chargeback process is therefore a workflow with hard deadlines, and the workflow must be built into the system, not left to a manual diary.

Rulebook changes flow into requirements through a defined process. When a new rulebook version is published, the compliance team reads it, identifies the changes that affect the bank, and writes an impact assessment. The impact assessment goes to the product and technology teams, who estimate the change, and to the board or the delegated committee, which approves the funding. The change is then built, tested, and deployed before the scheme deadline. The process sounds simple, but it fails when the rulebook is read late, when the impact assessment misses a change, or when the change is deprioritised against a commercial feature. The remedy is a calendar that tracks every rulebook version, every mandate, and every deadline, and a governance process that treats a scheme deadline as fixed, because it is.

For a business analyst, the rulebook work is a tracking and translation discipline. The analyst must be able to read a rulebook change, understand what it means for the product, and write the requirement that implements it. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a new rulebook version is assessed and the impact is documented, a test where a mandate is delivered before its deadline, a test where a chargeback is filed inside its time limit with the correct evidence, and a test where a scheme audit finding is remediated and closed. The evidence must be kept, because the scheme can ask for it, and because a rulebook obligation that is not tracked is an obligation that will be missed. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
