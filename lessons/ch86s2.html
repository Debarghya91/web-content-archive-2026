What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain DORA operational resilience and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. DORA turns resilience from good practice into a testable legal duty, and the analyst writes the requirements that prove it.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the regulatory framework that binds payments in Europe, the UK, the US and India, distinguishes PSD2 from PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation, and applies DORA, the Instant Payments Regulation, sanctions regimes, AML rules, RBI directions and FFIEC guidance to realistic payment scenarios with correct terminology.

Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Break: A difference found during reconciliation between two records that should agree, such as a bank statement and a ledger.

Correct result. Classify the incident against the DORA criteria, notify the competent authority within the deadline, activate the resilience plan, record every action, and file the follow-up report with root cause and remediation.

Why. DORA treats incident reporting as a control in itself: the regulator needs to know quickly so it can judge systemic risk.

The Digital Operational Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, known as DORA, applies to banks, payment institutions, electronic money institutions and the information technology providers they depend on, from 17 January 2025. DORA has five pillars: information technology risk management, incident reporting, digital operational resilience testing, third-party risk management, and information sharing. The core idea is simple and it is different from older rules. DORA does not ask for perfect uptime. It asks the firm to identify its important business services, to set an impact tolerance for each one, and to prove through testing that it can stay inside that tolerance when something breaks. An important business service is a service whose disruption would cause harm to customers or to the financial system. For a payment firm the obvious ones are payment initiation, payment execution, and the receipt of incoming payments. Each one gets an impact tolerance, which is the maximum tolerable level of disruption, expressed as a time, a volume, or a number of affected customers. A worked example makes the idea concrete. Suppose a retail payment service processes 400,000 SEPA credit transfers a day. The board might set a tolerance that no more than 2 percent of payments are delayed by more than 4 hours, and that no payment is delayed beyond the next business day. The tolerance must be set by the board, not by the technology team, because it is a statement about how much harm the firm is willing to accept, and the testing programme must prove the firm can meet it. The information technology risk management framework is the first pillar and it is where most of the engineering work sits. The firm must identify its information assets, map the dependencies between them, assess the risks, and put controls in place. For a payment hub the map includes the payment engine, the screening engine, the ledger, the messaging gateways, the network, and every database underneath. The framework must cover identification, protection, detection, response and recovery, and it must be reviewed when the architecture changes. A new payment corridor, a new screening vendor, or a move to a new cloud region all change the risk map, and the framework must be updated before the change goes live, not after. Third-party risk is where DORA bites hardest, because a payment firm that runs its hub on a cloud provider or uses a vendor for sanctions screening has outsourced the core of its service. DORA requires a register of all third-party information technology providers, a risk assessment before signing, contractual clauses that give the firm audit rights, security requirements and exit rights, and an exit plan that proves the firm can move the service to another provider. The European Supervisory Authorities can designate a provider as critical and supervise it directly, and a critical provider must then meet additional requirements and pay oversight fees. A firm that cannot show its exit plan for its screening vendor has a gap, and the supervisor will find it. Testing is the pillar most firms underestimate. DORA requires basic testing, such as vulnerability scanning and network security assessments, and advanced testing, such as threat-led penetration testing, for the most significant firms. Threat-led penetration testing is a live test in which a red team tries to break into the payment system using the tactics of a real attacker, while the blue team defends it. The test must be scoped to an important business service, it must use current threat intelligence, and the results must go to the board with a remediation plan and deadlines. A test that finds nothing is a test that was scoped too narrowly, and the board should ask why. Incident reporting under DORA has hard deadlines that the operations team must be able to meet. A major incident must be reported to the competent authority with an initial notification within 4 hours of classification, an intermediate report within 72 hours, and a final report within one month. A major incident is one that affects an important business service, affects a large number of customers, or causes a significant financial loss. Classification therefore has to happen within hours, not days, and the classification criteria must be written down before the incident happens. The payment operations team needs a procedure that says who classifies, what the thresholds are, and who sends the report, because at 3 in the morning there is no time to invent one. The United Kingdom runs a parallel regime that a firm operating in both markets must satisfy. The Prudential Regulation Authority requires firms to identify important business services, set impact tolerances, and test them, and the Bank of England can require a firm to prove it can recover within its tolerance. The UK Payment Systems Regulator also has an interest, because a payment system that fails harms consumers directly. The requirements are similar enough that one testing programme can cover both regimes, but the reporting lines differ, and the firm must know which authority gets which report and by when. For a business analyst, operational resilience is a requirements discipline. Every important business service needs a service definition, an impact tolerance, a dependency map, a test plan, and an incident classification procedure. The acceptance criteria must include a test where the primary payment hub fails and the service recovers within its tolerance, with the recovery time measured and recorded. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, Articles 5, 6, 19 and 28; the European Supervisory Authorities joint examination guidance on threat-led penetration testing; the Prudential Regulation Authority Supervisory Statement on operational resilience.
