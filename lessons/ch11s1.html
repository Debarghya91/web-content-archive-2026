  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

The Single Euro Payments Area, known as SEPA, is one of the most ambitious payment projects in the world. It makes a euro payment from a bank account in Germany to a bank account in France work in exactly the same way, with the same rules, the same formats and the same timelines, as a payment between two accounts in the same country. Before SEPA, a cross border payment inside Europe was slow, expensive and unpredictable, because each country had its own clearing system, its own formats and its own cut off times. SEPA replaced that patchwork with a single set of schemes and standards, so that a business in Spain can pay a supplier in Italy as easily as it pays a supplier in Madrid. SEPA covers more than the European Union. It includes all EU member states plus the countries of the European Economic Area and several others, including the United Kingdom for some purposes, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Monaco, San Marino and Andorra, covering more than thirty countries and over five hundred million people. The European Payments Council, known as the EPC, owns the SEPA rulebooks, and the European Central Bank oversees the system. Banks and payment service providers that join SEPA must follow the rulebooks, use the IBAN as the account identifier, and use the ISO 20022 message format for the interbank leg of the payment. The Single Euro Payments Area, known as SEPA, is one of the most ambitious payment projects in the world. It makes a euro payment from a bank account in Germany to a bank account in France work in exactly the same way, with the same rules, the same formats and the same timelines, as a payment between two accounts in the same country. Before SEPA, a cross border payment inside Europe was slow, expensive and unpredictable, because each country had its own clearing system, its own formats and its own cut off times. SEPA replaced that patchwork with a single set of schemes and standards, so that a business in Spain can pay a supplier in Italy as easily as it pays a supplier in Madrid.

The patchwork it replaced is worth describing, because it explains why SEPA was built. A German company paying a French supplier in 2005 had to use a cross border transfer with a different format, a different cut off and a fee that could be several times the domestic fee, and the payment could take three to five days. A French company collecting a direct debit from a German customer could not do it at all through the domestic direct debit scheme, because the scheme stopped at the border. Each country had its own clearing house, its own account numbering and its own rules, and a company operating in ten countries needed ten payment integrations. SEPA removed the border from the payment, not from the countries.

SEPA covers more than the European Union. It includes all EU member states plus the countries of the European Economic Area and several others, including Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Monaco, San Marino and Andorra, covering 34 countries and over five hundred million people. The European Payments Council, known as the EPC, owns the SEPA rulebooks, and the European Central Bank oversees the system. Banks and payment service providers that join SEPA must follow the rulebooks, use the IBAN as the account identifier, and use the ISO 20022 message format for the interbank leg of the payment. This standardisation is what makes SEPA work. Every participant speaks the same language, and a payment that follows the rulebook will be accepted by every participant.

The rulebooks are the governing documents, and an analyst must know how to read them. The EPC publishes a rulebook for each scheme, SCT, SCT Inst, SDD Core and SDD B2B, plus customer to bank and interbank implementation guidelines that specify exactly which fields are allowed, which are mandatory, which are optional, and what values they can take. New versions are usually published every year in November, and a bank must implement the new version by the effective date or risk having its payments rejected. The analyst's job is to turn the rulebook into requirements: which fields the screen captures, which validations run, which reason codes are mapped, and which timelines apply. A requirement that says the payment follows the SCT rulebook is not a requirement; a requirement that lists the fields, the validations and the codes is.

For a business analyst, SEPA is a lesson in how standardisation creates scale. A company that operates in ten European countries does not need ten different payment integrations; it needs one SEPA integration that works everywhere. A payroll file that pays employees in five countries can use the same format for all of them. A direct debit that collects a subscription from customers in eight countries can use the same mandate process. The analyst must still understand the local differences that remain, such as national cut off times, local clearing arrangements, and the way some countries handle specific use cases, but the core of the payment is the same across the whole area. That is the achievement of SEPA, and it is why it is studied as a model for regional payment integration around the world.

The economics are the reason businesses adopted it. Before SEPA, a cross border euro payment could cost 10 to 25 euro in fees and take days. Under SEPA, a credit transfer costs the same as a domestic payment, often nothing for a retail customer and a small fee for a corporate customer, and it arrives within one business day, or within ten seconds for an instant payment. The saving for a company that makes thousands of cross border payments a month is large enough to justify the integration cost many times over. The analyst who builds the business case for a SEPA integration should quantify that saving, because it is the argument that gets the project funded.

The Instant Payments Regulation is the latest chapter, and it changed SEPA from a set of optional schemes into a mandatory service. Regulation (EU) 2024/886 requires payment service providers in the euro area to receive instant payments from 9 January 2025 and to send them from 9 October 2025, with verification of payee mandatory from October 2025. An instant payment must cost no more than a standard credit transfer, and it must be available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, 365 days a year. The analyst must treat instant payments as the default euro payment, not as a premium option, and must design the initiation flow, the verification of payee step, the pricing and the 24 hour operations around that fact.
