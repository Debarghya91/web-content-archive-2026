Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Foreign exchange settlement carries a famous risk: Herstatt risk, named after the bank whose failure in 1974 left counterparties who had paid one currency leg without receiving the other. Continuous Linked Settlement, CLS, eliminates that risk by settling both legs of a foreign exchange trade simultaneously, payment versus payment. If one leg cannot settle, neither does. CLS is the global system for that settlement, used by the largest banks for the major currencies, and its timetable and member structure are designed around one idea: both legs, at the same time, in central bank money, every business day.

CLS settles through CLSBank, a special purpose bank that holds accounts in each eligible currency. Members fund their net pay-in obligations in each currency, and CLSBank settles the trades by simultaneously debiting and crediting the member accounts: the two legs of each trade settle together, linked, so that principal risk disappears. Members fund net, not gross: the pay-in for a currency is the net of all that member trades in that currency, which reduces the funding needed by a large factor compared with settling each trade gross. Netting plus simultaneous settlement is the CLS efficiency.

The settlement timetable runs in a window of several hours each business day, scheduled so that the real time gross settlement systems of the eligible currencies are open and overlapping. The window is organised in pay-in cycles: members fund their pay-in obligations according to a schedule through the window, and CLSBank settles trades as funding arrives. The schedule is published in advance, and members plan their liquidity around it: funding too late delays settlement, and late pay-ins follow defined procedures, including the use of liquidity facilities. The timetable is approximate in its detail and varies by currency and by day, but the shape is constant: a defined window, scheduled pay-ins, and settlement within the window.

The member structure has tiers. Settlement members participate directly: they hold accounts with CLSBank, fund their own pay-ins, meet capital and operational requirements, and settle their own trades and those of their customers. User members access CLS through a settlement member: they submit their trades via the settlement member, which funds and settles on their behalf. The tiering lets smaller institutions use CLS without meeting the direct membership requirements, while keeping the settlement obligations concentrated in the members best able to fund them. Liquidity providers, typically the settlement members themselves, support the system by providing funding in currencies where pay-ins run short.

CLS settles eighteen major currencies, covering the bulk of global foreign exchange volume. The currency set has grown over time as new currencies met the eligibility criteria: a real time gross settlement system, a supportive legal framework, and sufficient volume. Each currency adds a settlement window constraint, because its RTGS system must be open during the CLS window, and each currency adds a funding obligation for members active in it. The currency set is the footprint of the system, and it defines which trades CLS can protect.

The risk reduction CLS provides is systemic. Payment versus payment eliminates principal risk: no member pays away a currency leg without receiving the other. Netting reduces funding needs and liquidity risk. The centralised settlement, in central bank money through the member accounts, reduces credit risk. CLS is designated as a systemically important financial market infrastructure, and it is overseen cooperatively by the central banks of the currencies it settles. For a business analyst, CLS teaches the settlement design pattern that recurs everywhere: link the legs, net the funding, schedule the window, and tier the membership so that the obligations sit with those who can meet them.
