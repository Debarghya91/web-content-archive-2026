Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a $10,000 payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full amount. Under SHA the sender pays the sending charges and the beneficiary pays the receiving charges. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives the amount less all fees.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

Consider a small business in Manchester that needs to make four payments on the same morning, fifty pounds to a local supplier for stationery, two thousand pounds to a consultant, one hundred and fifty thousand pounds as a deposit on a new office, and salaries to thirty employees on Friday. A good analyst would not send all four through the same system. The fifty pound supplier payment and the two thousand pound consultant payment would go through Faster Payments, because they are small, they need to arrive today, and they are free or cheap.

The one hundred and fifty thousand pound deposit would go through CHAPS, because it is large, it must be final today, and the solicitor needs certainty that the money has settled before contracts are exchanged. This example shows the decision logic an analyst should apply to every UK payment requirement. First, ask the value. Above the Faster Payments limit, CHAPS is the answer. Second, ask the urgency.

If the credit must happen in seconds, choose Faster Payments; if it must be final in central bank money today, choose CHAPS; if it can wait three days, choose BACS. Third, ask the volume. If there are many payments, choose a batch system like BACS. Fourth, ask the operating hours. If the payment must move at midnight on a Sunday, choose Faster Payments, because CHAPS will be closed.

Fifth, ask the cost sensitivity. If the payment is low value and high volume, choose the cheapest system that meets the timing need. Writing these five questions into the requirements document makes the system selection transparent and reviewable, which is exactly what a payments BA is expected to do. The same logic applies to failure handling and customer communication. If a Faster Payments payment fails because the payee bank is not available, the app should explain the failure and offer to retry or to use another method.

If a CHAPS payment is submitted after the cut off, the system should warn the user before submission and offer Faster Payments as the alternative if the amount fits, with a clear statement of when the credit will happen. If the BACS salary file has an error, the system should reject the file before submission with a clear list of the bad records, not after half the employees have been paid. These are the details that separate a payment product that works in a demo from a payment product that works in production, and they are the details this chapter has been building towards.
