Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Fraud scoring: A risk score assigned to a payment based on factors such as amount, location, device, velocity and customer history.

Why. Screening is a control, not a suggestion. A missed true match is a regulatory breach, and an unexplained clearance is an audit finding.

Account validation is the first check, and it must confirm that the beneficiary account exists and can receive funds. The sender bank checks the account number format, the sort code or IBAN checksum, and the scheme routing table. Confirmation of payee then compares the name the payer typed with the name on the account, returning a match, a close match, or no match. A GBP 850 payment to a correctly named account passes in under 100 milliseconds. A no-match result does not automatically block the payment, but it triggers a warning that asks the payer to confirm, which has prevented many misdirected payments since the service launched.

Sanctions screening must run on every payment, because a single breach can cost a bank millions in fines. The screening engine compares the payer name, the payee name, and any remittance information against sanctions lists, such as those published by the United Nations, the European Union, and the United States Office of Foreign Assets Control. Modern engines use fuzzy matching to catch spelling variations, so a name like Mohamed and a name like Mohammed are treated as possible matches. The check must complete in under 200 milliseconds for most payments, which means the lists are held in memory and updated several times a day rather than queried from a slow external database.

Fraud scoring runs alongside screening, and it uses behaviour rather than lists to judge risk. The model looks at the amount, the time of day, the device, the location, and the history between the payer and the payee. A EUR 40 payment to a supermarket the customer visits weekly scores very low. A EUR 9,500 payment at two in the morning to a brand-new payee from a new device scores very high. The score is computed in under 150 milliseconds, and a high score routes the payment to step-up authentication, such as a one-time passcode or biometric approval, while a low score lets it proceed without friction.

Velocity and limit checks protect against both fraud and simple mistakes. A bank might allow a retail customer to send up to GBP 10,000 per day through Faster Payments, with a per-transaction limit of GBP 5,000. The system counts the payments already made that day and rejects or holds any payment that would exceed the limit. Velocity rules also catch bursts, such as ten payments of EUR 900 in five minutes, which can indicate an account takeover even when each payment is below the limit. These counters are stored in a fast cache with a short expiry, so the check adds only a few milliseconds to the journey.

When a check flags a payment, the system must hold it without losing it or confusing the customer. A held payment sits in a pending queue with a clear reason code, such as sanctions review or fraud review, and a deadline for a decision. An analyst reviews the case in a workbench that shows the full payment details, the screening hits, and the fraud score. If the analyst clears the payment, it proceeds to the scheme immediately, even if several minutes have passed. If the analyst rejects it, the debit is reversed and the customer receives a clear message. The key design rule is that a hold is a pause, not a failure, and the payment must never be silently dropped.
