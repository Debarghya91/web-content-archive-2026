A BIC, or Business Identifier Code, is defined by the ISO 9362 standard and identifies a financial institution or a branch of that institution. It is either eight characters long, which identifies the head office or the institution as a whole, or eleven characters long, which identifies a specific branch. The first four characters are the bank code, the next two are the ISO country code, the next two are the location code, and the final three, when present, are the branch code. Every SWIFT message carries a sender BIC and a receiver BIC, so the BIC is the routing address for the whole system.

An example makes the structure clear. The BIC BNPAFRPP is the eight character BIC for BNP Paribas in France. BNPA is the bank code for BNP Paribas, FR is the country code for France, and PP is the location code for Paris. The BIC BSCHESMM is the eight character BIC for Banco Santander in Spain, where BSCH is the bank code, ES is the country code for Spain, and MM is the location code for Madrid. The BIC PNBPUS3N is the eight character BIC for Wells Fargo in the United States, where PNBP is the bank code, US is the country code, and 3N is the location code for New York.

An eleven character BIC adds a three character branch code to the eight character base. The code DEUTDEFF500 identifies a specific branch of Deutsche Bank in Germany, where DEUT is the bank code, DE is the country code, FF is the location code for Frankfurt, and 500 is the branch code. When the branch code is XXX, the BIC refers to the head office or the primary office, so DEUTDEFFXXX means Deutsche Bank head office in Frankfurt. A payment sent to DEUTDEFFXXX will be handled by the head office, while a payment sent to DEUTDEFF500 will be handled by that specific branch, which may have its own accounts and operations team.

BICs are also classified by their status on SWIFTNet. A connected BIC belongs to an institution that is directly connected to SWIFTNet and can send and receive messages. A non connected BIC belongs to an institution that is registered on SWIFT but does not have its own connection, often because it uses a service bureau or a correspondent bank to receive messages. A live BIC is active and can be used for live payments, while a non live BIC is registered but not yet active. Operations teams must check the BIC directory before sending a payment, because a payment sent to a non live BIC will be rejected.

A BIC is not the same as an IBAN, a sort code, or an account number. The IBAN identifies a specific customer account, such as ES6300491800132710387658 for a Santander account in Spain, while the BIC identifies the bank that holds that account. The sort code identifies a branch in a domestic clearing system, such as 20-00-00 for a Barclays branch in London, while the BIC identifies the bank on SWIFTNet. A full payment instruction therefore needs both. The BIC to route the SWIFT message to the right bank, and the IBAN to credit the right customer account once the message arrives.
