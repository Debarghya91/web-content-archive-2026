Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Acquirer: A bank or licensed institution that processes card payments for merchants and receives transaction funds from card schemes on their behalf.

  1. What to do. State what happens at each stage, who pays whom, and when the merchant is actually credited.

Correct result. Authorisation holds the funds and returns a response code. Clearing sends the transaction in a batch file. Settlement moves the net amount between banks, and the acquirer credits the merchant after deducting interchange, scheme fees and its margin.

Why. A card payment is not one event. The customer sees it on Monday, the merchant gets it on Wednesday, and the difference is the clearing and settlement cycle.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A POS terminal is a certified payment device that captures card data, encrypts it immediately, and sends an authorisation request to the acquiring bank through the card network. The terminal holds a secure cryptographic key that is injected at manufacture or during deployment, and the card data is encrypted inside the secure element before it ever reaches the terminal software. The terminal also captures the amount, the merchant identifier, the terminal identifier and the transaction type, and prints or sends a receipt with a masked card number showing only the last four digits. A BA must document the terminal identifier structure, the receipt fields, and the rule that full card numbers must never appear on a receipt, a log or a screen.

Countertop terminals are the fixed machines seen at a shop counter, connected by broadband, Wi-Fi or a SIM card, and they are suited to high volume merchants such as supermarkets and department stores. Mobile POS terminals, often called mPOS, are small Bluetooth devices paired with a phone or tablet, and they suit delivery agents, small shops and field sales teams who need to accept payments on the move. Soft POS is the newest type. It turns an ordinary Android phone with NFC into a payment terminal through an app, with no extra hardware at all. A BA should capture the merchant segment, the connectivity available at the location, and the transaction volume when recommending a terminal type, because a soft POS device that works well for a street vendor may not suit a busy supermarket checkout.

When a customer taps a card, the terminal and the card communicate over NFC, the contactless radio technology that works within a few centimetres. The card sends a cryptogram, a one time code generated from the card secret, the amount and a transaction counter, and the terminal forwards that cryptogram to the issuer for verification. When a customer inserts a card, the chip and the terminal exchange a similar cryptogram over the physical contact, and the customer usually enters a PIN that is encrypted by the terminal before transmission. When a customer swipes a card, the magnetic stripe is read, but this is the least secure method because the stripe data can be copied, so most networks now prefer chip or contactless and many issuers decline or step up magnetic stripe transactions.

The terminal lifecycle is as important as the transaction itself, and a BA should document it from deployment to retirement. A terminal is first registered to a merchant and a location, loaded with the merchant keys and configuration, and tested with a test card before it goes live. During its life the terminal receives software updates, key rotations and configuration changes pushed from the terminal management system, and every update must be logged with the terminal identifier, the software version and the time. If a terminal is stolen, lost or damaged, the acquirer must be able to block it remotely and revoke its keys so that it cannot process payments. At retirement the terminal must be decommissioned and its keys destroyed, and the BA should specify the reports that prove no live terminal is missing from the inventory.

From a business analyst perspective, a POS project is not just about the device, it is about the whole merchant journey, onboarding, terminal deployment, transaction processing, settlement, dispute handling and support. The BA must write user stories for the merchant who receives the terminal, the cashier who operates it, and the customer who pays with it, and must define the error messages for common failures such as a declined card, a network timeout or a printer out of paper. A real example is a restaurant where the terminal loses connectivity during dinner service. The requirement should state whether the terminal can queue transactions offline, how many it can store, and how the merchant is warned. Without that detail, the development team will guess, and the merchant will discover the gap on a busy evening.
