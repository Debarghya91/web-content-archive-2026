Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Aggregator: A payment provider that lets many small merchants accept card payments under the aggregator master merchant account, without each merchant holding its own acquirer contract.

Acquirer: A bank or licensed institution that processes card payments for merchants and receives transaction funds from card schemes on their behalf.

Practise this lesson

Exercise

Compare the direct gateway model and the aggregator model for a merchant with 100,000 rupees of sales, and state the settlement path for each.

Use the direct model example in this lesson: the acquirer settles the net amount after a 2 percent fee directly into the merchant current account.

  1. What to do. State who holds the merchant account, who settles whom, and the net amount in the direct model, then name the extra check a business analyst must do before recommending an aggregator.

Correct result. In the direct gateway model the merchant signs its own agreement with the acquiring bank, the acquirer opens a merchant account in the merchant name, and the acquirer settles 98,000 rupees from 100,000 rupees of sales after a 2 percent fee directly into the merchant current account. In the aggregator model the aggregator holds one large merchant account, receives one bulk settlement from the acquirer and then splits it between its sub merchants. Before recommending an aggregator the analyst must check its authorisation status on the Reserve Bank website, because an unauthorised aggregator puts the merchant at risk of frozen settlements and regulatory action.

Why. The settlement path decides who controls the money and how fast the merchant is paid, and the authorisation check decides whether that path is safe.

A payment gateway is primarily a technology service. It provides the payment page, the API, the token vault and the routing engine, but it does not hold the merchant money. The merchant signs a separate agreement with an acquiring bank, the acquirer opens a merchant account in the merchant name, and settlement goes directly from the acquirer to the merchant bank account. Examples of pure gateway providers include companies that offer gateway software to banks and large merchants. The merchant keeps control of the acquiring relationship and can change gateway providers without changing acquirers, although the integration work still takes time. A payment aggregator, by contrast, holds one large merchant account with the acquirer and onboards many small merchants under it as sub merchants. The aggregator collects payments on behalf of all its sub merchants, receives one bulk settlement from the acquirer, and then splits the money and pays each sub merchant. Razorpay, PayU, Cashfree and CCAvenue operate as aggregators in India, and a small business can start accepting payments within a day or two, because it does not need its own direct acquiring agreement. The trade off is dependence. The aggregator controls the settlement timing, the fee structure and the risk checks, and the merchant cannot easily move its acquiring history elsewhere. Reserve Bank of India regulation draws a clear line between the two models. Payment aggregators must obtain authorization from the Reserve Bank under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, meet minimum net worth requirements, follow know your customer checks for each sub merchant, and hold customer funds in a nodal or escrow account until settlement. A pure technology gateway that does not touch merchant funds is not a payment aggregator, although it still must follow data security and outsourcing rules. A business analyst must check the authorization status of any aggregator on the Reserve Bank website before recommending it, because an unauthorized aggregator puts the merchant at risk of frozen settlements and regulatory action. The settlement flow differs in timing and in who the merchant sees on the bank statement. With a direct gateway and acquirer model, the acquirer settles the net amount, for example 98,000 rupees from 100,000 rupees of sales after a 2 percent fee, directly into the merchant current account with a reference such as ACQ SETT 12 NOV. With an aggregator model, the acquirer settles the bulk amount to the aggregator nodal account, and the aggregator then transfers each sub merchant share, often after deducting its own fee and holding a small reserve against refunds and chargebacks. The merchant bank statement then shows the aggregator name, not the acquirer, and the merchant must reconcile the aggregator settlement report against its own order records. Choosing between the models depends on merchant size, volume and control needs. A small retailer processing 5 lakh rupees per month benefits from an aggregator, because onboarding is fast, there is no separate acquirer negotiation, and the aggregator handles risk and settlement. A large enterprise processing 50 crore rupees per month usually prefers a direct acquirer relationship plus a gateway, because it can negotiate a lower merchant discount rate, control its own routing, and keep its settlement direct. Some large merchants use both, an aggregator for quick onboarding of a new brand, and a direct gateway for the main store. The business analyst documents the choice, the fee impact and the exit plan, so the merchant is not locked in without understanding the cost.
