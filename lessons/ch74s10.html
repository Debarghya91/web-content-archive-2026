Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

Exercise

Decide which settlement model fits the scenario in this lesson and explain why.

Use the settlement models, the liquidity trade-off and the finality rules in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

An overlay service is a customer-facing service built on top of the NPP rails, and it uses the addressing, messaging and settlement of the platform instead of building new rails. The rails move the money and carry the data, while the overlay decides what the customer sees, what the customer approves, and what the service is called. A bank that joins the NPP gains the rails once, then chooses which overlays to offer, so a new service can launch without a new settlement system. One platform can therefore serve instant payments, bill payments and mandates at the same time.

The rails underneath every overlay are the same three capabilities. PayID addressing lets a payer address a payment to a phone number, an email address or an Australian Business Number, and see the payee name before confirming. ISO 20022 messaging carries the payment with rich remittance data, so the receiver can reconcile automatically, and settlement through the Fast Settlement Service moves central bank money between the banks for each payment in real time. An overlay adds its own rules and screens on top of those capabilities, and a payment under an overlay still settles as an NPP payment.

Osko is the overlay most Australians meet first, and it is the fast payment service for person-to-person and business payments. Osko is operated by BPAY Group, and it lets a payer send money from a banking app to a PayID or a BSB and account number, with a rich description for the receiver. A payer splitting a restaurant bill sends AUD 45 to a friend PayID with a description naming the dinner, and the friend sees the money in seconds. A small business sends AUD 8,900 to a supplier with an invoice reference, and the supplier reconciles it without a separate email.

BPAY is the overlay for bill payment, and it lets a payer pay a bill through the NPP from inside their own banking app. The payer enters the biller code and customer reference from the bill, checks the biller name and amount, and confirms, and the payment travels over the NPP rails to the biller. A household paying an electricity bill of AUD 210 enters the biller code and reference, sees the energy company name, and pays on a Sunday evening. The reference is the feature that matters, because the biller matches the payment to the right customer account from the reference alone.

PayTo is the overlay for authorised recurring collection, and it lets a payee collect payments under a mandate the payer authorises in their own banking app. The payer sees the mandate terms, approves them, and keeps the power to amend, suspend or cancel the mandate at any time, while the payee initiates each payment under the mandate. A streaming service collecting AUD 18 a month and a gym collecting AUD 65 a month can both use PayTo, and each payer sees each mandate separately in their own bank app. PayTo turns a recurring collection into a payer-controlled agreement instead of a biller-controlled debit.

A single household can use all three overlays in one day without noticing the rails underneath. In the morning, one partner sends AUD 60 to a friend by Osko to settle a shared grocery shop, using the friend PayID. At midday, the other partner pays the AUD 210 electricity bill by BPAY, entering the biller code and reference from the bill. In the evening, a PayTo mandate collects the AUD 18 streaming subscription, and one set of rails settles each payment in seconds.

The BA decision on an NPP project is which overlay fits the customer need, because each overlay promises something different. A need to send money quickly to a person or a business points to Osko, a need to pay bills with a reference points to BPAY, and a need to collect recurring payments with payer control points to PayTo. The requirement must name the overlay, the addressing method, the data the customer sees before confirming, and the settlement and reconciliation behaviour. The test is to run one payment through each overlay and check the screens, the name and the reference.
