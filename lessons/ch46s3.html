What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain timeouts, duplicate prevention and idempotency. A timeout is a decision point, not just a delay, because it forces the system to choose a next step when an answer does not arrive.

Why it matters. The bank must therefore query the scheme for the status of that specific payment before deciding whether to retry, reverse, or mark it as unknown.

Chapter objective: Learner designs real-time payments architecture, covering 24 by 7 availability, instant clearing, liquidity, and failure handling, and explains how it differs from batch systems. Learner applies this skill in realistic banking scenarios, with clear examples and correct terminology throughout.

Key learning points

  • Timeout: decision point
  • Duplicate prevention
  • Idempotency: property makes retries safe
  • Timeout coordination across the chain
  • Layer knows its own deadline
  • Scheme shows no record

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Idempotency: The property that repeating the same request has the same effect as sending it once. In payments, an idempotency key lets a client safely retry a request after a timeout without creating a duplicate...

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

A timeout is a decision point, not just a delay, because it forces the system to choose a next step when an answer does not arrive. If a sender bank sends a pacs.008 message to the scheme and receives no pacs.002 response within five seconds, it cannot simply assume the payment failed. The money may already have been credited to the beneficiary, and a retry would send it twice. The bank must therefore query the scheme for the status of that specific payment before deciding whether to retry, reverse, or mark it as unknown. This status query uses the original instruction reference, so the scheme can answer definitively from its own records. Duplicate prevention starts at the point of entry, where the system checks whether an instruction has been seen before. A customer who taps the pay button twice on a mobile app can generate two identical requests for the same EUR 150 payment within a second. The channel layer assigns a unique request identifier to the first tap and recognises the second tap as a repeat of the same request. The system returns the result of the first payment instead of processing a second one. This check must happen before any balance is debited, because once the debit is made, unwinding a duplicate becomes an exception case that needs manual work. Idempotency is the property that makes retries safe, and it is built on a unique key carried with every payment instruction. The key might combine the customer identifier, the channel, and a universally unique identifier generated at initiation. When a service receives a request with a key it has already processed, it returns the stored result instead of executing the business logic again. A payment of INR 10,000 retried three times because of a network fault therefore moves money only once. The idempotency record must be stored durably before the payment executes, so that a crash during processing does not lose the proof that it happened. Timeouts must be coordinated across the whole chain, because a short timeout at one end can create a duplicate at the other. If the channel times out after three seconds but the payment service needs five seconds to complete, the channel may retry while the first attempt is still running. Architects therefore set timeouts from the outside in, giving each inner service a shorter budget than the outer caller. A scheme that allows twenty seconds end to end might give the sender bank fifteen seconds, which gives its internal services ten seconds, which gives the database five seconds. Each layer knows its own deadline and stops work cleanly when it passes. When a payment ends in an unknown state, the system must reconcile it rather than guess. An unknown payment is one where the debit may have happened but the confirmation was lost, and it appears in a reconciliation queue with its full message trail. Operations teams compare the sender ledger, the scheme record, and the receiver confirmation, usually by matching the end-to-end identifier and the UETR. If the scheme shows the payment as settled, the bank marks it complete and sends the confirmation late. If the scheme shows no record, the bank reverses the debit and tells the customer the payment did not go through. Speed matters here too, because the customer is waiting for an answer.
