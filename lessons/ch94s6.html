Nostro account optimisation: liquidity, pooling, sweeping and trapped cash

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain nostro account optimisation and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Nostro optimisation is a direct profit lever, and the analyst quantifies the saving.

Chapter objective: Learner explains cross-border payments in depth, traces correspondent banking chains, applies SWIFT gpi tracking and UETR, explains CBPR+ and the MT to MX migration, distinguishes serial from cover payments, prices FX, optimises nostro liquidity and applies sanctions screening across jurisdictions with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Nostro account
  • It: typically close to zero
  • Rules for each jurisdiction
  • Cost of trapped liquidity
  • Intraday liquidity monitoring
  • Payment queue and the liquidity forecast

Key terms

Intraday liquidity: Cash available to a bank during the business day to settle payments as they fall due. Intraday liquidity comes from account balances, incoming payments and central bank facilities.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Exercise

A bank holds 50 million dollars across 20 nostro accounts and pays 1.95 million dollars a year in funding costs. Work out the optimisation.

Use the rules, numbers and worked examples in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Work through the scenario and state the outcome with the reason.

Correct result. The bank pools the balances, sweeps surplus funds to where they are needed, closes unused accounts, and uses just-in-time funding to cut the trapped balance.

Why. Every dollar trapped in a nostro is a dollar that cannot be lent or invested, so the funding cost is real.

A nostro account is a bank's account with a foreign bank, held in the foreign currency. A UK bank's dollar nostro sits with its US correspondent, its euro nostro sits with its euro correspondent, and so on. Every cross-border payment in a currency debits or credits the nostro account for that currency. The nostro is therefore both the settlement engine and the liquidity pool for cross-border business. A bank with 40 nostro accounts across 12 currencies can hold hundreds of millions in these accounts at any moment, and the way it manages them is a direct driver of profitability. The core problem is that money in a nostro earns almost nothing and is trapped in that currency and that bank. A dollar sitting in a New York nostro cannot be used to fund a euro payment, and it cannot be lent to a customer. It earns the correspondent's overnight rate, which is typically close to zero, while the bank's own funding cost is far higher. Every dollar held in a nostro above what tomorrow's payments need is a dollar that is not earning its cost of capital. Nostro optimisation is the discipline of holding as little as possible in each account while never running out. Pooling lets the bank manage its nostro balances as a single position. Notional pooling gives the bank a consolidated view of balances across accounts for reporting and forecasting, without moving the money. Physical pooling, also called cash concentration, actually moves funds into a single header account at the end of each day. Both need the correspondent's agreement, and both face regulatory limits: some countries restrict moving funds out of a local account, and some require a minimum balance to be maintained. A bank must know the rules for each jurisdiction before it designs a pooling structure, because a sweep that breaches a local rule can be reversed and can attract a penalty. Sweeping is the daily routine that makes pooling work. An automated sweep rule moves surplus balances to a central account or an investment account at the end of the day. A typical rule says: keep 5 million dollars in the New York nostro for tomorrow's expected payments, sweep everything above that to the treasury account. The sweep is reversed in the morning if the forecast changes. The rules need limits and overrides: a sweep must not run if a large payment is expected the next morning, and the treasury desk must be able to stop a sweep when a corridor is disrupted. A sweep that runs blindly can leave an account short the morning after a large unexpected payment is instructed. The cost of trapped liquidity can be put in numbers. A bank holds 50 million dollars across its nostro accounts, earning 0.1 percent, while its funding cost is 4 percent. The annual drag is the difference, about 1.95 million dollars a year. If better forecasting and sweeping cut the average balance by 20 percent, the bank saves about 390,000 dollars a year on that currency alone. Across 12 currencies the saving is several million dollars. That is why treasury teams invest in forecasting models and why regulators ask banks to demonstrate that their nostro balances are actively managed rather than left to drift. Forecasting is what makes optimisation possible. The treasury team forecasts tomorrow's payment volume by currency, using historical patterns, the day of the week, known large payments from corporate customers, and the settlement calendar. Intraday liquidity monitoring then tracks the actual nostro balance through the day against the forecast. A large unexpected payment can push the account overdrawn, which triggers overdraft fees from the correspondent and can cause subsequent payments to be rejected. The payment hub must therefore share its queue with the treasury system, so a payment that would breach the nostro threshold is held and the treasury desk is alerted before the account goes negative. For a business analyst, nostro management is a set of integration requirements. The payment hub needs a real-time balance feed from each correspondent, a forecast model that predicts tomorrow's volume by currency, sweep rules with limits and overrides, and alerts when a balance falls below its threshold. The payment queue and the liquidity forecast must be linked, so the hub knows whether a payment can be released now or must wait for funding. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment that would breach the nostro threshold is held and the treasury desk receives an alert, and a test where an end-of-day sweep moves the surplus above the threshold to the treasury account and the movement is fully reconciled.
