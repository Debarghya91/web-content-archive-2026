Key terms

Suspense account: A temporary account used to hold funds or entries that cannot yet be posted to their final account. Banks use suspense accounts for unidentified receipts, pending investigations and items awaiting...

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Exercise

Investigate the reconciliation break described in this lesson and find its root cause.

Use the three-way matching rule and the break categories in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Compare the instruction, the settlement record and the statement, name the break type, and state the corrective action.

Correct result. A break is found by matching amount, currency, date and reference across the three sources. The root cause is a timing difference, an amount difference, a missing entry, or a duplicate, and the corrective action follows the cause.

Why. Reconciliation is the last line of defence. A break that is not investigated is money that cannot be proved.

A suspense account holds funds temporarily when the bank knows money has moved but does not yet know where it belongs, and it must never become a permanent home. For example, if a correspondent bank credits USD 18,500 to a nostro account with a reference that matches no payment the bank sent, the credit cannot be posted to a customer account because no customer is identified. The operations team posts it to a suspense account, opens an investigation, and contacts the correspondent bank to ask who sent the money and why. Until that question is answered, the USD 18,500 sits in suspense, visible on the daily reconciliation report and ageing by the day. Unmatched transactions fall into two groups, and the distinction determines how urgently each group must be worked. An unmatched statement item is money that moved on the external account without a matching internal record, which may mean an unexpected fee, an unsolicited credit, or a payment the bank never authorised. An unmatched internal item is a payment the bank recorded but the external system has not yet shown, which may mean timing, a rejected instruction, or a lost message. The first group is more dangerous because money has already moved, while the second group is more common because most payments take at least a day to appear on a statement. Duplicate payments are among the most expensive breaks, because the bank has paid the same obligation twice and must recover the second payment from the beneficiary. A duplicate can arise when a payment file is submitted twice, when an operator resends a payment after a timeout without checking whether the first attempt succeeded, or when a system retries a failed send automatically. Reconciliation detects the duplicate when two identical debits of, say, EUR 60,000 with the same reference appear on the statement against one expected payment. The bank must then contact the beneficiary bank, request a return through a pacs.004 message, and track the recovery until the money is back. Date mismatches are the most frequent breaks in most banks, and they are usually harmless, but they must still be explained and cleared. A payment with a value date of 10 November 2026 that settles on 11 November 2026 because of a public holiday in the beneficiary country will appear unmatched for one day, then match automatically when the settlement appears. The danger is that a genuine error can hide among dozens of harmless date mismatches, so the reconciliation team must age every break and investigate any break that survives past its expected clearing date. A break that is three days old deserves a phone call, while a break that is thirty days old deserves a formal escalation. The discipline that keeps breaks under control is daily ageing, ownership, and escalation, applied without exception. Every break on the reconciliation report must have a named owner, a next action, and a due date, and breaks above a threshold such as USD 10,000 or older than five business days must be reported to the operations manager each morning. Suspense account balances must be reviewed weekly by a senior manager who did not create them, because suspense is where errors and fraud can hide if nobody looks. A bank that works its breaks daily will keep its suspense balance small and its auditors calm, while a bank that lets breaks age will eventually find that a small break has become a large loss.
