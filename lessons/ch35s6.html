Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Irrevocable: A payment or instruction that cannot be cancelled or reversed once it has been made, except by agreement of all parties or by a court order.

When an interviewer asks what it means when a payment is settled, they want to hear that settlement is the final and irrevocable transfer of funds between banks to cover a payment. A good answer explains the three stages, authorisation, clearing, and settlement, and then gives an example. When a customer pays 10,000 rupees to a merchant by card, the authorisation happens in seconds and the merchant knows the payment is approved. The clearing happens later, when the transaction details are exchanged between the banks. The settlement happens when the issuing bank actually transfers the money to the acquiring bank, usually the next business day, and at that point the payment is final and cannot be reversed. The key word is finality, and a strong candidate says it. The difference between large value and retail payment systems is a classic interview question, and the answer has four parts. Large value systems, such as RTGS in India, handle high value, time critical payments between banks and large corporations, settle each payment individually in real time, and are operated by the central bank. Retail systems, such as NEFT, UPI, and IMPS, handle high volumes of low value payments from individuals and small businesses, settle in batches or through netting, and are designed for speed and convenience rather than individual finality. A strong answer adds that RTGS has a minimum amount, typically two lakh rupees in India, while retail systems have no minimum, and that large value systems are systemically important, which is why the central bank runs them. A third common question is the difference between authorisation and capture in card payments. Authorisation is the issuer's approval that the card is valid and the funds are available, and it places a hold on the amount. Capture is the merchant's request to actually take the money, which usually happens when the goods are shipped. A good answer explains that a merchant can authorise at order time and capture later, and that the gap between the two is what makes pre-authorisation work for hotels and car rentals. Another common question is what ISO 20022 is, and the answer should describe it as the global messaging standard for financial transactions, with structured messages such as pacs.008 for customer payments and pain.001 for payment initiation. Interviewers also ask about 3D Secure, chargebacks, and payment failures. A good answer on 3D Secure explains that it is an authentication protocol that verifies the cardholder, that version two supports frictionless and challenge flows, and that 3RI covers merchant initiated transactions. A good answer on chargebacks explains that a chargeback is a dispute raised by the cardholder through the issuing bank, that the merchant can defend it with evidence, and that too many chargebacks can lead to fines or loss of acquiring services. A good answer on payment failures explains the main causes, expired cards, insufficient funds, and fraud declines, and the recovery methods, including retries, dunning, and account updater services. The final set of questions covers the business analyst side of payments. An interviewer may ask how you would document a payment requirement, and the answer should describe a use case with actors, preconditions, main flow, alternate flows, and exceptions, plus the message mapping and the non-functional requirements. They may ask how you would handle a reconciliation break, and the answer should describe comparing the three records, finding the difference, and following the exception process. They may ask what a payment hub is, and the answer should describe the central routing layer. The strongest candidates answer with a real example from their own work, because a story with numbers and outcomes is far more convincing than a textbook definition.
