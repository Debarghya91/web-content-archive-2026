Exercise

Trace the payment in this lesson through its lifecycle stages.

Use the lifecycle stages in this lesson: acceptance, processing, clearing, settlement, beneficiary credit, ledger posting and reconciliation.

  1. What to do. Name each stage, state what happens, and say when the payment is truly final.

Correct result. Acceptance validates the instruction. Processing prepares it. Clearing sorts and nets it. Settlement moves the funds. Beneficiary credit puts the money in the creditor account.

Ledger posting records it. Reconciliation proves it. Finality is only at settlement, not at acceptance or at status update.

Why. The lifecycle is the map of a payment. If you cannot say which stage a payment is in, you cannot answer where the money is.

Acceptance criteria for an AI feature have to be testable, and they have to cover failure as well as success. With a normal feature you can write, when the user clicks pay, the payment is sent. With an AI feature you cannot write that so simply, because the model may be unsure, the data may be missing, or the answer may be wrong. So your criteria need to say what happens in each of those cases.

Start with the normal case. For example, when the model matches a payment of 9,400 pounds to an invoice with high confidence, the payment is allocated automatically and the customer sees it the same day. That is testable. Then write the failure cases. What happens when the model is unsure. What happens when two invoices look equally likely. What happens when the reference is missing entirely. In each case, name the fallback. The payment goes to a human queue, with the reason shown, and nothing is allocated automatically.

Also set limits on the failure rate. For example, no more than five in every hundred payments may go to manual review for this reason, and no payment above 50,000 pounds may be allocated automatically without a second check. Add a criterion for the fallback itself. The human must be able to see why the model was unsure, what data it used, and what it considered.

And add a criterion for turning the feature off. If the error rate rises above the agreed limit, the team can switch the feature off and return to manual processing without losing payments in flight. Write these criteria with the team before build starts, test each one with real examples, and do not accept the feature until every criterion, including the failure cases, has passed.
