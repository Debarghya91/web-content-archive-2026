Key terms

Market practice: An agreed way of using a message standard in a particular market or community, covering field usage, codes and validation rules.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

Exercise

Use the message structure, fields and example in this lesson.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Every country that built an instant payment system built its own: its own data, its own rules, its own message usage. The result is fast domestic payments and slow, inconsistent cross border ones, because each bilateral link between two instant systems must reinvent the data mapping, the status reporting, and the exception handling. Two groups exist to fix that, by writing the shared practice that instant systems adopt. IP+, the Instant Payments Plus group, and the RTPG, the ISO 20022 Real-Time Payments Group, are the harmonisation bodies for instant payments, and their guidelines are the starting specification for any cross border instant link.

IP+, also called Cross-Border Instant Payments Plus, is a market practice group relaunched by the Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures, the CPMI. Its task is to develop global ISO 20022 market practice guidelines for fast and cross border payments, based on the harmonised data requirements published to the G20 in October 2023 and maintained until the end of 2027. The IP+ usage guidelines cover the core instant payment messages: pacs.008 for the credit transfer, pacs.004 for the return, pacs.002 for the status report, and pacs.028 for the status request. The exceptions and investigations messages, including camt.110, are not yet in the IP+ portfolio, and their addition is future work. The guidelines narrow the base ISO 20022 standard to the usage the instant payments community agrees on.

The RTPG, the ISO 20022 Real-Time Payments Group, was formed in 2015 and brings together more than fifty organisations: payment systems, banks, vendors, and standards bodies. Its product is a best practice guidebook for using ISO 20022 in real time payments, with an interoperability focus. The guidebook helps new instant systems adopt ISO 20022 consistently, so that a system built in one country can interoperate with a system built in another without bilateral negotiation over every data element. Where IP+ writes usage guidelines for specific messages, the RTPG writes the broader practice: how to design, implement, and operate a real time system on ISO 20022.

The two groups sit in a family of market practice groups that apply the same pattern to different domains. CBPR+ writes the usage guidelines for cross border payments and reporting. HVPS+ writes them for high value payment systems. IP+ writes them for instant payments. The pattern is consistent: a community agrees how it will use the standard, publishes usage guidelines, and members implement the guidelines rather than the bare standard. The family matters because a payment can travel across domains: an instant payment that crosses a border touches IP+ and CBPR+ practice, and a high value payment that settles instantly touches HVPS+ and IP+ practice. The guidelines are designed to align, so the handover between domains does not break the data.

Harmonisation pays off in the links between systems. Without shared guidelines, each link between two instant systems is a bespoke integration: the data mapping, the status codes, the timeout handling, and the exception flows must all be negotiated. With shared guidelines, the link starts from a common specification, and the negotiation covers only the genuinely local differences. The G20 targets for cross border payments, faster, cheaper, more transparent, and more accessible, depend on this harmonisation: the targets cannot be met by bilateral deals alone, at the scale of the global payment network.

For a business analyst, the working rule is to start from the guidelines. An instant payment implementation, a cross border link, or a new instant system should name the IP+ usage guidelines and the RTPG guidebook in its requirements, with versions. Test cases should come from the guideline examples. Counterparty discussions should start from the shared practice, not from each side local habit. The groups exist so that analysts do not have to invent instant payment practice alone, and the guidelines are the closest thing the instant payments world has to a common language.
