Key terms

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Repair: Manual correction of a payment that failed straight through processing because of missing or invalid data.

Retry: Repeating a failed request or message delivery after a delay, usually with a limit on attempts and a back off period.

Exercise

Work out the net settlement position for the clearing cycle described in this lesson.

Use the clearing rules and the netting logic in this lesson.

  1. What to do. List the payments in the cycle, calculate the net position for each bank, and state what settles.

Correct result. Clearing validates, sorts, matches and nets the instructions, then produces a net position per bank. Only the net position settles, which needs far less liquidity than settling every payment individually.

Why. Netting is what makes high volume payments possible. Without it, every payment would need full liquidity at the moment it is sent.

In physical clearing, the paper cheque itself travelled from the collecting bank to a clearing centre and then to the paying bank, which checked the signature and the paper before paying. Vans carried sacks of cheques between banks every night, and the journey took days, which is why a cheque deposited on Monday might not clear until the following Monday. Physical clearing was slow, expensive and vulnerable to lost or damaged paper, and a cheque that was torn or stained could be rejected even when the account held enough funds. Most countries have now replaced physical clearing with image-based clearing, although some still move paper for high value or disputed cheques. Image-based truncation means the collecting bank captures a digital image of the cheque at the point of deposit and then destroys or archives the paper, so only the image travels through the clearing system. Truncation happens when the payee deposits the cheque at a branch scanner, an ATM or a mobile app, and the image must show the front and the back clearly, including the amount, the signature and the magnetic ink line. The image and the data are sent to the paying bank, which checks the image exactly as it would check the paper, and the clearing system settles on the basis of the image. Truncation removes the van journey, which is the main reason clearing times fell so sharply. In the United Kingdom, the Image Clearing System introduced in 2019 cut the clearing time to a maximum of two working days, and most cheques now clear by the next working day. The GBP 4,750 cheque deposited by the supplier on Monday is captured as an image, sent to the paying bank on Monday evening, checked on Tuesday morning, and the supplier account is credited on Tuesday, with the funds available to spend. The old paper timeline took six working days, with two days for the cheque to arrive, two days for interest to start and two days before the money could be withdrawn, which left suppliers waiting a full week. The new timeline matters for cash flow, because a small business that is paid by cheque can now plan around two days instead of six. Image quality is a control, not a detail, because a poor image can stop a cheque from clearing. The image must be sharp, complete and free of shadows, and the magnetic ink characters must be readable, because the paying bank uses them to identify the account. If the image is too dark, cropped or blurred, the collecting bank must ask the payee to deposit the cheque again, which adds a day to the timeline. A business analyst should define the image quality rules, the retry flow and the customer message when an image fails, and a product owner should consider whether the mobile app guides the customer to take a good photograph, because most image failures come from a bad photograph rather than from a bad cheque. Testing must cover good images, poor images and duplicate deposits of the same cheque.

Worked example with numbers: the GBP 4,750 cheque deposited by the supplier on Monday is captured as an image, sent through the Image Clearing System, and the paying bank checks the image and the magnetic ink characters, then honours the cheque, so the supplier account is credited by Tuesday, and the funds are available by Wednesday at the latest. Before image clearing, the paper cheque travelled by van to a clearing centre and then to the paying bank, which took up to six working days, so the supplier waited a week for money that now arrives in two days. The saving is five working days, and it matters most to small businesses that pay suppliers by cheque and cannot wait a week for the funds.

Failure and recovery: image clearing fails when the image is poor, because a poor image can stop a cheque from clearing. The image must be sharp, complete and free of shadows, and the magnetic ink characters must be readable, because the paying bank uses them to identify the account. If the image is blurred, the paying bank rejects the cheque, and the collecting bank must ask the payee to deposit the paper cheque again, which costs a day. If the magnetic ink characters cannot be read, the paying bank cannot identify the account, and the cheque goes to a repair queue. The recovery is a new deposit with a better image, and the collecting bank must check the image quality at the point of deposit, not after the cheque has been rejected.

For a business analyst, product owner or subject matter expert, the clearing requirement must state the two models, physical clearing and image-based truncation, and must state the timeline for each, up to six working days for physical and a maximum of two working days for image clearing in the United Kingdom. The requirement must also state the image quality control, sharp, complete and free of shadows, with readable magnetic ink characters, because that control decides whether the cheque clears. The test pack must include a clear image, a blurred image, and an image with unreadable magnetic ink characters, because each case exercises a different part of the control.
