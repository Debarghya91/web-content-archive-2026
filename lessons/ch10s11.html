Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Gateway: A service that connects a merchant website or app to payment networks and acquirers. It encrypts card data, routes authorisation requests, and returns the result to the merchant.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Phixius is a payment information network run by Nacha, the organisation that governs the ACH network in the United States. It moves information about payments, not money, and its purpose is to let a bank check the facts about an account or a payment before an ACH entry is sent. Requesters connect through one standard application programming interface gateway under one Nacha contract, and the network links each request to the data responders that can answer it. Phixius holds no central store of account data, and responders answer from their own records, so the information stays with the institution that holds it. The service was built to support ACH participants with account validation and risk reduction, and it sits alongside the ACH network rather than inside it.

The problem Phixius addresses comes from the way ACH entries are addressed. An entry carries a routing number and an account number, but those numbers alone cannot tell the sending bank whether the account is open, whether it belongs to the person named, or whether it is the type of account claimed. A wrong number, a closed account or a mismatched name produces a return, costing both banks time and money and forcing the originator to start again. Fraud adds a sharper risk, because stolen account details can be used to set up debits that the account holder never authorised. Nacha rules also require originators to validate account information for certain internet initiated debits, so validation is a compliance need as well as an operational one.

The information exchanged through Phixius centres on account validation and related risk checks. A requester can ask whether an account exists and is open, whether the account type matches what was claimed, and whether the name supplied matches the name on the account. Answers come from the institution holding the account or from specialist data providers that respond on the network, and the exact fields returned depend on the responder and the permissions agreed for the request. Responses arrive in near real time, so validation can happen while the originator is still setting up the payment. The network also supports other risk based payment services through the same gateway, but account validation remains the core use.

Follow a validation from start to finish to see where Phixius fits in the payment journey. An originator gives its bank the receiver's account and routing numbers, for example when setting up a new supplier for electronic payment. The bank, or a processor acting for the bank, sends a validation request through Phixius, which routes the request to the responder that holds the account data. The responder checks its records and returns an answer through the same gateway, and the bank decides what to do next: send the ACH entry, correct the details and ask again, or hold the payment and contact the originator. Only when the details check out does the ACH entry travel through the normal operator channels to a later settlement cycle.

The network design is what makes the service practical across a system with thousands of institutions. A bank connects once, directly or through a service provider, and reaches every responder without a separate connection to each one. There is no requirement to share its own data in return, and end users need not register, which removes two barriers that slow adoption of shared services. The value of the network grows as more responders join, because a validation service can only answer questions about accounts its responders can see. Coverage is therefore the limit an analyst should check first: where no responder covers an account, the bank must fall back on other validation methods, such as a prenote or a small test transaction.

For a payments professional, Phixius matters because it attacks returns and fraud before money moves, when both are cheapest to prevent. Every avoided return saves exception handling, customer contact and rework, and every blocked fraudulent debit saves a loss and a dispute. Validation also improves the customer experience, because a payment set up with checked details is far more likely to arrive on the date promised. Validation has limits: it checks the account at a point in time, an account can close or change afterwards, and a validated account can still lack funds on the settlement date. Phixius does not replace ACH operators, settlement services or fraud controls; its narrower job is to give the sending bank a trusted answer before it sends the payment.
