Key terms

Intraday liquidity: Cash available to a bank during the business day to settle payments as they fall due. Intraday liquidity comes from account balances, incoming payments and central bank facilities.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Show transcript

The Basel III framework added two liquidity ratios that every bank must live with: the Liquidity Coverage Ratio, the LCR, and the Net Stable Funding Ratio, the NSFR. The LCR guards the short term: can the bank survive thirty days of stress? The NSFR guards the long term: is the bank funding long term assets with stable funding, rather than with overnight borrowing that can disappear? Both ratios must be at least one hundred percent. Together they force banks to hold liquid assets and stable funding in proportion to the risks they run, and payment operations supply much of the data that proves it.

The LCR is the thirty day test. Its numerator is high quality liquid assets, HQLA: cash, central bank reserves, and high quality government bonds, with haircuts applied to reflect what they would actually realise in a stress sale. Its denominator is net cash outflows over the next thirty calendar days under a stress scenario: expected outflows minus expected inflows, with inflow caps. The ratio must be at least one hundred percent, meaning the bank holds enough liquid assets to cover a month of stressed outflows without central bank support. The stress assumptions are prescribed: deposit run off rates, wholesale funding withdrawal, and drawdowns on committed facilities.

The NSFR is the one year test. Its numerator is available stable funding: capital, long term debt, and stable deposits, each weighted by a factor reflecting how stable the funding is. Its denominator is required stable funding: the bank assets and off balance sheet exposures, each weighted by how much stable funding they require, reflecting how hard they are to liquidate or how long they are locked up. The ratio must be at least one hundred percent. A bank funding thirty year mortgages with overnight wholesale borrowing fails the NSFR, however profitable the trade, because the funding can vanish while the assets cannot be sold.

What counts in each ratio is defined in detail, and the definitions drive data requirements. HQLA is tiered: the highest quality assets count in full, lower tiers count with haircuts and caps. Stable funding factors reward longer maturities and stickier deposits: retail deposits are more stable than wholesale funding, and term funding is more stable than overnight. Required stable funding factors penalise illiquid assets: unencumbered high quality securities need little stable funding, while long term loans need much. The bank must classify every balance sheet item under these definitions, consistently, every reporting period.

Payment operations feed both ratios. Intraday liquidity, nostro balances, and payment flows shape the outflow profile that the LCR stress scenario models: a bank that cannot fund its payment outflows intraday strains its liquidity buffer, and the buffer is the HQLA that the LCR counts. Payment timing data, when outflows leave and when inflows arrive, informs the stress assumptions. Settlement obligations, committed facilities, and contingent outflows from payment services all enter the calculations. A payment system that cannot report its flows by time, currency, and counterparty cannot feed the ratios.

For a business analyst, the ratios are a data and reporting project. The requirements cover data sourcing: positions, outflows, inflows, HQLA holdings, and funding, from the core banking, treasury, and payment systems. Calculation: applying the prescribed factors, haircuts, and caps, with a full audit trail. Stress assumptions: documenting which scenario applies and why. And regulatory returns: producing the reports in the format and frequency the supervisor requires. The ratios turn liquidity from a treasury concern into a systems concern, and the analyst who understands both the regulation and the data is the one who can build the reporting that proves compliance.
