What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain validation, screening and fraud checks. Once a payment instruction is accepted, it enters a stage of validation, screening, and fraud checking that is invisible to most customers but critical to the safety of the system.

Why it matters. For a business analyst, this stage requires careful requirements.

Chapter objective: Learner walks a payment from initiation through validation, screening, clearing, settlement, and reconciliation, and identifies the controls and failure points at each stage of the lifecycle. Learner applies this skill in realistic banking scenarios, with clear examples and correct terminology throughout.

Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

Creditor: The party that is owed money and receives a payment, such as a supplier, a seller or a service provider.

Exercise

Decide what the screening or monitoring outcome should be for the scenario in this lesson.

Use the lists, rules and thresholds in this lesson.

  1. What to do. State whether the payment is stopped, held, frozen or released, and what must be recorded for audit.

Correct result. A true match against a sanctions list stops the payment. A false positive is cleared with a reason recorded. An alert that is escalated becomes a case, and a case that meets the threshold becomes a suspicious activity report.

Why. Screening is a control, not a suggestion. A missed true match is a regulatory breach, and an unexplained clearance is an audit finding.

Show transcript

Once a payment instruction is accepted, it enters a stage of validation, screening, and fraud checking that is invisible to most customers but critical to the safety of the system. The debtor agent screens the debtor, the creditor, and any intermediary parties against sanctions lists, politically exposed person lists, and internal watchlists. It checks the payment against anti money laundering rules, such as whether the amount is unusual for that customer, whether the destination is high risk, and whether the purpose is consistent with the customer profile. It also applies fraud rules, such as whether the payment is to a new beneficiary, whether it was initiated from an unusual device or location, and whether it matches a known fraud pattern. If a payment passes these checks, it moves forward immediately. If it matches a sanctions name or triggers a fraud alert, it is held for manual review by a compliance or fraud analyst, who must decide whether to release it, request more information, or reject it. A false positive, where a legitimate payment is held because a name is similar to a sanctioned person, is a common cause of delay in cross border payments, especially where names are transliterated from another script. The review process must be fast enough to meet cut off times but thorough enough to meet regulatory expectations, and that tension is a permanent feature of payment operations. For a business analyst, this stage requires careful requirements. You must document which lists are screened, at what point in the flow, what happens on a match, who can release a held payment, and how the customer is informed. You must also understand the difference between a hard block, where the payment must be rejected, and a soft hold, where it can be released after review. A payment that is held without a clear reason or without a service level for review will generate complaints and may breach scheme timelines. Clear, testable requirements for screening and for hold and release are therefore essential. Worked example with real numbers: a EUR 75,000 payment from a corporate to a new supplier in a high-risk jurisdiction is submitted at 09:00. Sanctions screening matches the supplier name against a list entry with a fuzzy score of 92 percent, so the payment is held at 09:02 for manual review. The compliance analyst reviews the match at 10:30, finds it is a false positive, because the supplier is a different company with a similar name in a different country, and releases the payment at 10:45. The hold cost 1 hour and 45 minutes, and the customer communication is the difference between a complaint and an understanding. Failure and recovery: a validation failure, such as a bad IBAN, is rejected immediately with a pain.002 and reason BE01, and the customer corrects and resubmits. A fraud check that scores the payment as high risk, because the amount is 10 times the customer normal payment, holds the payment for the fraud team, and if the team does not clear it by the cut-off, the payment is carried to the next day. A screening system that is down, because the list update failed, must fail closed, holding payments, not fail open, sending them unscreened, because sending an unscreened payment is a regulatory breach. Each check has a different owner, a different SLA and a different customer message. For a business analyst, product owner or subject matter expert, the test is to specify, for every check, when it runs, pre-debit or post-debit, what list or model it uses, what score or match triggers a hold, who reviews the hold, what the SLA is, and what the customer is told. The ISO 20022 pacs.002 carries the status, with RJCT for a rejection and ACTC for an accepted payment that is still being processed, and the requirement must map each check outcome to a status and a customer message. A requirement that says the payment is screened, without naming the timing, the list, the threshold and the SLA, will be interpreted differently by compliance, operations and the vendor, and the product will fail its first screening test.
