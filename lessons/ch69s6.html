Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain money and payment instruments. Every payment instrument is a way of moving a particular form of money, and the instrument decides the speed, the finality, the data and the risk.

Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Every payment instrument is a way of moving a particular form of money, and the instrument decides the speed, the finality, the data and the risk. Cash moves physical fiat money and settles on handover, with no message and no ledger entry. A credit transfer moves commercial bank money by a message, such as a pacs.008, and settles through the interbank system. A direct debit pulls commercial bank money under a mandate. E-money moves a claim on an electronic money institution through a wallet. A cheque moves commercial bank money by a paper instruction that must be cleared. Source: Bank for International Settlements, Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures; European Payments Council, SEPA rulebooks.

The parties change with the instrument, and the change decides who bears the risk when the payment fails. For a credit transfer the parties are the debtor, the debtor agent, the creditor agent and the creditor, and the debtor bears the risk of sending to the wrong account. For a direct debit the creditor initiates the pull, and the debtor bears the risk of an unauthorised collection, which is why mandates and refund rights exist. For a cheque the parties are the drawer, the drawee bank and the payee, and the payee bears the risk that the cheque bounces. A requirement must name the parties for the chosen instrument, because the failure handling follows the parties, not the technology.

A worked example compares three instruments for the same EUR 10,000 supplier payment. By SEPA Credit Transfer the manufacturer sends a pain.001, the bank sends a pacs.008, the supplier is credited the same day or the next day, and the supplier receives the full EUR 10,000 with remittance data that matches the invoice. By cheque the supplier would wait days for clearing, bear the risk of a bounce, and reconcile from a paper stub. The amount and the money are the same in all three cases, but the instrument changes the cost, the timing, the data and the risk, and the manufacturer chooses the credit transfer because it fits a high value supplier payment.

Failure is instrument specific, and a generic failure playbook will miss it. A credit transfer fails when the IBAN is wrong, the account is closed, or the payment is held by sanctions screening, and the return comes as a pacs.004 with a reason code such as AC04 for a closed account. A card payment fails when the authorisation is declined, the card is expired, or the merchant does not capture, and the failure is an ISO 8583 response code, not a pacs.002. A direct debit fails when the mandate is missing or the debtor disputes it, and the refund window under the SEPA Direct Debit scheme is 8 weeks for an authorised collection and 13 months for an unauthorised one. Each instrument needs its own failure and recovery section in the product specification.

The effect on the bank, the customer and operations is that the instrument decides the whole operating model. For the customer, the instrument decides how the payment is initiated, how long it takes, what it costs and what happens when it goes wrong. For operations, the instrument decides the exception queue, the reason codes, the return timelines and the reconciliation source: a pacs.008 reconciles against the clearing file, a card payment reconciles against the scheme clearing file, and a cheque reconciles against the paper clearing. A hub that treats all instruments as the same payment will route correctly and reconcile badly, and the breaks will appear at the first month end.

For a business analyst, product owner or subject matter expert, the instrument choice is a requirements decision, not a technical default. Ask what money is being moved, who initiates the movement, how fast it must arrive, how much data must travel with it, what it costs each party, and what the failure and refund rights are. Document the answer as a comparison table, with one row per instrument and columns for money type, initiator, timing, cost, data, finality and failure. The table is the evidence that the choice was made deliberately, and it is the first artefact a reviewer will ask for. The ISO 20022 catalogue, the card scheme rules and the SEPA rulebooks are the sources for the message and timing columns, and they must be cited with version and effective date.

Two instruments deserve a warning because they are often misdescribed. The first is the instant payment, which is not a separate instrument but a faster execution of a credit transfer, and its money is still commercial bank money settled in central bank money, only faster. The second is the stablecoin payment, which moves a claim on a token issuer rather than commercial bank money, and whose settlement asset and redemption right must be stated explicitly, because the customer may assume it is bank money when it is not. A product that calls a stablecoin transfer a bank transfer has misdescribed the money, and the misdescription will surface in the customer terms, the safeguarding and the first complaint.

Money and instruments also explain why new instruments keep appearing and why most of them fail. A new instrument succeeds when it moves an existing form of money faster, cheaper or with richer data, as the SEPA Instant payment did for euro credit transfers. It fails when it invents a new money without an issuer, a settlement asset or a legal basis, and the holders discover the gap only when they try to redeem. The business analyst test is therefore unchanged by fashion: name the money, name the issuer, name the settlement asset, name the instrument, and prove the failure handling with a worked example. If any of those five cannot be named, the product is not ready.

Takeaway: the instrument is the vehicle and the money is the cargo, and the choice of vehicle changes cost, timing, data, finality and failure. Cash, credit transfer, direct debit, card, cheque and e-money each move a different form of money through different parties and different messages. Choose the instrument by money type, initiator, timing, cost, data and failure rights, and document the comparison. Further reading: Bank for International Settlements, Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures, A glossary of terms used in payments and settlement systems; European Payments Council, SEPA Credit Transfer Rulebook 2025, version 1.1, effective 5 October 2025. Last reviewed 7 November 2026.
