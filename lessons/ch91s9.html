Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Interchange: A fee paid by the acquirer to the issuer for each card transaction, set by the card scheme. It compensates the issuer for handling the transaction, funding the interest free period and managing fraud...

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. State what happens at each stage, who pays whom, and when the merchant is actually credited.

Correct result. Authorisation holds the funds and returns a response code. Clearing sends the transaction in a batch file. Settlement moves the net amount between banks, and the acquirer credits the merchant after deducting interchange, scheme fees and its margin.

Why. A card payment is not one event. The customer sees it on Monday, the merchant gets it on Wednesday, and the difference is the clearing and settlement cycle.

Show transcript

A cash machine in the United Kingdom will usually accept the card of any UK bank, even though the machine belongs to a different bank or to an independent operator. The network that makes that possible is LINK. LINK is the United Kingdom ATM network: it connects cash machines and card issuers, so that a customer can use any LINK machine with any LINK card. LINK is shared infrastructure, owned by its members, and it is the reason cash access in the United Kingdom is universal rather than fragmented by bank.

A LINK transaction follows a simple route. The customer inserts a card at a cash machine and requests a withdrawal. The machine sends the authorisation request through LINK to the card issuing bank, which checks the account, the balance, and the card status, and authorises or declines. The machine dispenses the cash, and the transaction is recorded for settlement between the issuer and the machine operator. The customer sees one transaction; behind it, the network has routed the request, the issuer has authorised it, and the settlement has been arranged. The routing is invisible, and that invisibility is the service.

The economics run on interchange. The card issuer pays the machine operator an interchange fee for each withdrawal, compensating the operator for the cost of the machine, the cash, and the site. The fee level is set by LINK, and it is the subject of long running debate: operators argue the fee must cover costs, issuers argue it must reflect efficiency, and reductions in the fee have led to machine closures, particularly in areas where machines process few transactions. The interchange model is how a shared network funds itself: the users of the service, through their banks, pay for the infrastructure they use.

Access to cash is LINK most visible public role. LINK runs a financial inclusion programme that subsidises cash machines in remote, rural, and deprived areas, where transaction volumes would not support a machine commercially. The programme pays a premium interchange rate for machines in areas with poor cash access, keeping free machines where the community needs them. The access debate, how many machines, where, and who pays, is a live policy question in the United Kingdom, and LINK sits at its centre, balancing commercial viability against universal access.

LINK is the domestic ATM scheme, and it sits alongside the international card schemes. A withdrawal may travel via LINK or via the card scheme, Visa or Mastercard, depending on the card, the machine, and the routing rules. The two routes coexist: LINK for domestic UK cards and machines, the card schemes for international cards and for transactions where the scheme route applies. The coexistence is a reminder that payment networks overlap: the same machine, the same card, and the same withdrawal can travel by more than one route, and the routing decision is made by rules the customer never sees.

For a business analyst, LINK teaches the economics of shared infrastructure. The network is valuable because everyone connects to it, and it is funded by interchange that everyone pays. The access programme shows how a shared network can serve a public purpose, subsidising provision where the market alone would not. And the interchange debate shows the tension at the heart of every shared network: the cost of the infrastructure, the price of using it, and the access it provides must balance, or the network shrinks. Shared infrastructure is a design choice with economic consequences, and LINK is the United Kingdom working example.
