The core banking ledger, the chart of accounts and double entry

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain the core banking ledger, the chart of accounts and double entry and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Double entry is the control that makes every payment provable, and the analyst must know the entries.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how a bank's ledgers record every payment, distinguishes customer accounts, internal accounts, nostro and vostro ledgers, suspense and transit accounts, writes the exact general ledger postings for outgoing and incoming payments, and explains intraday processing, value dating, subledger feeds, reconciliation and error correction, with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Payment: journal entry in ledger
  • Chart of accounts
  • Either both sides of the entry
  • Day unable to close when the ledgers do not balance
  • Everything downstream
  • Acceptance criteria

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Correct result. Debit the customer account 10,000 pounds and credit the settlement or nostro account 10,000 pounds, so the ledger balances and the payment is traceable.

Why. If the debit and credit do not match, the trial balance fails and the payment is stuck, so the posting must be atomic.

Transcript and full text

Every payment a bank makes is a journal entry in a ledger, and every journal entry has two sides. A debit takes value from one account and a credit gives value to another, and the two sides are always equal. This is double entry bookkeeping, and it is the reason a bank can prove that no money has appeared from nowhere and none has vanished. When a customer pays 1,000 pounds to another customer at the same bank, the bank debits the payer's current account by 1,000 pounds and credits the payee's current account by 1,000 pounds. The bank's balance sheet does not change in total; the money simply moves from one liability to another, because a customer deposit is a liability of the bank, money the bank owes the customer. The chart of accounts is the map of every account the bank holds in its general ledger. It is organised into five families: assets, liabilities, equity, income and expense. Customer deposits sit in liabilities. Loans to customers sit in assets. Cash held at the central bank sits in assets. Nostro accounts, the bank's own accounts with other banks, sit in assets. Fee income sits in income, and interest paid to depositors sits in expense. Every product a bank sells must have its accounts defined in the chart before it can go live, and finance must approve the mapping, because a payment posted to the wrong account produces a wrong balance sheet and a wrong regulatory return. A payment becomes a debit and a credit at the moment it is posted, and posting is atomic. Either both sides of the entry are written or neither is, so the ledger can never be left half updated by a system crash. When a customer sends 1,000 pounds out through CHAPS, the core banking system debits the customer's current account by 1,000 pounds and credits the bank's settlement account at the Bank of England by 1,000 pounds. The settlement account is an asset, and crediting it reduces it, because the bank's reserves at the central bank have fallen by the amount paid out. When a CHAPS payment arrives, the entries reverse: the settlement account is debited and the customer's account is credited. The trial balance is the daily proof that the ledger is intact. At the end of each day the system adds up all debits and all credits across every account, and the two totals must be exactly equal. If they are not, something is wrong and the day cannot close. A difference of even one penny stops the close, because a difference means an entry was lost, duplicated, or posted to the wrong side. This is why ledger systems are built to be boring and exact rather than clever. They do one thing, record value moving between accounts, and they do it with a guarantee that the books always balance. The ledger is the system of record, and everything else is derived from it. The balance a customer sees in their app comes from the ledger. The statement comes from the ledger. The interest calculation reads the ledger. The regulatory returns, the liquidity reports and the financial statements are all built from the ledger. If the ledger is wrong, everything downstream is wrong, which is why banks protect the ledger with the strictest controls they have: maker-checker approval, append-only audit trails, and daily reconciliation. A payments business analyst who understands the ledger understands why a payment cannot simply be edited when it goes wrong, and why every fix is a new entry that references the old one. For a business analyst, the ledger is the first thing to map when a new payment product is designed. Every payment flow must be drawn as a set of journal entries before a line of code is written, and the entries must be reviewed by finance. The map must show the debit and the credit for each step, the account each side posts to, the value date, and what happens when the payment fails halfway. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment is made and the trial balance still balances, and a test where a payment fails and no partial entry remains. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: the Bank of England guidance on RTGS and settlement accounts; the International Accounting Standards Board framework on double entry and the chart of accounts; the Basel Committee principles for the management of operational risk in payment systems.
