What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain sanctions screening and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Sanctions screening is where a payment system meets criminal law, and a wrong release can cost the bank its licence.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the regulatory framework that binds payments in Europe, the UK, the US and India, distinguishes PSD2 from PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation, and applies DORA, the Instant Payments Regulation, sanctions regimes, AML rules, RBI directions and FFIEC guidance to realistic payment scenarios with correct terminology.

Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Correct result. The screening engine flags the payment, a sanctions analyst reviews the match, and if it is a true match the payment is blocked, the funds frozen and OFAC notified; if it is a false positive it is released with the reason recorded.

Why. The difference between a true match and a false positive is decided by a human analyst, and the decision and the evidence must be recorded.

Show transcript

Sanctions screening is the control that decides whether a payment can legally be made, and it is the control with the harshest consequences for getting it wrong. Three regimes dominate. The United States Office of Foreign Assets Control, OFAC, enforces US sanctions with strict liability, which means a bank can be penalised even if it did not intend to breach. The European Union imposes sanctions through Common Foreign and Security Policy decisions, implemented by member states, with the UK enforcing its own regime through the Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation. The United Nations Security Council imposes sanctions that all member states must implement. A single payment can be caught by all three, and the bank must comply with each one. Screening works by matching names and identifiers against lists. The OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list, the EU consolidated list, and the UN list together contain hundreds of thousands of entries, and the bank must screen against all of them. Matching is not exact string comparison. It is fuzzy matching that handles transliteration, name order, spelling variants, and initials, because a sanctioned person will not spell their name the way the list does. A screening engine typically scores each match from 0 to 100 and holds the payment when the score crosses a threshold. The threshold is a policy decision: set it too low and operations drowns in false positives; set it too high and a true match slips through. The threshold must be tested with seeded names, including known true matches and known near misses, and the test results must be documented. What is screened is wider than most people expect. The payer name, the payee name, and the names of any intermediary banks are screened. The remittance information is screened, because it can name a sanctioned vessel, a sanctioned port, or a sanctioned goods description. In trade finance the screening covers the goods, the ports, the vessels and the parties to the underlying transaction. A payment whose remittance field says freight to a sanctioned port is a sanctions problem even when the payer and the payee are clean. The screening must also cover the customer base itself, not just the payments, because a customer who becomes sanctioned after onboarding must be caught the day the list changes, not the day they next make a payment. When a payment hits, the process is hold, investigate, decide. The payment is held, not rejected, while the investigation runs, because a false positive must be released and a true match must be blocked. The investigator checks the match against the customer record, the account documentation, and the payment details, and records the reasoning. A true match against a blocked person means the payment is blocked and the funds are frozen, and the bank must report the blocking to the relevant authority. A true match against a person who is not blocked but is on a watchlist means the payment is rejected and the customer is told. A false positive is released, and the release decision is logged with the name of the person who made it. No payment is released on a single person's say-so when the score is high, and the four-eyes rule applies. The OFAC 50 Percent Rule is the detail that catches firms out. Under the rule, an entity is blocked if blocked persons own 50 percent or more of it in aggregate, even if no single blocked person owns 50 percent. The arithmetic matters: if three blocked persons each own 20 percent, the entity is 60 percent blocked-owned and is itself blocked, even though none of them individually crosses the threshold. The bank must therefore know the ownership of its corporate customers, including indirect ownership through holding companies, and must recalculate when ownership changes. A corporate customer that was clean at onboarding can become blocked when a sanctioned person buys a stake, and the bank must catch that through ongoing monitoring, not at the next periodic review. The extraterritorial reach of US sanctions is why a European bank cares about OFAC even when it has no US presence. Any payment in US dollars clears through a US correspondent bank, and that correspondent applies OFAC screening. A European bank that sends a dollar payment to a sanctioned person will have the payment blocked by its correspondent, and the correspondent will report it. Secondary sanctions go further: the US can penalise a non-US bank for dealing with a sanctioned person even when the transaction has no US nexus. The practical consequence is that most international banks apply OFAC screening to all payments in all currencies, because it is cheaper than maintaining separate screening policies per currency and because a single miss can cost the correspondent relationship. For a business analyst, sanctions screening is a set of precise system requirements. The screening engine must screen payer, payee, intermediaries and remittance information, must update its lists within an agreed time after publication, must log every screening decision with the score and the list version, and must support the hold, investigate and release workflow with four-eyes control. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a known true match is blocked and reported, a test where a known false positive is released with a logged reason, a test where a list update is applied within the agreed time, and a test where the 50 Percent Rule calculation is correct for an aggregated ownership case. The evidence for each test must be kept, because a supervisor will ask for it after an enforcement action, not before. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list and 50 Percent Rule guidance; Council Regulation (EU) on restrictive measures; the UK Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation guidance; the Wolfsberg Group payment transparency standards.
