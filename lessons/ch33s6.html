FEMA is the Indian law that regulates foreign exchange transactions, and its core principle is that foreign exchange can only be dealt with through authorised persons, such as banks licensed as authorised dealers, and only for permitted purposes. The Act replaced the earlier FERA, which was more restrictive, and it introduced a framework where current account transactions, such as trade payments, travel and education, are generally permitted, while capital account transactions, such as investments abroad, are subject to limits and conditions. A BA working on a cross-border payments project must know which category a transaction falls into, because the documentation, the approval and the reporting differ. The BA should never design a flow that allows a customer to send foreign exchange for a purpose that FEMA does not permit. FEMA affects cross-border payment projects in concrete ways that must appear in the requirements. Every outward remittance must capture the purpose code, the beneficiary details, the source of funds, and the declaration that the customer has made under FEMA, and the system must validate the purpose against the permitted list before the payment is released. The system must also check the customer limits, screen the beneficiary and the destination country against sanctions lists, and store the declaration and the supporting documents for audit. A BA should work with the compliance team to produce a purpose code matrix that maps each purpose, such as education, medical treatment or family maintenance, to the documents required, the limit that applies, and the approval workflow. Without that matrix, the development team will build a generic remittance screen that compliance cannot approve. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme, known as LRS, is the framework that allows a resident Indian individual to remit up to 250,000 US dollars per financial year for permitted current and capital account transactions. The limit is per person per financial year, and it covers purposes such as travel, education, medical treatment, gifts, maintenance of relatives abroad, and investment in foreign shares and property. The system must track the cumulative remittances of each customer across the financial year and block any remittance that would exceed the limit, and it must report the remittances to the Reserve Bank of India. A BA must specify how the cumulative total is calculated, how it handles remittances made through other banks, and what the customer sees when the limit is reached. A customer who has already remitted 240,000 dollars cannot remit a further 20,000 dollars in the same financial year. The documentation a BA must capture for a FEMA compliant project starts with the customer declaration, usually in the form of an A2 form or an online equivalent, where the customer states the purpose, the amount and the beneficiary, and declares that the remittance is within the LRS limit and for a permitted purpose. The BA must also capture the PAN of the remitter, the supporting documents such as a university admission letter for education or a hospital estimate for medical treatment, and the tax collected at source where applicable. The system must store these documents with the transaction, link them to the purpose code, and retain them for the period required by the regulator. The requirements should also define the maker checker process, one user enters the remittance, another approves it, and the system records both identities with timestamps. FEMA and RBI regulations are related but not identical, and a BA should be able to explain the difference in a meeting. FEMA is the Act passed by Parliament that sets the legal framework for foreign exchange, while the RBI issues the regulations, directions and master directions that implement FEMA in practice, such as the Master Direction on Liberalised Remittance Scheme and the Master Direction on Export and Import of Goods and Services. In other words, FEMA says what is permitted, and the RBI directions say how it must be done, what forms to use, and what reports to file. A BA should cite the specific RBI Master Direction in the requirements document, include the version and date, and maintain a change log so that when the RBI updates a direction, the impact on the requirements can be assessed quickly and accurately.
