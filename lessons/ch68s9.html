Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Return: A payment that is sent back to the sender after settlement because it cannot be credited to the beneficiary.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

When a payment cannot settle immediately, because funds are insufficient or a limit is reached, it waits in a queue. Queues are where settlement systems manage the gap between instruction and settlement, and queue management decides which payments settle, in what order, and what happens to the ones that never settle. Two queue disciplines matter most: expiry, which sets a lifetime for a queued payment, and re-sequencing, which reorders the queue to maximise settlement. Both are risk controls disguised as plumbing.

Queue disciplines set the default order. First in first out, FIFO, settles payments in the order they arrived: fair, predictable, and sometimes inefficient, because a large payment at the head of the queue can block smaller payments behind it that could settle. Priority based ordering settles urgent payments first, regardless of arrival order, which suits time critical payments but needs a priority discipline to prevent everything being marked urgent. FIFO bypass, used in several real time gross settlement systems, lets a later payment jump the queue if it can settle while the payment ahead of it cannot: the queue is respected unless respecting it wastes a settlement opportunity. The discipline is a system rule, and participants design their submission behaviour around it.

Expiry sets a lifetime. A queued payment that cannot settle by a defined cut off, or after a defined period, expires: it is returned to the sender, rejected with a reason, or carried to the next operating day, depending on the system rules. Expiry prevents stale instructions from settling unexpectedly: a payment queued on Monday that settles on Thursday, after the business need has passed, is worse than a payment returned on Monday. The expiry rules, the cut off times, and the return reasons must be published and predictable, so that participants can manage their queues and their customers expectations.

Re-sequencing reorders the queue to settle more. The classic problem is gridlock: participant A waits for funds from B, B waits for funds from C, and C waits for funds from A, and no payment in the cycle can settle alone. Gridlock resolution finds the cycle and settles the payments simultaneously, by offsetting: the net effect of the cycle is computed, and the payments settle together, each funding the next. Optimisation algorithms run gridlock resolution cycles periodically, searching for sets of queued payments that can settle together. Manual re-sequencing lets operations reorder the queue, promoting a payment that unblocks others, or deferring a payment that blocks the queue.

The interaction between expiry and re-sequencing shapes the operating day. Early in the day, queues are short and re-sequencing settles most payments quickly. As the day progresses and liquidity thins, queues grow, gridlock becomes more likely, and the resolution cycles matter more. Near the cut off, expiry dominates: payments that cannot settle must be returned in time for the sender to act. Operations teams watch queue depth, queue age, and the oldest queued payment, and intervene before expiry forces their hand.

For a business analyst, queue requirements cover the discipline, the expiry rules, the re-sequencing rights, and the notifications. The discipline must be specified: FIFO, priority, bypass, or a combination. Expiry must name the cut offs, the return reasons, and the carry over rules. Re-sequencing must name who may reorder, under what authority, and with what audit trail. And notifications must tell the sender when a payment queues, when it is re-sequenced, and when it expires, because a queued payment is a customer waiting. Queue behaviour shapes liquidity needs, and liquidity needs shape funding: the queue is where settlement design meets treasury reality.
