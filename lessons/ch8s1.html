Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

ISO 20022: An international standard for financial messaging that defines XML message formats for payments, securities and trade.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

Migration from MT to ISO 20022 was needed because MT could no longer carry the rich, structured data that modern payments require, and because domestic schemes around the world were adopting ISO 20022 independently, which would have created fragmentation if cross-border payments had stayed on MT. The migration was therefore run as a community programme, CBPR Plus, with a coexistence period during which both MT and MX would be supported. Coexistence meant that a bank could send a payment as an MT103 or as a pacs.008, and the receiving bank had to be able to accept either format, process it, and, if needed, translate it to the other format for onward transmission. The coexistence period ended on 22 November 2025. From that date, MT messages are no longer accepted for cross-border payments on the SWIFT network. Core messages such as MT103 and MT202 cannot be sent for cross-border payment instructions. All cross-border payment instructions now use ISO 20022 messages, pacs.008 for customer credit transfers, pacs.009 for bank-to-bank transfers, pacs.002 for status reports, pacs.004 for returns, and camt.056 for cancellation requests. Banks that had not completed their migration by the deadline could no longer send cross-border payments on SWIFT until they did. During coexistence, a payment could start as an MX message, be translated to MT for a bank that had not yet migrated, and then be translated back to MX for the next bank that had migrated. Each translation was a point where data could be lost, truncated, or misinterpreted, and each translation added cost and operational risk. That translation risk is now gone for cross-border payments, because every bank in the chain uses the same format. The data that used to be truncated in translation now flows end to end in structured fields. For a business analyst, the end of coexistence changes the work. Your requirements no longer need to state which formats are supported for each flow, because there is only one format for cross-border payments. Your test plan no longer needs to cover MT to MT, MX to MX, MT to MX, and MX to MT flows. Instead, your work focuses on the richness of the ISO 20022 data, making sure structured addresses, remittance information, and party data are populated correctly, and making sure screening, reconciliation, and reporting systems use the new structured fields. MT knowledge is still valuable, because many payment investigations, historical records, and domestic systems still involve MT messages, and because understanding what MT could not carry explains why ISO 20022 was designed the way it was. The timeline ran over several years. SWIFT published the CBPR Plus usage guidelines and set out the migration plan, and ISO 20022 went live for cross border payments in March 2023. From that date, banks could send cross border payments as pacs.008 and pacs.009, and the coexistence period began. During coexistence, both MT and MX were accepted, and banks migrated at their own pace. The coexistence period ended on 22 November 2025. From that date, MT103 and MT202 are no longer accepted for cross border payment instructions on the SWIFT network, and any bank that had not migrated could no longer send cross border payments until it did. Three forces drove the change. The first was data. Compliance teams needed structured names and addresses to screen payments properly, and MT free text made screening slow and unreliable. The second was straight through processing. Richer structured data means fewer payments fall into repair queues, which means lower cost and faster delivery for customers. The third was consistency. Domestic schemes around the world were moving to ISO 20022 on their own timetables, and if cross border payments had stayed on MT, every payment that crossed from a domestic scheme to the cross border network would have needed translation. One shared standard removes that friction. MT did not disappear entirely. Statements still arrive as MT940 and MT950, trade finance still uses MT700 series messages, collections still use MT400 series messages, and some domestic flows still use MT. Investigations also still involve MT, because a payment from before the cutover, or a statement line from last year, will be in MT form. That is why this chapter still matters. A business analyst who can read an MT103 and a pacs.008, and who can explain what changed between them, is far more useful than one who only knows the new format.
