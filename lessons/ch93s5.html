Key terms

Refund: A repayment of funds to a customer after a payment fails, is cancelled or is disputed. In card payments a refund returns money to the cardholder account.

Scheme: A card or payment network that sets the rules, standards and fees for transactions between issuers and acquirers.

IBAN: An international bank account number that identifies a customer account in a standard format across countries.

Verification of Payee is a check that the name the payer typed matches the name of the account holder for the IBAN they typed, and it runs before the payment is sent. It exists because authorised push payment fraud, where a fraudster tricks a payer into sending money to an account the fraudster controls, had become the largest fraud type on credit transfers. Verification of Payee is required by the Instant Payments Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2024/886, and payment service providers in the euro area must offer it from 9 October 2025, for instant payments and for regular credit transfers. It does not check whether the payee is honest. It checks whether the name matches the account. The matching has four outcomes, and the payer must be shown the outcome in plain language. A match means the name and the account agree, and the payment proceeds. A close match means the name is similar but not identical, for example a missing middle name or a small spelling difference, and the payer is shown the account holder name as held by the payee bank so they can decide. A no match means the name does not agree with the account, and the payer is warned clearly before they proceed. The fourth outcome, cannot check, means the payee bank does not offer Verification of Payee, or the account type cannot be checked, and the payer must be told that the check could not be done. The payer can still proceed after a no match in most implementations, but they do so against a warning, and that decision matters for liability. The scope is wider than many people expect. Verification of Payee applies to credit transfers initiated by the payer, including instant payments and regular SCT payments, and it applies to both natural persons and legal persons. For a company payee, the check is against the legal name held by the payee bank, which is often different from the trading name on the invoice, and that mismatch produces close match outcomes that confuse payers. Bulk files are a special case: a corporate uploading a file with a thousand payments needs the check to run on each one, and the file must not be rejected wholesale because one payment produced a no match. The check must also work for accounts held at payment institutions, not just banks, and for accounts in other SEPA countries, which means the matching service must work across borders. The liability shift is the part that changes behaviour. Under the regulation, if a payment service provider fails to perform Verification of Payee when it was required, and the payment goes to the wrong account because of that failure, the provider is liable for the loss and must refund the payer. That is a direct financial consequence for a missing control. The other side of the shift is the payer: if the provider performed the check, warned the payer of a no match, and the payer proceeded anyway, the payer bears the loss, because they were warned and chose to continue. This is why the warning must be clear, must be recorded, and must be shown before the payment is confirmed, not buried in a confirmation screen the payer has already accepted. Verification of Payee reduces fraud but does not eliminate it, and a practitioner must understand its limits. It stops the fraud where the fraudster gives the payer an account number belonging to someone else, because the name will not match. It does not stop the fraud where the fraudster controls an account in their own name, a mule account, because the name matches the mule. It does not stop the fraud where the payer is tricked into paying a genuine supplier account that the fraudster has taken over. It also creates a new operational load: close match outcomes generate calls to the contact centre, and a poorly tuned matching algorithm that produces too many close matches will train payers to ignore the warning, which defeats the purpose. Take a worked example. A company pays a supplier 9,800 euro. The invoice shows the trading name, but the account is held in the legal name, which differs by one word. The check returns a close match and shows the legal name. The accounts payable clerk compares the legal name with the supplier record, confirms it is the same company, and proceeds. The payment settles correctly. Two weeks later the same clerk pays a different supplier 14,200 euro, and the check returns a no match. The clerk calls the supplier, the supplier says the account number on the invoice was typed wrong, and the corrected account returns a match. The payment goes through. In the third case, the clerk ignores a no match warning and pays a fraudster 22,000 euro, and the bank refuses the refund because the warning was shown and recorded. The three cases show the check working, the check preventing an error, and the check being ignored. For a business analyst, Verification of Payee work is a set of user experience and liability requirements. Every requirement should state the four outcomes, the exact wording shown to the payer for each one, the recording of the outcome and the payer decision, and the bulk file handling where one no match must not block the rest. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a match proceeds without friction, a test where a close match shows the held name, a test where a no match warns and records the payer decision, a test where a payment to a bank without Verification of Payee returns cannot check, and a test where a failure to perform the check triggers the provider liability.
