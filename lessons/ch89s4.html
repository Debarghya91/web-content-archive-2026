Show transcript

Liquidity is the cash a bank has available to settle its payments when they fall due, and in a clearing system it is the difference between a payment that settles and a payment that waits. A bank with plenty of liquidity settles everything on time. A bank with too little watches its payments queue, and its customers wait. Intraday liquidity is the liquidity available during the day, as opposed to overnight. A bank starts the day with a balance in its settlement account, receives credits during the day as other banks pay it, and pays debits as its own customers' payments settle. The balance moves up and down all day, and the treasury team watches it in real time. The central bank supports intraday liquidity in two ways: it provides intraday credit against collateral, and it operates the real-time gross settlement system in a way that helps banks use their liquidity efficiently.

In a gross settlement system such as T2, a payment that cannot settle for lack of funds does not fail. It waits in a queue. The queue is ordered, usually first in first out, with a bypass rule: if the payment at the front of the queue cannot settle, a smaller payment behind it can settle first if the funds allow. That rule, called FIFO with bypass, stops one large payment from blocking hundreds of small ones. The payment stays in the queue until funds arrive, either from an incoming payment or from a treasury top-up, or until the end of the day, when it is rejected and returned.

Gridlock is the failure mode that queueing creates. Bank A has a payment queued because it is waiting for money from Bank B. Bank B has a payment queued because it is waiting for money from Bank A. Neither payment can settle, because each bank is waiting for the other. In a system with many banks the gridlock can involve dozens of payments and several banks, and no single bank can break it, because no single bank has enough liquidity to settle all of its queued payments at once. The RTGS system resolves gridlock with an offsetting algorithm. It looks at the queue, finds a set of payments that can settle together because the amounts offset each other, and settles them as a group.

A worked example shows it. Bank A owes Bank B 10 million, and the payment is queued. Bank B owes Bank A 9 million, and that payment is queued. Bank A has 1 million in its account, Bank B has 2 million. Neither payment can settle on its own. The offsetting algorithm sees that the two payments net to 1 million from Bank A to Bank B, settles them together using the 1 million Bank A holds, and both payments complete. The gridlock is broken without any new liquidity. The algorithm runs periodically through the day, and in some systems it runs continuously.

Liquidity-saving mechanisms go further than gridlock resolution. Bilateral offsetting pairs payments between two banks and settles the net amount. Multilateral offsetting does the same across all banks in the queue. Prioritisation lets a bank mark a payment as urgent, so it jumps the queue, but the bank must use that sparingly, because every urgent payment delays the normal ones behind it. Reservation lets a bank set aside liquidity for critical payments, such as a payment to a central counterparty, so that it cannot be used by ordinary payments.

A worked day shows the scale. A large bank sends 5,000 high-value payments a day through T2, worth 8 billion euro in total. Its opening balance is 400 million. During the day it receives 7.6 billion and pays 8 billion, so its net outflow is 400 million, exactly its opening balance. Without queueing and offsetting, the bank would need far more than 400 million, because the payments do not arrive in the same order they are sent. With offsetting, the bank settles the full 8 billion with an average balance of only 200 million. That is the value of liquidity management: it turns a small balance into a large throughput.

Instant payments create a different liquidity problem. TIPS needs prefunded liquidity 24 hours a day, including weekends and holidays when the treasury team is not working and T2 is closed. The bank must forecast the weekend instant payment volume on Friday, move the money to its TIPS account, and accept that the money sits idle if the forecast is too high, or that payments are rejected if the forecast is too low. A bank that processes 50,000 instant payments over a weekend, at an average of 150 euro, needs about 7.5 million euro of prefunding, plus a buffer for a busy weekend. For a business analyst, liquidity is a set of requirements about monitoring, forecasting, queue management, and escalation. The requirements must state the liquidity monitoring dashboard, the alert thresholds, the escalation procedure when the balance falls below the threshold, and the gridlock resolution expectations. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment queues for lack of funds and settles when funds arrive, and a test where a gridlock is resolved by the offsetting algorithm without manual intervention.
