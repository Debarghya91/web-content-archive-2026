What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain foreign exchange in cross-border payments and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. FX is where cross-border payments make or lose money, and the analyst must price it correctly.

Chapter objective: Learner explains cross-border payments in depth, traces correspondent banking chains, applies SWIFT gpi tracking and UETR, explains CBPR+ and the MT to MX migration, distinguishes serial from cover payments, prices FX, optimises nostro liquidity and applies sanctions screening across jurisdictions with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Foreign exchange conversion and conversion
  • Customer sending 10,000 pounds
  • Payment terms stating which FX model applies
  • Hub linking the value date to each currency settlement calendar
  • Real example showing how the numbers work
  • Rate and spread shown before confirmation, not after settlement

Key terms

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Every cross-border payment that changes currency needs a foreign exchange conversion, and the conversion is where much of the cost and much of the risk sits. The payment is instructed in one currency and settled in another, and the conversion can happen at the ordering bank, at an intermediary bank, or at the beneficiary bank. Each point applies a different rate and a different spread, and the customer usually has no visibility into which point was used. A payment instructed in sterling and credited in euros may be converted by the ordering bank at its own rate, or passed through in sterling and converted by the beneficiary bank at its rate, and the two outcomes can differ by hundreds of euros on a large payment. The spread is the bank's margin on the conversion. The bank buys currency at one rate and sells it at another, and the difference is the spread. Take a customer sending 10,000 pounds to a euro account. The mid-market rate is 1.17 euros per pound, so the true value is 11,700 euros. The bank offers 1.15, so the beneficiary receives 11,500 euros. The 200 euro difference is the spread, about 1.7 percent of the payment. On a 1 million pound payment the same spread costs 20,000 euros. The spread is rarely shown as a separate line on the statement, which is why customers often do not realise how much the conversion cost them. The exchange rate risk is the question of who loses when the rate moves between instruction and settlement. If the ordering bank fixes the rate at the moment the customer confirms the payment, the bank bears the risk until it hedges the position in the market. If the rate is applied at settlement, the customer bears it, and the amount the beneficiary receives is not known until the payment settles. The terms of the payment must state which model applies. A corporate treasurer who budgets a payment at a fixed rate and then receives less because the rate moved will escalate, and the first question will be what the terms said. Cut-off times and value dates make the risk concrete. Most currency pairs settle on a spot basis, two business days after the trade, known as T plus 2. A payment instructed on a Friday may not settle until the following Tuesday, and a public holiday in either country pushes it further. The value date in the payment message must match the foreign exchange settlement date. If the payment value date is Monday but the foreign exchange settles on Tuesday, the nostro account goes overdrawn on Monday, and the bank pays overdraft interest or the payment is rejected. The payment hub must therefore link the payment value date to the currency's settlement calendar, including the holidays of both countries. Double conversion is the hidden cost that catches customers out. An intermediary bank may convert a payment even when no conversion was requested, applying its own spread on the way through. A dollar payment routed through a euro correspondent can be converted to euros and back to dollars, losing a spread at each conversion. The customer instructed a dollar payment and expected a dollar credit, but the beneficiary receives less because of two conversions they never asked for. The defence is to instruct the payment with a clear currency and to use correspondents that pass the currency through unchanged, and to check the tracker for unexpected deductions. A real example shows how the numbers work. A UK company pays a US supplier 50,000 dollars from a sterling account. The bank converts at instruction at a rate of 1.27 dollars per pound, so 39,370 pounds is debited from the company's account. The payment is delayed by two days because of a screening hit, and in that time the rate moves to 1.29. At the new rate the 50,000 dollars would cost only 38,760 pounds, so the bank's hedge has lost about 610 pounds. The bank absorbs the loss because the rate was fixed at instruction. If the rate had been applied at settlement, the customer would have saved 610 pounds, but they would also have carried the risk of the rate moving the other way. For a business analyst, foreign exchange handling is a set of precise requirements. The payment hub must state the rate source, the spread table by currency pair, the rate lock and its expiry time, and the foreign exchange deal reference linked to the payment. The customer must be shown the rate and the spread before they confirm, not after the payment has settled. The value date must be validated against the settlement calendar for the currency pair. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment with a locked rate settles at exactly the quoted amount even when the market rate moves, and a test where a payment value date falls on a currency holiday and the system moves it to the next valid date.
