What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain AML and counter-terrorist financing in payments and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. A risk-based approach is the core of AML law, and the analyst must be able to show the risk rating and the control that follows it.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the regulatory framework that binds payments in Europe, the UK, the US and India, distinguishes PSD2 from PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation, and applies DORA, the Instant Payments Regulation, sanctions regimes, AML rules, RBI directions and FFIEC guidance to realistic payment scenarios with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist financing law in payments
  • Rules and classic typologies
  • Cash-intensive business with cross-border suppliers
  • Correspondent services from smaller banks
  • European Union AML package
  • Acceptance criteria

Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

AML: Anti money laundering, the set of laws, rules and controls that stop criminals from hiding the proceeds of crime through the financial system.

  1. What to do. Work through the scenario and state the outcome with the reason.

Correct result. The salary customer gets standard due diligence, while the money service business gets enhanced due diligence, ongoing transaction monitoring, senior management approval and periodic review.

Why. Enhanced due diligence is the control that matches higher risk, and it must be documented so an examiner can see why it was applied.

Anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist financing law in payments rests on the risk-based approach, which is the principle that controls should be proportionate to the risk. The Financial Action Task Force sets the global standard, and the European Union implements it through the Anti-Money-Laundering Directives, with the sixth directive and the new EU Anti-Money-Laundering Authority, AMLA, based in Frankfurt, taking the regime further. The risk-based approach means the bank must first assess its own risks, by customer type, geography, product and channel, and then design controls that match. A bank that applies the same controls to a domestic salary payment and to a high-value payment to a high-risk jurisdiction is not being thorough, it is being lazy, and the supervisor will say so. Transaction monitoring is the operational heart of the regime. A monitoring system ingests every payment, applies rules and models, and generates alerts for human review. The rules cover the classic typologies. Structuring is the splitting of a large amount into smaller payments below a reporting threshold, and the rule looks for multiple payments just under the threshold from the same customer in a short period. Layering is the movement of money through several accounts to obscure its origin, and the rule looks for rapid in-and-out flows with no economic purpose. Round amounts, dormant accounts that suddenly become active, and payments to high-risk geographies all generate alerts. The rules must be tuned to the bank's own risk profile, because a rule copied from another bank will generate alerts the operations team cannot handle. Customer risk rating is what makes the monitoring proportionate. Every customer gets a risk score based on geography, customer type, product, channel and behaviour, and the score decides how closely they are monitored and how often they are reviewed. A domestic retail customer with a salary and a mortgage is low risk. A cash-intensive business with cross-border suppliers is higher risk. A customer in a high-risk jurisdiction, or a politically exposed person, is high risk and gets enhanced due diligence. The rating must be dynamic: a customer whose behaviour changes, who starts receiving large payments from a new country, must be re-rated, and the re-rating must trigger a review. A static rating that is set at onboarding and never changed is a common examination finding. Correspondent banking is the highest-risk part of the payment business and the part under the most pressure. A respondent bank that provides dollar clearing for a foreign bank must understand that bank's customers, its controls, and its jurisdiction. Nested accounts, where a respondent bank's customer itself provides services to other banks, and payable-through accounts, where a foreign bank's customers can draw directly on the correspondent account, are both high risk and require enhanced due diligence. The result of the pressure has been de-risking, where large banks withdraw correspondent services from smaller banks and from whole regions. De-risking pushes payments into less regulated channels, which is worse for everyone, and supervisors now expect banks to manage the risk rather than simply exit. Counter-terrorist financing differs from anti-money-laundering in ways that matter for monitoring. Money laundering usually involves large amounts and complex flows. Terrorist financing often involves small amounts, legitimate sources such as salaries and donations, and simple transfers. A rule that looks for large round amounts will miss a terrorist financing payment of 400 euros. The controls that work are different: they focus on the destination, the stated purpose, and the network of senders and recipients, rather than on the amount. Non-profit organisations are a particular focus, because they can be abused as conduits, and banks must apply a risk-based approach to them rather than refusing them all, which would cut off legitimate humanitarian work. The European Union AML package changes the landscape again. A single rulebook will replace the directive-based patchwork, the new authority AMLA will directly supervise the highest-risk firms, and a 10,000 euro cash limit will apply across the Union. The single rulebook means the same customer due diligence, the same record keeping, and the same reporting rules in every member state, which removes the arbitrage that let firms choose the lightest regime. For a bank operating across Europe the practical work is to map the current national implementations against the single rulebook, identify the gaps, and close them before the new rules apply. For a business analyst, the AML and counter-terrorist financing work is a set of requirements that must be testable. The monitoring system must ingest all payment types, must apply rules that are documented and owned, must generate alerts with the evidence attached, and must record the disposition of every alert with a reason. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a structuring pattern generates an alert, a test where a customer risk rating changes on a behaviour trigger, a test where a correspondent banking review is completed on schedule, and a test where an alert is dispositioned with a full audit trail. The tuning of the rules must be governed, with above-the-line and below-the-line testing, so the bank can prove its rules catch what they should and do not drown the team in noise.
