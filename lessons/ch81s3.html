Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

MT103: A SWIFT message that carries a single customer credit transfer instruction from one bank to another. It names the ordering customer, the beneficiary, the amount, the currency and the value date.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Every cross-border payment is also a cross-border data transfer. When a European bank sends an MT103 to a correspondent in the United States, it transfers the ordering customer name, the beneficiary name, the account numbers and the remittance information outside the European Economic Area. When a payment goes through SWIFT, the message is stored and processed in data centres in Belgium, the Netherlands and the United States. GDPR Chapter V applies to all of this, and the bank must have a transfer mechanism in place before the payment is sent.

The simplest transfer mechanism is an adequacy decision. The European Commission has decided that a small number of countries provide an adequate level of data protection, and transfers to those countries need no additional safeguard. The United States is not covered by a general adequacy decision for all transfers, although the EU-US Data Privacy Framework provides adequacy for certified US organisations. A payment to a correspondent that is not covered by adequacy needs another mechanism, usually the standard contractual clauses.

Standard contractual clauses are the workhorse for payment data transfers. They are a set of contract terms published by the European Commission that the sending bank and the receiving correspondent sign. The clauses require the receiver to protect the data, to notify the sender of government access requests, and to allow audits. The bank must also carry out a transfer impact assessment, which asks whether the law of the receiving country undermines the protection the clauses promise. For a payment to a country with broad government surveillance powers, that assessment can be difficult.

SWIFT is a special case that every payments BA should understand. SWIFT is a messaging network, not a bank, and it processes personal data as a processor on behalf of its member banks. SWIFT has binding corporate rules approved by the Belgian data protection authority, which provide the transfer mechanism for the data SWIFT processes. But the bank's own transfer to the correspondent, and the correspondent's onward transfer to the beneficiary bank, are separate transfers that need their own mechanisms. The message does not become compliant just because SWIFT is compliant.

The payment message itself creates a data minimisation problem. Field 70 of an MT103 can carry any free text the payer writes, and payers write medical details, legal references and personal notes. Field 72 can carry instructions that name individuals. Once that text is in the message it travels to every bank in the chain, and it is stored in every screening system along the way. The practical control is to limit free-text fields at the point of initiation, to warn the customer that the reference will be visible to other banks, and to strip the field before it reaches an analytics system.

Sanctions screening makes cross-border transfers more complex, because the screening itself is a transfer. When a European bank screens a payment against a US sanctions list hosted in the United States, the payment data is transferred to the screening provider. When a correspondent bank in Singapore screens a payment it received from Europe, the data has already crossed the border. The screening contract must therefore include the transfer mechanism, and the bank must be able to show which screening providers hold payment data and where they are located.

For a business analyst, the transfer work is a mapping exercise. Every payment flow must be mapped to the countries the data passes through, the transfer mechanism for each hop, and the contract that contains it. The requirements must state that a new correspondent cannot be onboarded until the transfer mechanism is signed, and the acceptance criteria must include a test that proves the mechanism exists for each corridor. Last reviewed 7 November 2026. Further reading: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Chapter V; European Commission standard contractual clauses, Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914; European Data Protection Board recommendations on supplementary transfer tools; SWIFT binding corporate rules.
