Key terms

Intraday liquidity: Cash available to a bank during the business day to settle payments as they fall due. Intraday liquidity comes from account balances, incoming payments and central bank facilities.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Show transcript

A bank can be solvent at the end of the day and still fail during it. Payments leave before receipts arrive, and the gap between the two needs funding: intraday liquidity. If the gap exceeds the liquidity available, payments queue, settle late, or fail, and the failure can cascade to other banks waiting for those payments. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, the BCBS, published a framework of intraday liquidity monitoring tools in 2013, so that banks and supervisors can watch liquidity within the day, not just at the close. The tools are measurements, not ratios: they describe what happened intraday, and the supervisor judges whether the pattern is safe.

The framework defines six monitoring tools. Daily maximum intraday liquidity usage: the largest net cumulative outflow during the day, the peak funding need. Available intraday liquidity at the start of the business day: the liquidity the bank begins with, including central bank balances and collateral. Total payments: the value of payments sent during the day, the scale of the operation. Time specific and other critical obligations: payments that must settle by a particular time, such as settlement system obligations and time critical customer payments. Value of customer payments made on behalf of financial institution customers: the payments the bank makes for its respondent banks, which create intraday exposure to those customers. And intraday throughput: the profile of payments through the day, showing when value moved.

What the tools reveal is the shape of the day. Peak usage against available liquidity shows the buffer: how close the bank came to exhausting its intraday funding. The timing of obligations shows clustering: if critical payments all fall due in the same hour, the bank needs the liquidity ready for that hour, not spread evenly. Throughput shows dependence: a bank that receives most of its inflows late in the day, but must pay early, runs a larger intraday gap than a bank whose flows are balanced. Customer payments on behalf of other banks show where the bank is funding others intraday, and how much exposure that creates.

The tools complement the LCR and NSFR, which are end of day and monthly measures. The LCR asks whether the bank can survive thirty days; the intraday tools ask whether it can survive today, hour by hour. A bank can pass the LCR and still run dangerous intraday patterns: a thin morning buffer, a heavy reliance on incoming payments to fund outgoing ones, or a concentration of time critical obligations. The supervisor uses the tools to spot those patterns, to compare banks, and to challenge a bank whose intraday profile looks fragile, even when its ratios look comfortable.

Reporting follows a regular rhythm. Banks report the tools to their supervisors, typically monthly, with daily data, and supervisors may ask for more frequent reporting in stress or for systemically important banks. The reporting includes stress scenarios: what the intraday profile would look like if a major counterparty failed to pay, or if a settlement system was delayed. The bank must also have its own intraday monitoring, in real time, so that its treasury can act during the day: drawing on central bank facilities, delaying non critical payments, or calling for funds, before the gap becomes a failure.

For a business analyst, the monitoring tools are an intraday data project. The tools need data at intraday granularity: payment timestamps, queue positions, position snapshots through the day, and obligation schedules. End of day systems do not have this data; the payment hub, the settlement systems, and the treasury workstation do, and the data must be captured, timestamped, and aggregated consistently. Building the feed, defining the calculations, and proving the numbers reconcile to the end of day positions is the work. A bank that cannot measure its intraday liquidity cannot manage it, and the BCBS tools are the measurement standard.
