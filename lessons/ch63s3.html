What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain anatomy of a bank card. The front of a card carries the cardholder name, the card number, the expiry date and usually the scheme logo, while the back carries the magnetic stripe, the signature panel and the three digit security code.

Why it matters. Each element of the card maps to a data field in the transaction, and a business analyst must know which field carries which value.

Chapter objective: Learner explains card types, the three-corner and four-corner models, and the full authorisation, clearing and settlement flow for a EUR 120 online purchase. Learner describes card anatomy, processor roles, fee sharing, ATM withdrawals, and the SEPA Cards Framework.

Key learning points

  • Front of a card
  • Printed on the back for most cards
  • Magnetic stripe
  • Tap: set by country
  • Element card mapping to data field
  • Testing covering a card with an expired date

Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Acquirer: A bank or licensed institution that processes card payments for merchants and receives transaction funds from card schemes on their behalf.

Scheme: A card or payment network that sets the rules, standards and fees for transactions between issuers and acquirers.

Exercise

Trace the card payment in this lesson through authorisation, clearing and settlement.

Use the four-party model and the stage timings in this lesson.

  1. What to do. State what happens at each stage, who pays whom, and when the merchant is actually credited.

Correct result. Authorisation holds the funds and returns a response code. Clearing sends the transaction in a batch file. Settlement moves the net amount between banks, and the acquirer credits the merchant after deducting interchange, scheme fees and its margin.

Why. A card payment is not one event. The customer sees it on Monday, the merchant gets it on Wednesday, and the difference is the clearing and settlement cycle.

The front of a card carries the cardholder name, the card number, the expiry date and usually the scheme logo, while the back carries the magnetic stripe, the signature panel and the three digit security code. The card number, also called the Primary Account Number or PAN, is typically 16 digits long, and its first six to eight digits identify the issuing bank and the scheme, while the remaining digits identify the individual account. The expiry date tells a terminal and a website when the card stops being valid, and a card used after expiry will be declined. The security code, printed on the back for most cards, proves the cardholder holds the physical card, which is why it is used for online purchases. The chip, the small gold contact plate on the front of the card, is a secure computer that stores the card data and runs the checks that approve or decline a transaction. When a card is inserted into a terminal, the chip talks to the terminal, checks the PIN, applies the card limits and creates a cryptogram, which is a one time code that proves the card is genuine. The magnetic stripe on the back is an older technology that stores the same card data in a magnetic band, and it is read by swiping the card through a terminal. The stripe can be copied more easily than the chip, which is why chip transactions replaced stripe transactions in most countries, and why some cards no longer carry a stripe at all. The contactless antenna is a thin coil inside the card that lets it communicate with a terminal by radio when the card is held near the reader. A tap creates the same kind of cryptogram as a chip insertion, but without a PIN for low value payments, and the limit for a PIN free tap is set by the country or the scheme, often around EUR 50. Above that limit, or after several consecutive taps, the terminal asks for a PIN, which resets the contactless counter. The antenna also works inside a phone or a watch when the card is tokenised, which means the real card number is replaced by a device specific token, so a stolen phone does not expose the real PAN. Each element of the card maps to a data field in the transaction, and a business analyst must know which field carries which value. The PAN identifies the issuer and the account, the expiry date is checked at authorisation, the service code tells the terminal whether chip, stripe or contactless is allowed, and the cryptogram proves the card is genuine. The security code is checked only for card-not-present payments, and it must never be stored after authorisation, because storing it breaks the scheme rules and creates a fraud risk. Testing should cover a card with an expired date, a wrong security code, a blocked chip and a damaged stripe, because each failure must produce a clear decline code and a message the cardholder can understand.

The acquirer routes the request to the scheme, the scheme routes it to Meridian Bank, and the bank checks the account balance before it approves, all in under two seconds.

If the chip is damaged, the terminal falls back to the magnetic stripe, but the issuer may decline a fallback transaction, because the stripe is easier to copy than the chip, and the cardholder must then use another payment method or a new card. If the card is expired, the issuer declines the transaction, and the cardholder must use a replacement card. The recovery for a damaged chip is a replacement card, which takes three to five working days, and the cardholder should be given a way to pay in the meantime, such as a mobile wallet linked to the same account.

For a business analyst, product owner or subject matter expert, the card anatomy requirement must state which data element maps to which transaction field, the PAN to the account, the expiry date to the validity check, the service code to the terminal behaviour, and the cryptogram to the authentication. The requirement must also state the masking rule, only the last four digits of the PAN may be shown on a receipt or a screen, and must state that the full PAN must never be stored in plain text. The test pack must include a card with a damaged chip, an expired card, and a card with a wrong PAN, because each case exercises a different part of the anatomy.
