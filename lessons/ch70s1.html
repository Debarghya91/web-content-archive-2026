What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain account taxonomy: current, savings, nostro, vostro, suspense, settlement, clearing, transit and wash. An account is a record of a claim, and the type of account decides what the claim is, who can move it, and what happens when it is wrong.

Why it matters. Takeaway: current and savings accounts hold customer deposits, nostro and vostro accounts are the two views of the same correspondent balance, suspense holds items that cannot yet be posted, settlement holds amounts due to or from a clearing system, and transit and wash accounts must net to zero.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the account taxonomy, including nostro, vostro, suspense, settlement, clearing, transit and wash accounts, and states what each account holds and who can move it. Learner distinguishes ledger balance from available balance and value date from booking date, and explains how settlement and clearing accounts sit between banks.

Key terms

Suspense account: A temporary account used to hold funds or entries that cannot yet be posted to their final account. Banks use suspense accounts for unidentified receipts, pending investigations and items awaiting...

Transit account: An internal account used to hold funds temporarily while they move between systems or accounts. Transit accounts record the movement of money during settlement, currency conversion or internal...

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

An account is a record of a claim, and the type of account decides what the claim is, who can move it, and what happens when it is wrong. A current account holds a customer demand deposit that can be drawn on at any time, and it is the account from which most payments are made. A savings account holds a deposit that earns interest and may have withdrawal limits, and it is not normally used for daily payment instructions. A clearing account holds items in transit between systems, and a transit or wash account holds items that pass through and must net to zero. Source: Bank for International Settlements, Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures; ISO 20022 cash management messages, camt.052 and camt.053. The rules for each account type are different, and confusing them creates breaks that are hard to clear. A current account can be overdrawn only within an agreed limit, and an overdraft is a loan that must be priced and approved. A savings account may not be overdrawn, and a payment instruction drawn on it should be rejected. A vostro account is a liability of the holding bank, and the bank must be able to pay out on demand, subject to cut-offs and screening. A suspense account must be aged, reviewed daily, and cleared within a defined period, because an old suspense item is an unreconciled break. A wash account must net to zero at the end of the day, and any balance left in it is a posting error by definition. A worked example shows the taxonomy in one payment. A customer of Bank A in London holds a current account with GBP 50,000 and a savings account with GBP 200,000, and instructs a GBP 25,000 payment to a supplier in Germany. Bank A debits the current account, not the savings account, and the current balance falls to GBP 25,000. Bank A pays through its euro nostro account at Bank B in Frankfurt, so the nostro balance falls by EUR 29,000, the euro equivalent. During the day a EUR 500 item arrives with a missing reference, so operations parks it in a suspense account, and a EUR 29,000 settlement item passes through a transit account that nets to zero by the close. Each account did a different job, and each is reconciled against a different source. The parties and the systems that own each account are distinct. The customer owns the current and savings accounts, and the bank is the debtor for the balance. The bank owns the nostro account, and the correspondent bank is the debtor. The bank owns the vostro account as a liability, and the foreign bank is the creditor. Treasury owns the settlement account, and the clearing system is the counterparty. The core banking system holds the customer accounts, the nostro management system holds the nostro and vostro accounts, and the payment hub holds the transit and wash accounts, and the three systems must agree at the end of the day, with the general ledger as the single record. Failure is account specific, and the failure handling follows the account type. A payment drawn on a savings account that does not allow payments is rejected with a reason code, and the customer must be told to use the current account. A suspense account that grows is a warning that instructions are arriving with missing data, and the fix is in the instruction capture, not in the suspense process. A wash account that does not net to zero means a posting is missing or duplicated, and the investigation starts with the payment that left the balance, not with the account itself. Each failure has a different owner, a different system and a different fix. The effect on the bank, the customer and operations is that the account type decides the customer experience and the control. For the customer, the difference between a current and a savings account is whether the payment is allowed, how fast it arrives and what it costs. For the bank, the nostro and vostro accounts are its liquidity and its credit exposure to other banks, and they are managed by treasury under limits that the board approves. A business analyst must name the account type for every debit and credit in a requirement, because the posting rule, the reconciliation source and the failure handling all follow from it. For a business analyst, product owner or subject matter expert, the taxonomy is a checklist for every payment flow. Ask which account is debited, which is credited, what type each account is, who owns it, what system holds it, and what source reconciles it. Ask what happens when the account does not exist, is closed, is frozen, has insufficient funds, or is the wrong type for the payment. Ask how long an item may sit in suspense, who reviews it, and who approves a write-off. The ISO 20022 camt.052 intraday report and camt.053 end of day statement report the balances for each account type, and the account type is carried in the message, so the reconciliation can be automated if the mapping is correct. A requirement that says only account, without naming the type, is incomplete. Two accounts deserve a special warning because they are often misused. The first is the suspense account, which is a temporary parking place, not a permanent home, and a product that routes exceptions to suspense without an ageing rule and a clearance owner will accumulate breaks that nobody owns. The second is the wash or transit account, which must net to zero, and a product that posts fees, charges or foreign exchange differences into a wash account will hide them from the profit and loss account and from the customer. The correct design is that a wash account holds only items in transit between two postings that happen on the same day, and any difference is posted to a proper income, expense or foreign exchange account, with the customer charged only what was disclosed. Takeaway: current and savings accounts hold customer deposits, nostro and vostro accounts are the two views of the same correspondent balance, suspense holds items that cannot yet be posted, settlement holds amounts due to or from a clearing system, and transit and wash accounts must net to zero. Name the account type for every debit and credit, state who owns it and what reconciles it, and age suspense daily. Further reading: Bank for International Settlements, Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures, A glossary of terms used in payments and settlement systems; ISO 20022, camt.052 and camt.053 account reporting.
