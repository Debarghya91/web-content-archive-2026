Fraud detection is the job of spotting a payment that looks wrong before the money is gone, and AI helps by scoring each payment against patterns it has learned from past fraud and from normal behaviour. Take a retail customer who normally spends around three thousand rupees a day in Bangalore, and suddenly there is a payment of one lakh eighty thousand rupees to a new beneficiary in another country at two in the morning. That payment breaks the normal pattern on amount, time, place and beneficiary, so the model gives it a high fraud score. A high score does not mean the payment is fraud. It means a human must look at it now. The analyst checks the customer history, whether the customer has travelled or added a new beneficiary, and may call the customer to confirm. If the customer confirms, the payment goes ahead and the model learns this pattern was genuine. If the customer says no, the payment is stopped and the account is protected. The hard part is false positives, genuine payments that get flagged and delayed. Too many false positives mean angry customers and a team that starts ignoring alerts, while too few flags mean fraud slips through. Your job is to help set the balance, and to write the rule that a fraud score is a signal for a human, not a decision by itself. No payment is blocked, no account is frozen and no customer is accused on a score alone. Every block needs a named human decision, a reason recorded, and a way for the customer to appeal quickly. The model must also show its reasons in plain words, the amount, the new beneficiary and the unusual hour, so the analyst can check the facts instead of trusting a black box number.
