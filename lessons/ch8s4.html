Truncation risk is the risk that data is lost when a longer MX field is translated to a shorter MT field, and it is one of the most important risks in the MT to MX migration. An MX message can carry a full structured postal address with street name, building number, town, postcode, and country, each in a separate element with generous length limits. An MT message can carry an address only as four lines of 35 characters each, and the total length is far shorter. When an MX address is translated to MT, the address must be truncated or abbreviated, and the receiving bank may not be able to identify the party correctly. Similarly, an MX remittance block with multiple invoices and amounts must be truncated to 140 characters in MT field 70, and the beneficiary may not be able to reconcile the payment.

Truncation is not just a technical inconvenience, it has business consequences. A truncated name can cause a sanctions screening false positive, because the truncated name may match a sanctioned person even though the full name does not. A truncated remittance reference can cause a reconciliation break, because the creditor cannot match the payment to an invoice. A truncated address can cause a payment to be rejected by the creditor agent, because the agent cannot verify the beneficiary. These consequences are often silent, because the payment is not rejected at the translation point, but fails later, in a different system, with a different error code. That makes truncation hard to diagnose without understanding the translation chain.

Mitigating truncation risk requires a combination of design, testing, and communication. The design should prioritise which data is most important and ensure that it is not truncated, for example by placing the invoice number at the start of the remittance field rather than at the end. Testing should include cases with long addresses, long names, and long remittance blocks, to see what is actually truncated and what the downstream impact is. Communication should warn customers and corporates that data may be truncated during coexistence, and should advise them to keep critical references short and at the start of the field. An analyst who understands truncation risk can write requirements that minimise it, and can explain to stakeholders why a payment that looks correct in MX may fail after translation to MT.

Take a concrete example. A corporate customer sends a pacs.008 with a structured address that includes a long street name, a building number, a floor, a town, and a postcode. Translated to MT, that address must fit into four lines of 35 characters in field 59. The street name is cut, the floor is dropped, and the postcode is squeezed onto the last line. The receiving bank gets an address it cannot fully match to its customer record. Or take remittance information with several invoice numbers. Translated to MT field 70, only the first 140 characters survive, and the later invoice numbers are lost. The beneficiary receives the money but cannot reconcile it, because the reference for the second invoice never arrived.

A truncated name can cause a sanctions screening false positive, because the shortened name may match a sanctioned person even though the full name does not. A truncated remittance reference can cause a reconciliation break, because the creditor cannot match the payment to an invoice. A truncated address can cause a payment to be rejected by the creditor agent, because the agent cannot verify the beneficiary. These consequences are often silent, because the payment is not rejected at the translation point. It fails later, in a different system, with a different error code, and the team investigating the failure has to work backwards through the translation chain to find the cause.

Mitigation starts with design. Put the most important data first, so that if truncation happens, the critical reference survives. Keep invoice numbers short and at the start of the remittance field. Use structured fields natively in MX so that no translation is needed at all. Testing should include the longest realistic names, addresses, and remittance blocks, so the team sees what is actually truncated before customers do. Translation engines should log every truncation, so operations can see what was lost. And customers should be told during coexistence that data could be truncated, and advised to keep critical references short and at the start of the field.
