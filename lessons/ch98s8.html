You cannot own an AI feature without measuring it, and you cannot measure it with one number. You need a small set of metrics that together tell you whether the feature is helping, whether it is safe, and whether it is worth the cost. Start with accuracy, but define it for your job. For a payment matching feature, accuracy might mean the share of payments matched correctly on the first try, without human correction. Then add the human override rate. How often does a person change or reject what the model did. If the override rate is high, the model is not trusted, or it is wrong in a way the number alone hides. Next, measure the cost of errors. An error on a payment of 120 pounds is not the same as an error on a payment of 120,000 pounds. Track the number of errors, the value affected, and how many were caught before the money moved. Then measure time saved. How many minutes per case does the feature save, and does that time actually go to better work, or just to fixing the model's mistakes. Suppose the feature saves ten minutes on each of two hundred cases a day, but creates twenty extra cases that each take thirty minutes to fix. The headline saving is false. Finally, agree what to do when a metric moves the wrong way. Write the trigger in advance. If accuracy falls below the agreed level for two days in a row, or if overrides rise above ten in every hundred, the team pauses new releases, checks the data and the model, and tells the product owner. A metric without a response is just a chart. A metric with a named owner and a clear action is a control.
