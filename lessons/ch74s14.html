Show transcript

An account number is hard to remember and easy to mistype, so instant payment systems let people pay to something easier: a phone number, an email address, a national identifier, or another alias that the system resolves to an account. This is proxy and alias initiation, and it has two parts that must not be confused. The proxy is the alias itself, the friendly identifier the sender types. The resolution is the lookup that turns the proxy into the account details the payment system needs. The sender sees only the proxy, the payment system sees only the account, and the directory in between is what makes the payment possible.

The directory is the heart of the design. When a customer registers a proxy, the bank records the link between the proxy and the account in a directory, and the directory answers lookups from other banks. A proxy must resolve to exactly one account at a time, and the registration process must prove that the customer owns both the proxy and the account, usually by sending a code to the phone number or email address and asking the customer to confirm it. A proxy can be moved from one account to another, for example when the customer changes bank, and the directory must update atomically, so that a payment sent during the move goes to one account or the other, never to both and never to neither. A proxy that is deregistered must stop resolving, and a payment to a deregistered proxy must be rejected with a clear reason, not routed to a stale account.

Different systems implement proxies differently, and the analyst must know the local model. In India, UPI uses a virtual payment address, an alias in the form of a name plus a handle, which resolves to the customer bank account. In Australia, PayID lets customers register a phone number, email address or business number as the alias for an account. In Brazil, Pix keys can be a phone number, an email address, a national tax identifier, or a random key generated by the system. In the euro area, the SEPA Proxy Lookup scheme lets a payment service provider look up whether a mobile number is registered for instant payments and, if so, which provider holds the account. The pattern is the same everywhere: register, verify, resolve, and confirm before paying.

Confirmation is the control that makes proxies safe. Before the payment is sent, the system shows the sender the name of the account holder that the proxy resolves to, and the sender confirms that it is the person they intended to pay. This is the same principle as Confirmation of Payee, applied to aliases, and it catches the two most common proxy failures: a mistyped phone number that resolves to a stranger, and a recycled phone number that now belongs to someone else. The confirmation must show enough of the name for the sender to recognise the payee, without exposing the full account details to someone who is guessing numbers. The analyst must design the confirmation step as a hard gate: no confirmation, no payment, even if the sender is in a hurry.

Portability and lifecycle are the operational edge cases. A phone number can be ported between mobile operators, and the proxy must survive the port, because the proxy belongs to the customer, not to the operator. A phone number can be recycled to a new subscriber, and the old proxy registration must be removed before the number is reissued, or payments will go to the wrong person. An email address can be closed, and the proxy must be deregistered. The directory operator must run lifecycle processes that detect stale registrations, challenge them, and remove them, and the banks must handle the case where a proxy resolves today but not tomorrow. The analyst must include these lifecycle cases in the requirements, because they are where proxy payments fail in production.

A worked example shows the flow. A customer wants to pay a friend 50 pounds and enters the friend phone number into the banking app. The app sends the number to the proxy directory, which returns the name of the account holder and the bank that holds the account. The app shows the name, the customer confirms it is the right person, and the payment is sent as a Faster Payment to the resolved sort code and account number. The friend receives the money in seconds, and neither party ever saw an account number. If the number had not been registered, the app would have said so immediately, and the customer would have asked for the account details instead. The proxy made the payment easy, and the directory and the confirmation made it safe.
