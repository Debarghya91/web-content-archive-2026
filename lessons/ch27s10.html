Key terms

Correspondent banking: A service where one bank holds accounts for another bank so the second bank can make payments in currencies or countries where it has no branch.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Use the settlement models, the liquidity trade-off and the finality rules in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Ripple is a company whose products aim at the same problem as SWIFT gpi and the instant payment links: making cross border payments faster and cheaper. Its offering has three parts that are often confused with each other: RippleNet, the network; XRP, the digital asset; and On-Demand Liquidity, the service that uses XRP as a bridge currency. Understanding the three parts separately is the key to understanding what Ripple does, what it does not do, and where it fits in the payment landscape.

RippleNet is a network of banks, payment service providers, and other financial institutions that exchange payment instructions using Ripple technology. Participants connect to RippleNet, exchange payment messages, and settle the payments. Settlement can happen through traditional correspondent accounts, the nostro and vostro accounts that banks already hold, or through XRP. RippleNet provides the messaging, the routing, and the tracking: the participants agree the payment details, and the network carries the instruction. A bank can use RippleNet for messaging and settlement through its existing accounts without ever touching XRP.

XRP is a digital asset designed for payments. It settles in seconds, and its transaction cost is a fraction of a cent. Its role in the Ripple offering is as a bridge currency: instead of holding funded nostro accounts in every currency pair, a payment service provider can convert the source currency to XRP, transfer the XRP, and convert it to the destination currency. The bridge removes the need for pre funded accounts in the currency pair, which is the capital cost that makes many cross border corridors expensive. XRP is traded on digital asset exchanges, and its price moves with the market, so bridge transfers are executed quickly to limit price exposure.

On-Demand Liquidity, known as ODL, is the service that puts the bridge to work. A sending provider takes the source currency from its customer, converts it to XRP, and sends the XRP to the receiving provider, which converts it to the destination currency and pays the beneficiary. The liquidity is on demand: it is sourced at the moment of the payment, from the exchange market, rather than pre funded in a nostro account. ODL operates in corridors where the XRP market is liquid enough to support the conversions at scale, and where the regulatory treatment of digital assets permits it. The customer sees a fast cross border payment; the bridge mechanics are invisible.

The distinction between the three parts matters commercially and regulatorily. RippleNet is a network and a messaging service. XRP is a digital asset whose regulatory treatment varies by jurisdiction and has been contested in the courts. ODL is a service that depends on both the network and the asset. A bank evaluating Ripple must decide which parts it wants: network participation alone, or network plus bridge liquidity, and it must assess the regulatory position in each jurisdiction where it operates. The three parts are sold together in the marketing, but they are adopted separately in practice.

Ripple sits in a crowded field of approaches to faster cross border payments. SWIFT gpi improved the correspondent banking rails. Instant payment links connect domestic fast systems across borders. Stablecoins offer dollar and euro denominated digital settlement. Central bank digital currencies promise a public sector alternative. Each approach trades off speed, cost, reach, and regulatory certainty differently. Ripple approach, a private network plus a bridge asset, is one answer. For a business analyst, the lesson is to evaluate the approach on its mechanics: how does the payment move, where does the liquidity come from, who bears the risk, and what does the regulator say. The answers decide whether the approach fits a given corridor.
