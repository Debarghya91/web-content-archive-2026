Key terms

Practise this lesson

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a $10,000 payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full amount. Under SHA the sender pays the sending charges and the beneficiary pays the receiving charges. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives the amount less all fees.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

A multinational group may have a hundred subsidiaries, each with its own bank accounts, each making its own payments. Paying on behalf of, POBO, centralises that activity: one central entity, a shared service centre, a payment factory, or an in-house bank, makes payments on behalf of the group entities. The subsidiaries send their payment instructions to the central payer, the central payer executes them from central accounts, and the intercompany accounting records that the payment was made on behalf of the subsidiary. The beneficiary is paid, the group has fewer accounts and better control, and the subsidiary sees the payment in its own books.

The flow has three steps. The subsidiary creates the payment instruction in its own system: the beneficiary, the amount, the value date, and the reference. The instruction goes to the central payer, which validates it, checks the authorisation and the limits, and releases it. The central payer executes the payment from a central account, and the beneficiary receives the funds. The intercompany accounting follows: the central payer records a receivable from the subsidiary, and the subsidiary records a payable to the central payer, so that each entity books reflect the payment correctly. The beneficiary may see the central entity name or the subsidiary name on the payment, depending on the arrangement and the bank capability, and the choice is a design decision with reconciliation consequences.

The benefits of POBO are the benefits of centralisation. Fewer bank accounts: the group holds accounts centrally instead of in every entity. Better rates: the central payer negotiates bank fees and foreign exchange rates on group volumes. Centralised control: payment authorisation, fraud checks, and sanctions screening run once, centrally, to one standard. Standardised processes: every entity follows the same instruction format, the same cut offs, and the same exception handling. And improved liquidity: payments are funded from one pool, so the group holds less idle cash across scattered accounts.

POBO has structures and a mirror image. A payment factory is the central execution unit: it receives instructions and executes payments, without holding internal accounts for the entities. An in-house bank goes further: it holds internal accounts for the entities, settles intercompany payments internally, and only the net external payments leave the group. COBO, collect on behalf of, is the mirror: a central entity collects receivables on behalf of the group entities, and the collections are allocated internally. POBO and COBO often run together, giving the group centralised payables and receivables.

Controls and considerations shape every POBO design. Authorisation: who may instruct on behalf of whom, with what limits, and with what evidence. Intercompany reconciliation: the central and subsidiary records must agree, and breaks must be cleared promptly. Regulatory and tax: the payment must be attributable to the right entity for reporting, tax, and regulatory purposes, and some jurisdictions restrict on behalf of structures. And beneficiary communication: the beneficiary must be able to reconcile the payment, which means the reference and the remittance data must identify the subsidiary obligation even when the payer name is central.

For a business analyst, POBO design covers the instruction flow, the account structure, the intercompany postings, and the exception handling. The requirements name the instruction format, the authorisation model, the central accounts, the intercompany accounting rules, and the beneficiary facing data. The test cases cover a subsidiary instruction that fails validation, a payment that is returned, and a reconciliation break between the central and subsidiary records. POBO is a treasury structure expressed as a payment flow, and the flow must be designed as carefully as the structure.
