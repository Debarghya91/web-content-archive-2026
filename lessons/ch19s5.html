  • Liquidity pool

Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Show transcript

A liquidity pool is an arrangement where a group of banks, often part of the same banking group, combine their liquidity for settlement purposes. Instead of each entity holding its own separate balance and managing its own queues, the group shares a common pool, and payments from any member can draw on the pool. This reduces the total liquidity the group needs, because a surplus in one entity can fund a deficit in another without a separate transfer. It also simplifies operations, because the group manages one position instead of many. Liquidity pooling is common in banking groups that operate in several countries or through several legal entities. The parent bank may hold a central pool at the central bank, and subsidiaries draw on it as needed, subject to limits and controls. The arrangement must respect legal and regulatory boundaries, because funds in one legal entity cannot always be freely used to settle obligations of another, especially across borders. Regulators may require that pooling does not weaken the standalone resilience of any individual entity, and that it can be unwound quickly if one member gets into difficulty. Shared arrangements also exist between unrelated banks in some systems, often in the form of a liquidity saving facility operated by the central bank or the system operator. Participants contribute to a common fund, and the facility can provide short term liquidity to a participant that is temporarily short, secured against the fund. These arrangements are designed to prevent a temporary shortage at one bank from disrupting the whole system. They are not a substitute for sound liquidity management at each bank, but they add a layer of resilience that benefits everyone. Compare pooled and separate liquidity with one group. A banking group has three entities with settlement balances of EUR 30 million, EUR 5 million, and EUR 40 million. Pooled, the group manages EUR 75 million as one position. When the smallest entity needs to send a EUR 45 million payment, it settles from the pool without delay. Without pooling, the same payment would queue against a EUR 5 million balance, and the entity would have to borrow or wait for an internal transfer from a sister entity, which takes time and creates its own payment. The saving is real, but it has a boundary. Funds held by the London entity cannot be used to settle an obligation of the Frankfurt entity without a transfer, because they belong to different legal entities in different jurisdictions. Regulators also require that pooling does not weaken any single entity. If the pool were unwound tomorrow, each entity must still be able to settle on its own, and the group must be able to prove it. An analyst documents a pooling arrangement in the same way as any shared service. The agreement terms, the per entity limits, and the unwind plan. The requirements should state the maximum any one entity may draw from the pool, the controls that stop one entity draining it, and the reporting that shows each entity's position separately as well as the pooled total. The test pack should include a simulated failure of one member, confirming that the other members can still settle, and a simulated unwind, confirming that each entity can operate standalone. A pool that has never been tested for unwind is a pool that will surprise everyone on the day a member gets into difficulty.
