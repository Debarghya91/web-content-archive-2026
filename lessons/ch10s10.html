Key terms

Irrevocable: A payment or instruction that cannot be cancelled or reversed once it has been made, except by agreement of all parties or by a court order.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

The National Settlement Service settles the money behind private sector clearing arrangements in the United States, using accounts at the Federal Reserve. It is owned and operated by the Federal Reserve Banks, and it works on a multilateral basis, which means one settlement file can settle the net obligations of many institutions at once. A clearing arrangement first works out what each participant owes or is owed, and the service then moves central bank money between the settlers' master accounts. Credits posted through the service are final and irrevocable, so a receiving institution can rely on the money once settlement has happened. The terms for the service sit in Federal Reserve Operating Circular 12.

The service settles a wider set of arrangements than ACH alone, although ACH is the largest and best known user. Arrangements that use the service include ACH networks, local check clearinghouse associations, credit card processors, automated teller machine networks, financial exchanges and other clearing and settlement groups. Each arrangement clears its own transactions under its own rules and produces a net position for each settling institution, and the service does not re-examine the underlying transactions. For ACH, the Electronic Payments Network, operated by The Clearing House, submits its net settlement through the service, while settlement for the Federal Reserve's own ACH service follows separate Federal Reserve arrangements.

A settlement agent stands at the centre of every arrangement that uses the service. The agent, usually the arrangement's operator, calculates each settler's net debit or net credit position, prepares a file listing each settler and the amount to be debited or credited, and submits it electronically to a Federal Reserve Bank. A settler is a depository institution that holds a master account at a Federal Reserve Bank and settles for itself or for other participants. A participant without its own master account settles through a correspondent institution acting as its settler, so the file may show fewer settlers than participants. The agent acts on behalf of the settlers, and its file is the single instruction that drives the whole settlement cycle.

Settlement entries work on net positions, never on individual payments. For each cycle the file carries one net entry per settler, a single debit or credit for the overall position, regardless of how many transactions produced it. The Federal Reserve Bank processes the file on receipt, working through the debits first and holding the debited funds in a settlement account on its books before applying the credits to the settlers' master accounts. Every debit therefore has matching credits, the file balances, and the individual entries behind it are never posted one by one. Once the entries have posted, the settlement agent receives an acknowledgement confirming the outcome, and the interbank obligations for that cycle are discharged in central bank money.

Timing is what most often confuses analysts, because clearing, customer posting and interbank settlement happen at different moments. ACH files move through the operators during the day, receiving banks typically credit customers by the settlement date, and the net interbank money moves when the cycle's settlement file is processed. Same day ACH uses several settlement cycles during the business day, while standard ACH settles on the next business day, so a payment received in the morning may not complete interbank settlement until a later cycle. Each settler must hold enough in its master account, or have access to intraday credit from the Federal Reserve, to fund its net debit when the file arrives. Banks therefore monitor expected net positions and master account balances through the day, because a net debit that cannot be funded cannot settle on schedule.

The contrast with Fedwire shows what the National Settlement Service is for. Fedwire settles individual payments one by one in real time on the direct instruction of the sending bank, at any point during its operating hours. The National Settlement Service settles only net entries, only from files submitted by a settlement agent, and only at the scheduled cycles of each arrangement. Both settle in central bank money with finality, but Fedwire pays one amount finally and at once, while the National Settlement Service settles the net result of very large numbers of cleared transactions efficiently. When an ACH payment looks delayed, the analyst should ask which settlement cycle its file fell into and whether the net position was funded, rather than searching for a single payment inside the settlement system, where individual payments never appear.
