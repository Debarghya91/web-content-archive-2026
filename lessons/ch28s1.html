Key terms

Purpose code: A code that states the commercial reason for a payment, such as supplier payment, salary or goods. Purpose codes are taken from an ISO external code list and support regulatory reporting, screening...

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Capture: The step in a card transaction where the merchant collects the authorised amount from the issuer, usually when goods are shipped.

Requirements gathering is the first and most important phase of any payments project, because a requirement missed at this stage becomes a defect, a delay or a failed audit later. A business analyst starts by identifying every stakeholder group that touches the payment flow. Business users define what the product must do, operations teams describe how payments are processed day to day, compliance officers set the rules that cannot be broken, and IT explains what the existing systems can and cannot support. Each group sees the same payment from a different angle, and the analyst must capture all of them before any design work begins.

Stakeholder interviews are the analyst's main tool, and they work best when they are planned rather than improvised. Before the interview, the analyst prepares a question list tailored to the interviewee's role and sends the agenda in advance so the person can gather facts. During the interview the analyst listens more than talks, asks open questions such as how a payment is processed today, and follows up with probing questions whenever an answer is vague. A good interview ends with the analyst reading back a short summary, which gives the stakeholder a chance to correct misunderstandings immediately.

Business users are asked about the commercial need behind the project. For example, if a bank wants to add instant payments, the analyst asks which customer segments need it, what transaction limits apply, and what the expected volumes are. A typical answer might be that retail customers need transfers up to 200,000 rupees with immediate confirmation, while corporate customers need bulk files of up to 10,000 payments. These answers shape the functional scope, the performance targets and the priority of each requirement, so they must be recorded with the exact numbers and not as approximate guesses.

Operations teams are asked about the daily reality of processing payments. The analyst asks how many payments are handled each day, what the peak hours are, how exceptions are worked, and which manual steps exist today. An operations manager might explain that the team clears 45,000 outward remittances daily, that the 3 in the afternoon cut-off creates a queue of 2,000 held payments, and that four staff members spend two hours each morning repairing messages with missing beneficiary details. These facts reveal pain points that the new solution must fix and become the basis for measurable improvement targets.

Compliance officers are asked about the rules that constrain the design. The analyst asks which sanctions lists apply, what screening must happen before release, which fields are mandatory for audit, and how long records must be retained. A compliance answer might specify that every outward payment above 50,000 rupees needs a purpose code, that all payments are screened against the OFAC and UN lists, and that the bank must store the full payment record for the retention period that applies in its jurisdiction and for that record type, for example ten years under some regimes and five years under others. These are non-negotiable requirements, and the analyst marks them as such in the requirements document so nobody treats them as optional.

IT teams are asked about the current landscape and its limits. The analyst asks which core banking system holds the accounts, which payment hub routes the messages, what formats the systems exchange today, and where the known constraints sit. An architect might explain that the core banking system posts in real time but the payment hub still processes SWIFT MT messages in a batch every fifteen minutes, which means same-day value cannot be promised for payments received after 4 in the afternoon. Capturing these constraints early prevents the project from promising a feature the platform cannot deliver.
