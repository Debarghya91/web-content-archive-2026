Key terms

Serial payment: A payment method where the customer instruction and the funds travel together through each bank in the chain.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Cover payment: A payment method where the customer instruction travels one route while the funds travel separately as a bank to bank cover message.

In a serial payment, the payment instruction and the funds travel together through the same chain of banks. The ordering bank sends an MT103 to the next bank, which sends it on, and so on until the beneficiary bank receives it. The money moves with the message at each hop, as a debit and credit on nostro accounts. In a cover payment, the two are separated. The ordering bank sends the MT103 directly to the beneficiary bank, so the beneficiary bank knows the payment is coming, while the funds move separately through a different chain of banks using an MT202 COV, a cover payment message. The beneficiary bank credits its customer when the cover arrives, or earlier if it trusts the ordering bank.

Cover payments exist because they are faster and often cheaper. The instruction reaches the beneficiary bank immediately, so it can start its screening and prepare the credit, while the cover settles through the cheapest or fastest route available. A serial payment must wait for each bank in the chain to process the message and move the funds before the next bank sees it. On a long chain, that adds a day or more. The cover route can use a different set of banks from the instruction route, which matters when the ordering bank has a cheap dollar correspondent but the beneficiary bank is in a country where the ordering bank has no presence at all.

The problem with cover payments is transparency. In the original design, the MT202 COV carried only the settlement information: the amount, the currency, the ordering bank and the beneficiary bank. It did not carry the underlying customer details, so the intermediary banks moving the cover could not see who was actually sending or receiving the money. That gap was exploited for money laundering, because the banks moving the funds had no way to screen the real parties. The issue became public and the industry had to fix it.

The fix is a set of transparency rules that every bank must follow. The industry transparency principles require the MT202 COV to carry the ordering customer and beneficiary information from the underlying MT103, and require the cover to be linked to the underlying payment by reference. SWIFT enforces this in its message rules: a cover message that does not carry the underlying party information must be rejected by the receiving bank. The intermediary bank must be able to screen the real ordering customer and the real beneficiary, not just the two banks named in the cover. A payment hub that generates a cover must therefore copy the customer details from the MT103 into the MT202 COV, and an operations team that receives a cover without those details must stop it.

The choice between serial and cover depends on the corridor and the value. Serial is the default for most payments and is mandatory in some corridors, particularly where the regulator requires the full payment chain to be visible. Cover is used when the ordering bank and the beneficiary bank have no direct account relationship and the cover route is materially faster or cheaper than the serial route. High-value payments and payments to certain jurisdictions are usually sent serial, because the beneficiary bank wants to see the full chain before it credits the funds. Low-value commercial payments are the typical cover case, where the speed and cost saving matter more than the chain visibility.

A worked example makes the trade-off concrete. A German exporter pays a Brazilian importer 250,000 euros. The serial route runs through three banks, takes two days, and costs 45 euros in fees. The cover route sends the MT103 directly to the Brazilian bank and moves the cover through two banks in one day for 30 euros. The Brazilian bank sees the instruction immediately and can tell its customer the payment is coming, but it will not release the funds until the cover arrives, unless it has a credit line with the ordering bank. If the cover is delayed, the customer waits even though the instruction arrived on time. That is the risk the ordering bank accepts when it chooses cover.

For a business analyst, cover handling is a defined set of requirements. The payment hub must link the MT103 and the MT202 COV by reference, so an investigation can follow both legs. It must copy the underlying customer details into the cover message, and it must validate that an incoming cover carries those details before processing it. The reconciliation must match the cover leg to the underlying payment, and the exception queue must show both legs together. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a cover payment arrives with the underlying customer details stripped out, and the system rejects it instead of processing it. That test proves the transparency control works.
