Key terms

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

UCP 600: Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, revision 600, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs documentary credits.

Capture: The step in a card transaction where the merchant collects the authorised amount from the issuer, usually when goods are shipped.

Exercise

An importer in London asks Barclays to issue a commercial credit for 800,000 dollars for an exporter in Shanghai. The same importer holds a standby for 300,000 dollars from a supplier. Decide which instrument is the payment method and which is the guarantee, and state what triggers each one.

Use the purpose, trigger and usage differences in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Name the instrument used to pay the exporter, the instrument held as security, and the trigger for a drawing under each.

Correct result. The commercial credit is the payment instrument: it is drawn when the exporter ships the goods and presents the bill of lading, invoice and certificate of origin. The standby is the guarantee: it is drawn only on a demand stating the supplier defaulted, and the importer hopes never to present it.

Why. Confusing the two instruments means using the wrong one and paying the wrong price for the wrong protection. Purpose and trigger decide which instrument fits.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A commercial letter of credit is a payment instrument. It is the normal way the seller expects to be paid. An importer in London asks Barclays to issue a commercial credit for 800,000 dollars in favour of an exporter in Shanghai. When the exporter ships the goods and presents the bill of lading, the commercial invoice, and the packing list, the bank pays. Payment is the expected outcome, and the documents prove that the goods were shipped.

The credit exists to make payment happen, not to punish a failure. A standby letter of credit is a guarantee instrument. Payment is the exception, not the expectation. The beneficiary hopes never to draw on it, because a drawing means the underlying deal has gone wrong. An importer may hold a standby for 300,000 dollars from a supplier, and may never present it if the supplier delivers on time.

The standby protects the beneficiary if the supplier fails to deliver, fails to repay an advance, or fails to honour a warranty. The documents in a standby usually prove default, not shipment. The documents differ sharply between the two instruments. A commercial credit calls for shipping documents, such as a bill of lading, an airway bill, an insurance certificate, and a certificate of origin. A standby calls for a simple demand, often a signed statement that the applicant has defaulted, plus a copy of an unpaid invoice or a copy of the underlying contract.

A standby for a construction project may require only a written demand stating that the contractor failed to complete the work by 30 June 2026, without any shipping document at all. The parties use the two instruments for different risks. A commercial credit protects the seller against the risk that the buyer will not pay after the goods are shipped. A standby protects the beneficiary against the risk that the applicant will not perform an obligation, which may be payment, delivery, or completion of work.

A bank in Frankfurt may issue a commercial credit to pay for machinery, and a standby to guarantee that the machinery supplier will refund a 200,000 euro advance if it fails to deliver. The same bank may issue both for the same deal, for different purposes. The cost and the accounting treatment also differ. A standby often costs less in handling charges because the document check is simpler, but the bank still takes credit risk on the applicant for the full amount until expiry. A commercial credit involves more document examination and often a discounting or negotiation step.

Both instruments appear as contingent liabilities on the issuing bank books until they expire or are drawn. A treasurer who understands this distinction can choose the right instrument for each risk, instead of using a commercial credit where a standby would be cheaper and simpler.
