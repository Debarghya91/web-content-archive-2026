Standby vs Commercial Letter of Credit

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain standby vs commercial letter of credit. A commercial letter of credit is a payment instrument.

Why it matters. This matters because cross-border payments involve more banks, more charges, more time and more ways to fail than any domestic payment. Getting the chain right is the job.

Chapter objective: Learner explains what a standby letter of credit is, distinguishes it from a commercial letter of credit, and describes issuance, notification, drawing and expiry. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Commercial letter of credit
  • Standby letter of credit
  • Documents differing sharply between the two instruments
  • Two instruments for different risks
  • Same bank issuing both instruments for one deal, different purposes
  • Cheaper and simpler

Key terms

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

UCP 600: Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, revision 600, the set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that governs documentary credits.

Capture: The step in a card transaction where the merchant collects the authorised amount from the issuer, usually when goods are shipped.

Lesson 3 of 7, Standby Letter of Credit, Narration 2:51

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A commercial letter of credit is a payment instrument. It is the normal way the seller expects to be paid. An importer in London asks Barclays to issue a commercial credit for 800,000 dollars in favour of an exporter in Shanghai. When the exporter ships the goods and presents the bill of lading, the commercial invoice, and the packing list, the bank pays. Payment is the expected outcome, and the documents prove that the goods were shipped. The credit exists to make payment happen, not to punish a failure. A standby letter of credit is a guarantee instrument. Payment is the exception, not the expectation. The beneficiary hopes never to draw on it, because a drawing means the underlying deal has gone wrong. An importer may hold a standby for 300,000 dollars from a supplier, and may never present it if the supplier delivers on time. The standby protects the beneficiary if the supplier fails to deliver, fails to repay an advance, or fails to honour a warranty. The documents in a standby usually prove default, not shipment. The documents differ sharply between the two instruments. A commercial credit calls for shipping documents, such as a bill of lading, an airway bill, an insurance certificate, and a certificate of origin. A standby calls for a simple demand, often a signed statement that the applicant has defaulted, plus a copy of an unpaid invoice or a copy of the underlying contract. A standby for a construction project may require only a written demand stating that the contractor failed to complete the work by 30 June 2026, without any shipping document at all. The parties use the two instruments for different risks. A commercial credit protects the seller against the risk that the buyer will not pay after the goods are shipped. A standby protects the beneficiary against the risk that the applicant will not perform an obligation, which may be payment, delivery, or completion of work. A bank in Frankfurt may issue a commercial credit to pay for machinery, and a standby to guarantee that the machinery supplier will refund a 200,000 euro advance if it fails to deliver. The same bank may issue both for the same deal, for different purposes. The cost and the accounting treatment also differ. A standby often costs less in handling charges because the document check is simpler, but the bank still takes credit risk on the applicant for the full amount until expiry. A commercial credit involves more document examination and often a discounting or negotiation step. Both instruments appear as contingent liabilities on the issuing bank books until they expire or are drawn. A treasurer who understands this distinction can choose the right instrument for each risk, instead of using a commercial credit where a standby would be cheaper and simpler.

A standby letter of credit and a commercial letter of credit look similar, because both are bank payment undertakings issued in the form of a letter of credit, governed by UCP 600 or ISP98, and drawn by presentation of documents. But their purpose, their trigger, their usage, and their cost are different, and an SME must be able to explain the difference clearly, because a client who confuses them will use the wrong instrument and will pay the wrong price for the wrong protection.

The purpose is the first difference. A commercial letter of credit is intended to be used as the normal means of payment. The exporter ships the goods, presents the documents, and is paid by the bank. The letter of credit is the payment mechanism, and it is expected to be drawn. A standby letter of credit is intended NOT to be used. It is security, held by the beneficiary in case the applicant defaults. The beneficiary hopes never to draw it, and the applicant hopes never to have it drawn. The standby is a guarantee, and the commercial letter of credit is a payment instrument. That distinction drives everything else.

The trigger is the second difference. A commercial letter of credit is triggered by the presentation of documents that show the goods were shipped, such as the bill of lading, the commercial invoice, and the certificate of origin. The bank examines the documents for compliance with the letter of credit, and pays if they comply. A standby is triggered by a demand stating that the applicant has defaulted, plus any documents the standby requires, which are normally a written demand and sometimes a certificate of default or a copy of an unpaid invoice. The bank does not examine shipping documents, because there are no goods to ship. It examines the demand, and pays if the demand complies with the standby terms.

The usage frequency is the third difference. A commercial letter of credit is drawn in most cases, because the exporter ships and presents the documents as part of the normal trade. A standby is drawn in a small minority of cases, because most applicants perform their obligations, and the beneficiary has no reason to draw. The bank therefore prices the two instruments differently. A commercial letter of credit carries an issuance fee and a negotiation fee based on the amount and the period. A standby carries an issuance fee, often a percentage per annum on the amount, and a much lower expectation of payment. The standby fee is a guarantee fee, and it is charged for the period the standby is outstanding, whether or not it is drawn.

The documents are the fourth difference. A commercial letter of credit requires a full set of shipping documents, and the bank examines each one for compliance with the UCP 600 articles on documents. A standby requires a demand and, at most, a small number of supporting documents. The examination is simpler, and the risk of a discrepancy is lower, because there are fewer documents to examine. But the consequence of a discrepancy is the same, the bank will refuse to pay if the demand does not comply with the standby terms. A beneficiary who presents a demand with a wrong amount, a wrong reference, or a missing signature will have the demand refused, and will need to present a corrected demand before the expiry date.

The accounting treatment is the fifth difference. A commercial letter of credit, when issued, creates a contingent liability for the issuing bank, and the bank records it as an off-balance sheet item. A standby also creates a contingent liability, and it is also recorded off-balance sheet. But the capital treatment differs, because the risk weight for a standby that guarantees performance is different from the risk weight for a commercial letter of credit that finances trade. The bank capital team must classify the standby correctly, and the applicant must understand that the standby consumes its credit limit, even though it may never be drawn. A standby for USD 1,000,000 uses USD 1,000,000 of the applicant credit limit, just as a loan would, and the applicant cannot use that limit for another purpose while the standby is outstanding.

Here is a side by side comparison. Commercial letter of credit, purpose, payment for goods, trigger, presentation of shipping documents, expected usage, usually drawn, documents, full set of shipping documents, cost, issuance and negotiation fees, risk, bank pays on complying documents. Standby letter of credit, purpose, security for performance or payment, trigger, demand stating default, expected usage, rarely drawn, documents, demand plus at most a few supporting documents, cost, guarantee fee per annum, risk, bank pays on complying demand. An SME who can reproduce this comparison, and explain each line, can advise a client on which instrument to use, and can explain why a standby is cheaper than a commercial letter of credit for a guarantee, and why a commercial letter of credit is the right instrument for a payment.

For a business analyst, the requirement must state which instrument is being built. The requirement should say whether the system handles a commercial letter of credit, a standby letter of credit, or both, and it should state the differences in the fields, the documents, the examination, and the fees. A system built for a commercial letter of credit that is used for a standby will ask for shipping documents that do not exist, and will fail. A system built for a standby that is used for a commercial letter of credit will not capture the shipping documents, and will fail. The instrument type is a fundamental design decision, and it must be stated at the start of the requirement.
