Error correction in ledgers: reversals, adjustments and the audit trail

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain error correction in ledgers and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Error correction is where accounting discipline is tested, and the analyst must enforce the reversal rule.

Chapter objective: Learner explains how a bank's ledgers record every payment, distinguishes customer accounts, internal accounts, nostro and vostro ledgers, suspense and transit accounts, writes the exact general ledger postings for outgoing and incoming payments, and explains intraday processing, value dating, subledger feeds, reconciliation and error correction, with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Cardinal rule of ledger correction
  • Payment: credited to customer B
  • Foreign exchange rate
  • Correction sign-off from financial controller
  • High-value adjustments needing senior approval, two approvers above the threshold
  • Last reviewed 8 November 2026

Key terms

Suspense account: A temporary account used to hold funds or entries that cannot yet be posted to their final account. Banks use suspense accounts for unidentified receipts, pending investigations and items awaiting...

Transit account: An internal account used to hold funds temporarily while they move between systems or accounts. Transit accounts record the movement of money during settlement, currency conversion or internal...

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

The cardinal rule of ledger correction is that a posted entry is never deleted and never edited. It is corrected by a new entry that reverses it or adjusts it, and the new entry references the old one. This rule exists because the ledger is a record of what happened, and deleting an entry would destroy the evidence that it happened. If a payment of 10,000 pounds was posted to the wrong customer, the bank does not go back and change the entry. It posts a reversal, which is the exact opposite entry, and then posts the payment correctly. The ledger then shows three entries: the original mistake, the reversal, and the correct payment, and anyone who reads it can follow the whole story. A reversal is the exact opposite of the original entry, with the same amount and the same value date, and it is linked to the original by reference. A payment was credited to customer B instead of customer A. The reversal debits customer B by 10,000 pounds and credits a suspense or transit account by 10,000 pounds, undoing the original credit. Then the correct entry is posted: debit the suspense account 10,000 pounds and credit customer A 10,000 pounds. The suspense account nets to zero, customer B is back where they started, and customer A has the money. If customer B has already spent the money, their account goes overdrawn, and the bank has a recovery process to follow, but the ledger still tells the truth about what happened. An adjustment is a partial correction, used when the original entry was right in direction but wrong in amount. A fee of 25 pounds was charged when the correct fee was 15 pounds. The adjustment is a credit of 10 pounds to the customer and a debit of 10 pounds to fee income. A foreign exchange rate was applied incorrectly, and the customer was charged 100 pounds too much. The adjustment credits the customer 100 pounds and debits foreign exchange income 100 pounds. An adjustment must state the reason, the original entry it corrects, and the person who approved it, and it must be posted with the same value date as the original where possible, so that the interest calculation is not distorted. Value date corrections need special care, because they change interest. A payment that should have been value-dated on Monday was value-dated on Thursday, so the customer lost three days of interest on the balance. The correction is a back-valued adjustment, and the system must recalculate the interest for the whole period and post the difference. Back-valued corrections need four-eyes approval and are reported to finance, because moving value across a period boundary changes a period that may already have been reported. A correction that moves value across a month end needs additional approval, and a correction that moves value across a year end needs sign-off from the financial controller. What cannot be changed after posting is as important as what can. The original entry, its timestamp, the person who made it, and the person who authorised it are all fixed. The audit trail is append-only, which means new records can be added but existing records cannot be altered or deleted. This is enforced by the system, not by policy, and it is tested by internal audit and by external auditors. The reason is fraud prevention: a user who could edit history could hide a theft by changing the ledger to match the stolen money. Segregation of duties supports the same goal. The person who posts a manual journal cannot approve it, and the person who approves it cannot be the person who benefits from it. High-value adjustments need senior approval, and adjustments above a threshold need two approvers. Retention and retrieval complete the control. Ledger records are kept for at least five years to satisfy anti-money-laundering law, and usually for seven to ten years to satisfy tax and audit requirements. The audit trail must be retrievable, which means the bank must be able to produce the full history of an account, including every reversal and adjustment, within a reasonable time when the regulator or an auditor asks. For a business analyst, the requirements must state the correction methods, the approval thresholds, the segregation of duties, and the retention period. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a payment is posted to the wrong account, reversed, and reposted correctly, and a test where a user without approval rights tries to post a manual journal and is blocked. Further reading: the International Auditing and Assurance Standards Board guidance on audit trails; the Basel Committee principles on internal controls; the Financial Reporting Council guidance on the maintenance of accounting records.
