What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain access controls and segregation of duties inside a payments operation and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. Segregation of duties is the oldest fraud control in banking, and every bypass is an audit finding.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the control framework that keeps payments safe, distinguishes fraud typologies, transaction monitoring, SAR and STR filing, KYC and CDD, access controls, audit trails and scheme rulebooks, and applies each control to realistic payment operations with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • Access control in a payments operation
  • Threshold for dual authorisation
  • Person who can post
  • Payment release authority
  • Leavers whose access
  • Evidence kept for the examiner: access control tested first

Key terms

Authorisation: Approval by the issuer for a card transaction to proceed, confirming the card is valid and funds or credit are available.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Break: A difference found during reconciliation between two records that should agree, such as a bank statement and a ledger.

Exercise

A payment of 500,000 pounds needs to be released urgently and the second approver is on leave. Work out the correct handling.

Use the rules, numbers and worked examples in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Work through the scenario and state the outcome with the reason.

Correct result. The payment waits for a second authorised approver, or a pre-approved deputy approver releases it; a single person must never release it alone, even under time pressure.

Why. Urgency is the classic reason segregation of duties is bypassed, and it is exactly when the control matters most.

Access control in a payments operation answers a simple question: who can do what, and who approved it. The principle is least privilege, which means each person has only the access their job needs, and nothing more. An operations analyst who processes exceptions does not need the ability to change a payment limit. A team leader who approves payments does not need the ability to create a new payee. A system administrator who maintains the server does not need the ability to release a payment. Every access right must be justified by a job, approved by a manager, and reviewed regularly. An access right that nobody can justify is a risk, and a review that nobody performs is a gap. The four-eyes principle, maker and checker, is the control that stops a single person from moving money alone. One person creates or amends a payment, and a different person approves it. The principle applies to the payment itself, to the payee, to the limit, and to the release. The threshold for dual authorisation is set by value and by risk: a payment above a set amount needs two authorisers, a payment to a new payee needs two authorisers, and a payment outside the normal pattern needs two authorisers. The system must enforce the rule, so that the same person cannot approve their own work even if they try, and the enforcement must cover all channels, including the emergency procedures, because the emergency route is the one a fraudster will use. Segregation of duties is the formal version of the same idea, expressed as a matrix of conflicting duties. The matrix lists the duties, such as creating a payee, approving a payee, releasing a payment, amending a limit, and reconciling the ledger, and it marks which combinations a single person must never hold. A person who can create a payee and release a payment can pay themselves. A person who can amend a limit and approve their own amendment can raise their own authority. A person who can post to the ledger and reconcile it can hide a theft. The matrix must be built, approved, and enforced by the system, so that granting a new access right automatically checks for a conflict and blocks it or escalates it. Privileged access is the highest-risk access of all, because an administrator can change the system itself. Administrator accounts must be separate from daily accounts, so that a person who administers the payment hub does not use the same login to browse email. Administrator sessions must be recorded, so that every change is attributable to a person and a time. Break-glass accounts, which give emergency access when the normal system is unavailable, must be held under dual control, used only in a declared emergency, and reviewed after every use. A break-glass account that is used and never reviewed is a hole in the control framework, and an examiner will find it. Payment release authority is the operational expression of access control. Each role has a limit by value, by currency, and by payment type. A junior analyst can release payments up to 10,000 pounds. A senior analyst can release up to 100,000 pounds. A team leader can release up to 1,000,000 pounds. Above that, two authorisers are required, and the second must be senior to the first. Standing instructions, such as a regular salary run, have their own authority, and a change to a standing instruction needs the same approval as a new one. The limits must be in the system, not in a document, because a limit in a document is a suggestion and a limit in a system is a control. The failure modes are consistent and they repeat across every bank. Shared logins, where a team uses one account because it is faster, destroy accountability, because no one can prove who did what. Dormant access, where a person who changed roles keeps the old access, creates a conflict that nobody notices. Leavers whose access is not removed on the day they leave can still move money. A team leader who approves their own payments because the second authoriser is on holiday defeats the four-eyes rule. Each of these is a finding, and together they show a control framework that exists on paper and not in practice. The remedy is automation: access reviews on a schedule, automatic removal on role change and on leaving, and system enforcement of maker and checker. For a business analyst, the access control work is a role matrix and a set of system rules. The role matrix must list every role, every permission, and every limit. The segregation of duties matrix must list every conflicting combination. The system must enforce both, and must log every grant, every change, and every use of privileged access. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a person tries to approve their own payment and is blocked, a test where granting a conflicting access right is blocked or escalated, a test where a leaver access is removed on the leaving day, and a test where a break-glass use is reviewed after the event. The evidence must be kept, because access control is one of the first things an examiner tests, and one of the easiest to fail. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: the Basel Committee principles for the sound management of operational risk; the National Institute of Standards and Technology guidance on access control; the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, Requirement 7; the European Banking Authority guidelines on internal governance.
