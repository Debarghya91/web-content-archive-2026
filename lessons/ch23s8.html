Correct result. A true match against a sanctions list stops the payment. A false positive is cleared with a reason recorded. An alert that is escalated becomes a case, and a case that meets the threshold becomes a suspicious activity report.

Why. Screening is a control, not a suggestion. A missed true match is a regulatory breach, and an unexplained clearance is an audit finding.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Every payment a bank sends or receives must be checked against sanctions lists: the lists of persons, entities, and countries that the law forbids doing business with. Banks build their own screening systems, but SWIFT also offers a screening service, and many banks use it as a complement or as a safety net. SWIFT Sanctions Screening is a hosted service that screens messages against sanctions lists as they pass through the SWIFT infrastructure. It does not replace the bank own compliance programme, but it adds a screening layer at the point where messages enter and leave the bank.

The service screens FIN and MX payment messages, and other message types, against the major sanctions lists: the United Nations list, the European Union list, the United States OFAC lists, and national lists as configured. Screening is name and data matching: the names, addresses, and identifiers in the message are compared against the list entries, using matching rules that allow for spelling variations, transliteration differences, and partial matches. The lists and the matching rules are configurable, so a bank can screen against the lists that apply to its jurisdictions and tune the sensitivity to its risk appetite.

Deployment is hosted. The service is run by SWIFT, and the bank connects it to its message flow, typically through the SWIFT interface, or through an application programming interface or file transfer for bulk screening. Screening happens as messages pass through, in real time for payment flows that cannot wait, or in batch for flows where a short delay is acceptable. The hosted model means the bank does not maintain the list data itself: SWIFT updates the lists as the authorities publish changes, and the bank benefits from the update without an internal data feed project.

When screening finds a possible match, it raises an alert for the compliance team. The team investigates: is this a true match, a false positive, or a near match that needs more information? False positives are tuned over time, through matching thresholds, whitelists for known good names, and feedback from investigations. The tuning matters, because an over sensitive screen floods operations with alerts and slows payments, while an under sensitive screen misses real matches. The alert workflow, the investigation, and the decision are the bank responsibility: the service finds the possible matches, and the bank decides what they mean.

A companion capability is Sanctions Testing. When a sanctions list changes, the bank needs to know what the change will do to its alert volumes before the change goes live. Sanctions Testing runs the updated lists against historic message data, so the bank can see which messages would have matched, how many new alerts the change would create, and whether the tuning needs adjustment. Testing a list change before deploying it prevents the morning when operations arrives to find a thousand new alerts and no plan for working them.

The limits of the service are as important as its capabilities. It screens what it sees: the messages that pass through it. It does not screen customers at onboarding, it does not monitor transactions for money laundering patterns, and it does not make compliance decisions. The bank remains responsible for its sanctions programme, for customer screening, and for transaction monitoring. The service is one control in a layered defence, alongside the bank own screening, its customer due diligence, and its transaction monitoring. A business analyst specifying screening must place each control in its layer, and never assume that one hosted service covers the whole obligation.
