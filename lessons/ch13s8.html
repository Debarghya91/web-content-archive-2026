Transcript and full text

Show transcript

Nostro accounts are managed in the core banking system as a special class of account, with one record per currency per correspondent bank. Each record holds the account number, the correspondent, the currency, the current balance, the available balance, and the intraday limits agreed with the correspondent. Every payment that uses that nostro posts a debit or credit to the account in real time, and the balance is updated in the same transaction as the payment status change, so the two can never drift apart.

A large international bank may hold 200 to 500 nostro accounts across 50 or more currencies, and the core system must keep every balance accurate to the cent at all times. Real time balance tracking relies on event driven updates and a fast balance cache. When a payment is released, the system reserves the funds immediately, reducing the available balance even before the payment leaves the bank. When the correspondent confirms settlement, the reservation becomes a final debit. This two step approach prevents the bank from overcommitting funds on a busy morning when hundreds of payments compete for the same account.

The balance cache is updated in milliseconds and is replicated to a standby site, so a failure in one data centre does not lose the latest position. Treasury teams watch a dashboard that shows every nostro balance, the reserved amount, and the headroom left for the rest of the day. Liquidity forecasting predicts how much money each nostro will need before the day ends. The forecast combines the payments already queued, the expected receipts from incoming payments, the cut off times of each currency, and historical patterns for the day of the week and the month.

A forecast run every 15 minutes projects the balance at each cut off and flags any account that will fall below its minimum threshold. If a shortfall is predicted, treasury can move funds between accounts, draw on a credit line, or delay lower priority payments. A typical forecast covers 5,000 to 20,000 expected payments per currency per day, and accuracy within 5 percent is considered good enough to act on with confidence. Choosing a correspondent is a routing decision based on cost, speed, cut off time, and the relationship with the correspondent.

The routing table ranks correspondents per currency and destination, with the cheapest and fastest option first, and the payment engine picks the best available option that can meet the value date. If the preferred correspondent has low liquidity or is approaching its cut off, the engine falls through to the next option automatically. The decision is logged with the reason, so operations can explain why a payment went through one bank rather than another. This dynamic routing keeps costs down, improves settlement speed, and spreads volume so no single correspondent becomes a bottleneck.

The nostro reconciliation engine matches the bank's own records against the statements received from each correspondent, usually once a day and intraday for high volume currencies. It matches on amount, currency, value date, and reference, and it auto matches 95 to 98 percent of items without human help. Unmatched items fall into exception queues, such as a payment the bank sent but the correspondent has not yet reported, or a charge the correspondent deducted that the bank did not expect. The engine also enforces cut off times across time zones, so a payment sent at 4 in the afternoon London time for a New York nostro must respect the US cut off, not the UK one.

A shared cut off calendar, stored in UTC, keeps every team working from the same clock.
