KYC

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain KYC and CDD and apply it to a real banking scenario.

Why it matters. CDD is the front door of every control downstream, and a weak onboarding decision poisons everything after it.

Chapter objective: Learner explains the control framework that keeps payments safe, distinguishes fraud typologies, transaction monitoring, SAR and STR filing, KYC and CDD, access controls, audit trails and scheme rulebooks, and applies each control to realistic payment operations with correct terminology.

Key learning points

  • KYC and CDD deciding who the bank does business with
  • Video-based identification
  • United Kingdom
  • Customer whose review
  • Failure modes in onboarding
  • Gates enforced by the system, not by a skippable checklist

Key terms

KYC: Know your customer, the process of verifying a customer identity, understanding their business and assessing their risk before opening an account or providing services.

Correct result. Identify the company and its directors, verify the registration, identify the beneficial owners, understand the business and expected payment volumes, and set the risk rating.

Why. Without beneficial ownership the bank cannot know who really controls the account, which is the most common CDD failure.

Know Your Customer, KYC, and customer due diligence, CDD, are the controls that decide who the bank does business with, and they are the first line of defence in payments. KYC is the identification and verification of the customer. CDD is the wider assessment: understanding the nature and purpose of the relationship, building a risk profile, and monitoring it over time. Enhanced due diligence, EDD, applies where the risk is higher. The three are a ladder, and the bank must be able to show which rung each customer is on and why. A customer who is onboarded with simplified due diligence because they look low risk, and who is never reassessed, is a gap. Identity verification has changed from documents to data. A passport or a driving licence is still the basis, but the verification now includes checking the document against a database, checking the person against a biometric, and checking the liveness of the person presenting it. Video-based identification, permitted under the RBI Know Your Customer Master Direction and under European rules, requires a live video call, a liveness check, and a recording of the session. The recording must be kept, because the supervisor can ask to see it. The failure mode is a verification that looks complete but is not: a document that was checked against a database that was out of date, a liveness check that was bypassed, or a video that was recorded but never reviewed. Beneficial ownership is the part of CDD that takes the most effort and causes the most findings. The rule in most regimes is that the bank must identify every person who owns 25 percent or more of a legal entity customer, and every person who controls it. Control is the harder part, because a person can control a company without owning a large share, through voting rights, through a nominee arrangement, or through a chain of holding companies. The United Kingdom has a public register of persons with significant control. The European Union requires member states to hold beneficial ownership registers. The United States requires most companies to report beneficial ownership information to FinCEN. A payment firm onboarding a corporate customer must collect the ownership information, verify it against the registers, and record it, because a customer whose ownership is unknown is a customer whose risk is unknown. Ongoing monitoring is what makes CDD a control rather than a one-off exercise. The customer risk profile must be reviewed when something changes: a new country of operation, a new product, a large payment outside the expected pattern, a change in ownership, or a negative news item. The review cycle is set by risk: high-risk customers are reviewed annually, medium-risk every two or three years, low-risk less often. The review must be recorded, with the date, the person who did it, and the outcome. A customer whose review is overdue is a finding, and a portfolio with hundreds of overdue reviews is an enforcement action waiting to happen. Enhanced due diligence applies to the highest-risk customers, and the requirements are specific. A politically exposed person, their family members and their close associates must be identified, and the relationship must be approved by senior management. The source of wealth and the source of funds must be established, which means understanding where the customer's money came from and where the money for this payment came from. A customer in a high-risk jurisdiction, or a customer with a complex structure that has no clear commercial purpose, must be asked harder questions, and the answers must be documented. The bank must be able to show the questions it asked and the answers it received, because a file that says enhanced due diligence was performed, without saying what was done, is not evidence. The failure modes in onboarding are well known and they repeat. An incomplete record, where a document was requested but never received, and the account was opened anyway. An expired document that was never renewed. A shell company with no real activity that was onboarded because the paperwork looked complete. A customer whose risk rating was set to low at onboarding and never changed, even though their behaviour changed completely. Each of these is a finding on its own, and together they show a control that is not operating. The remedy is not more paperwork, it is a system that blocks onboarding until the required documents are present, that flags expired documents automatically, and that triggers a review when behaviour changes. For a business analyst, the onboarding work is a workflow with gates. The workflow must collect the required documents, verify them, score the risk, and decide the due diligence level before the account is opened. The gates must be enforced by the system, not by a checklist that can be skipped. The acceptance criteria must include a test where an onboarding with a missing document is blocked, a test where a beneficial owner above 25 percent is identified and verified, a test where a politically exposed person is escalated to senior management, and a test where a behaviour change triggers a review. The evidence for each test must be kept, because the examiner will ask for the file, not for a description of the process. Last reviewed 8 November 2026. Further reading: Financial Action Task Force recommendations 10 and 22; Directive (EU) 2015/849, Articles 13 and 14; Reserve Bank of India Know Your Customer Master Direction; FinCEN customer due diligence rule, 31 CFR 1020.220.
