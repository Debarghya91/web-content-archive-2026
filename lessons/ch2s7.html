Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Creditor: The party that is owed money and receives a payment, such as a supplier, a seller or a service provider.

Exercise

Decide which settlement model fits the scenario in this lesson and explain why.

Use the settlement models, the liquidity trade-off and the finality rules in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Name the model, state when settlement becomes final, and explain what happens if the sending bank does not have enough liquidity.

Correct result. Real time gross settlement settles each payment individually in central bank money with immediate finality, but it needs liquidity for every payment. Deferred net settlement nets payments and settles the net position, which needs less liquidity but leaves settlement risk until the net settles.

Why. Settlement is where money actually moves. The model decides the risk, the liquidity need, and when the payment is truly final.

A domestic payment is one where the debtor and creditor accounts are held in the same country and usually in the same currency, and it is processed through a national clearing system. Domestic payments are typically faster and cheaper than cross border payments because they do not need correspondent banks, currency conversion, or multiple time zones. A cross border payment is one where the debtor and creditor are in different countries, or where the payment crosses a border even if the currency is the same. Cross border payments involve more parties, more compliance checks, and more scope for delay, deduction of charges, and foreign exchange differences. The customer experience is therefore very different, even when the payment message looks similar.

Retail payments are high volume, low value payments made by individuals and small businesses, such as card purchases, direct debits, and person to person transfers. They are processed in bulk, they rely on automation, and the cost per payment must be very low. Wholesale payments are low volume, high value payments made by banks, large corporates, and financial market participants, such as interbank settlements, securities settlements, and large corporate treasury payments. They are processed individually, often through a real time gross settlement system, and the focus is on finality, liquidity, and risk control rather than on low unit cost.

The distinction matters because the systems, the risks, and the regulations are different. A retail payment that fails affects one customer, while a wholesale payment that fails can affect market liquidity. A domestic retail system may settle the next day, while a wholesale system must settle in central bank money the same day. When you analyse a payment product, always state whether it is domestic or cross border, and whether it is retail or wholesale, because those two classifications decide the clearing path, the settlement model, the charges, and the regulatory expectations that apply.

Compare the same amount on two routes. A customer sends Rs 50,000 to another account in the same country. It travels through the national clearing system, settles the same day, and costs a few rupees. The same customer sends USD 10,000 to a supplier in Singapore. It travels through correspondent banks, is screened at each step, may lose USD 25 in correspondent charges, and arrives in one to two days, with the exchange rate applied along the way. The message looks similar, but the cost, the speed, and the number of parties are completely different. Now compare retail and wholesale with the same logic. A EUR 50 card purchase is one of millions netted together and settled in a batch, where the cost per payment must be a fraction of a cent. A EUR 50 million interbank payment settles individually through the central bank in minutes, where the cost is irrelevant next to the risk of it failing. The classification decides everything that follows.

An analyst should state the classification of every payment product in the first paragraph of its specification, domestic or cross border, retail or wholesale. Those two labels decide the clearing path, the settlement model, the charges, the cut off times, and the regulatory expectations. A retail product that is accidentally built on a wholesale settlement model will be too expensive to run, and a wholesale product built on a retail batch model will be too slow and too risky. The test pack should include the boundary cases, a payment in the same currency but across a border, which is cross border even though no conversion is needed, and a large retail payment that approaches wholesale values, to confirm that the system routes it correctly and applies the right controls.
