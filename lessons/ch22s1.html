Key terms

Chargeback: A dispute raised by a cardholder through the issuer to reverse a card payment, for example for fraud, non delivery or a duplicate charge.

Issuer: A bank or licensed institution that issues payment cards to customers and holds their accounts. It authorises transactions, debits cardholder accounts, and manages credit limits and fraud controls.

Show transcript

Card payments face a basic problem. The card itself is not present in an online transaction, and the merchant cannot see the person paying or check their signature. A fraudster who has obtained a card number, expiry date and security code can use them to buy goods online, and the genuine cardholder may only discover the fraud when they check their statement. This card not present fraud has been the fastest growing type of card fraud for many years, and it is the reason the industry has invested heavily in authentication, tokenization and dispute processes.

The challenge is to add security without destroying the customer experience. Every extra step at checkout, every password to remember and every code to type, increases the chance that a genuine customer will abandon their purchase. Merchants measure this abandonment carefully, because a checkout that is too strict loses sales. Issuers face the same trade off, because a card that is declined too often will be replaced by a competitor's card in the customer's wallet. The best security solutions are therefore those that are invisible to most customers and only challenge the small minority of transactions that look risky.

This chapter covers three technologies and processes that work together to solve this problem. Three D Secure provides authentication, confirming that the person paying is the genuine cardholder. Tokenization replaces the real card number with a surrogate value, so that the real number is never exposed. And the chargeback process provides a remedy when fraud or an error still occurs, giving the cardholder a way to recover their money and giving the merchant a fair chance to defend the transaction. Together they form the trust framework that makes card payments work at scale.

Put numbers on both sides of the trade off. A merchant doing Rs 10 crore of online sales a month with fraud running at 0.1 percent of value loses Rs 1 lakh a month to fraud. If it adds a clumsy authentication step that stops the fraud but causes 5 percent of genuine customers to abandon their baskets, it loses Rs 50 lakh of sales to save Rs 1 lakh, which is a bad bargain. The right answer is risk based. Challenge only the transactions that look risky, and let the rest through silently. A fraudster who has bought a stolen card number, expiry date, and security code can place an order for Rs 25,000 in minutes, and the genuine cardholder may not notice until the statement arrives. Without authentication, that Rs 25,000 becomes a chargeback the merchant must absorb. With authentication, the liability generally shifts to the issuer, because the issuer confirmed the cardholder. That shift is worth more to the merchant than the fraud loss itself, because it turns an unpredictable cost into a predictable one.

An analyst writes the security requirements as rules with thresholds, not as vague intentions. The risk engine should score each transaction on device, history, amount, and delivery address, and challenge only above an agreed score. The monitoring should track two numbers every day. The fraud rate and the abandonment rate, because improving one at the expense of the other is not an improvement. The chargeback process should map every reason code to an owner and a response time, because a chargeback answered late is a chargeback lost. And the requirements should state what happens when the authentication service itself is down, because a security control that blocks all payments when it fails is worse than the fraud it prevents.
