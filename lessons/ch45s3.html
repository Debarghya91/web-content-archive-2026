Pricing, Fees, Interchange and Commercial Models

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain pricing, fees, interchange and commercial models. The cost of a payment has several layers, and a product manager must understand each one before setting a price.

Why it matters. The manager chooses the model that matches how the customer values the service and how predictable the bank needs its revenue to be.

Chapter objective: Learner applies product management to payments, defining a product vision, roadmap, metrics, and pricing, and writes a product brief for a new payment feature. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key learning points

  • Several layers and product manager
  • Payment that looks cheap
  • Scheme fees
  • Commercial models
  • Model matching customer value and revenue predictability
  • Pricing: revisited every year

Key terms

Interchange: A fee paid by the acquirer to the issuer for each card transaction, set by the card scheme. It compensates the issuer for handling the transaction, funding the interest free period and managing fraud...

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Scheme fee: A fee charged by a card scheme to issuers and acquirers for using its network, rules and brand. Scheme fees cover processing, clearing, compliance and fraud services.

Practise this lesson

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a 100 pounds payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full amount. Under SHA the sender pays the sending charges and the beneficiary pays the receiving charges. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives the amount less all fees.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

The cost of a payment has several layers, and a product manager must understand each one before setting a price. The first layer is the scheme or clearing cost, which is the fee paid to the payment system, for example 2 pence for a Faster Payments transaction in the United Kingdom. The second layer is the processing cost, which covers the payment hub, the core banking system and the staff who handle exceptions, often 8 to 12 pence per payment. The third layer is fraud and risk, which covers losses from authorised push payment fraud and the cost of screening, often 3 to 5 pence per payment. The fourth layer is the cost of sales, support and technology, which can add another 10 pence. A payment that looks cheap at 5 pence can cost the bank 25 pence in total. Interchange is the fee paid between banks on card payments, and it is often misunderstood. When a customer pays 100 pounds with a debit card, the merchant bank pays an interchange fee to the cardholder bank, typically around 0.2 percent for a consumer debit card in Europe, which is 20 pence on a 100 pound purchase. That fee is capped by regulation in Europe, but it is higher in other markets. The merchant also pays a scheme fee to Visa or Mastercard, and a merchant service charge to its acquirer. A product manager who builds a card product must model all three fees, because a small change in interchange can turn a profitable product into a loss making product when volumes reach millions of transactions a month. Scheme fees are the charges set by the card schemes and the payment systems for using their network. Visa and Mastercard charge a scheme fee on every transaction, plus additional fees for cross border transactions, currency conversion and premium services. A cross border card payment of 500 euros may attract a scheme fee of 0.4 percent plus a currency conversion fee of 1 percent, which the customer sees as part of the total charge. For account to account payments, the scheme fee is the charge from the clearing system, such as the fee for a SEPA Instant payment through the Eurosystem. The product manager must track scheme fee changes, because the schemes publish new fee schedules twice a year and a change can affect the margin on millions of payments. Commercial models describe how the bank charges the customer, and the choice depends on the segment and the product. The simplest model is pay per transaction, where a small business pays 35 pence for each instant payment and nothing when it does not use the service. A second model is a monthly subscription, where a company pays 250 pounds a month for up to 5,000 payments, with extra payments charged at 20 pence each. A third model is a bundled price, where payments are included in a wider package with the current account and lending. A fourth model is revenue share, where the bank and a partner such as a software company share the fee. The manager chooses the model that matches how the customer values the service and how predictable the bank needs its revenue to be. Testing a price before launch is essential, because a price that looks right in a spreadsheet can fail with real customers. The manager tests the price with ten or twenty customers in discovery interviews, asking whether they would pay 35 pence, 50 pence or 75 pence for an instant payment, and what they would do if the price rose. The manager also runs a pilot with a real price for three months, watching the take up rate and the revenue per customer. If the pilot shows that only 5 percent of customers use the service at 75 pence, but 25 percent use it at 35 pence, the lower price may earn more total revenue. Pricing is revisited every year, because costs, competition and regulation all change.
