Why take this lesson

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain cheque. A cheque is a written instruction from an account holder, called the drawer, to the bank that holds the account, called the drawee, telling the bank to pay a stated amount to a named person or business, called the payee. For a business analyst, a cheque is a pull payment, which means the payee pulls the money from the drawer account by presenting the cheque, rather than the drawer pushing the money to the payee.

Transcript and full text

Show transcript

A cheque is a written instruction from an account holder, called the drawer, to the bank that holds the account, called the drawee, telling the bank to pay a stated amount to a named person or business, called the payee. The drawer signs the cheque, writes the amount in words and figures, dates it and hands it to the payee, who then presents it to a bank to be paid. The cheque itself moves no money, because it is only an instruction, and the money moves later when the paying bank debits the drawer account and the collecting bank credits the payee account. A cheque for GBP 4,750 from a small business in Leeds to a supplier in Bristol is a promise that the bank will pay, provided the account holds enough funds and the cheque is valid.

Cheques still exist because they solve problems that electronic payments do not always solve. A small business can issue a cheque without knowing the supplier bank details, because only the supplier name is needed, and a cheque can be handed over in person as proof that payment has been made. Cheques also work without internet access, without a card terminal and without an online banking login, which matters for older customers, rural businesses and organisations with strict approval processes. Some payments, such as a deposit on a property, a gift, or a payment to a person who has no bank account details on file, are still made by cheque because the paper itself carries the instruction, the signature and the record.

Cheques are disappearing in some countries and holding on in others, and the pattern depends on habit, regulation and the alternatives available. In the United Kingdom, cheque volumes have fallen sharply since the 1990s, but millions are still written each year, and the image-based clearing system introduced in 2019 cut the clearing time from six working days to two. In the United States, cheques remain common for business payments, rent and payroll, while in the Netherlands and Scandinavia they have almost vanished. A business analyst should never assume cheques are gone, because a bank that closes its cheque service without warning will strand customers who still rely on paper, and a product owner must plan a migration path before removing the option.

For a business analyst, a cheque is a pull payment, which means the payee pulls the money from the drawer account by presenting the cheque, rather than the drawer pushing the money to the payee. That single fact changes the risk, the timing and the exception handling, because the drawer does not control when the cheque is presented, and the payee does not know whether the funds are there until the cheque clears. The ledger entries are also delayed, because the drawer account is debited only when the cheque is presented and cleared, not when the cheque is written. Testing a cheque flow must cover presentation, clearing, a successful payment and a bounce, because each path changes the account balance, the customer statement and the messages sent to the drawer and the payee.
