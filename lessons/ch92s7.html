Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Payment hub: A central system in a bank that receives payment instructions from all channels, validates them, applies screening and routing rules, and sends them to the correct clearing system or correspondent.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

The United States has five payment systems that matter, and they are not competitors in the simple sense. They are different tools for different jobs, and a bank that understands them routes each payment to the system that fits it. Fedwire is the large-value real-time gross settlement system run by the Federal Reserve, settling in central bank money. CHIPS is the private-sector large-value system run by The Clearing House, settling by prefunded balance and netting. ACH is the batch network for low-value payments, governed by NACHA. FedNow is the Federal Reserve's instant payment service, settling in central bank money within seconds. RTP is The Clearing House's instant payment network, settling by prefunded balance within seconds. The routing decision. A bank routes a payment by asking four questions. How much is it. How fast does it need to arrive. Is the receiver enrolled on the instant networks. And what does it cost. A payment of 50 dollars to a consumer goes by ACH, because it is cheap and the consumer does not need it in seconds. A payment of 5,000 dollars to a consumer who needs it now goes by FedNow or RTP, if the receiver is enrolled. A payment of 500,000 dollars to a business that needs it today goes by Same Day ACH or by wire, depending on the deadline. A payment of 5 million dollars goes by Fedwire or CHIPS, because the instant networks cannot take it and the ACH limit is too low. A payment in dollars to a bank outside the United States goes by CHIPS or Fedwire through a correspondent, because the instant networks are domestic only. The cost picture. ACH is the cheapest, at a fraction of a dollar per payment. Same Day ACH costs a little more. An instant payment costs more than ACH but far less than a wire. A Fedwire payment costs around 25 dollars or more, and a CHIPS payment is similar. The bank passes some of that cost to the customer, and the customer chooses accordingly. A corporate treasurer who pays 25 dollars to send 5 million dollars does not care about the fee. A consumer who pays 25 dollars to send 200 dollars cares a great deal, and will choose the instant payment or the ACH instead. The value date and the customer promise. Each system makes a different promise about when the money arrives. Fedwire promises today, in minutes. CHIPS promises today, usually within the hour. Same Day ACH promises today, by 1.00 pm, 5.00 pm or 6.00 pm depending on the window. Standard ACH promises tomorrow or the day after. FedNow and RTP promise seconds, at any hour. The bank's job is to make the promise it can keep, and to tell the customer honestly which promise applies. A customer who is told a payment is instant and sees it arrive tomorrow will not trust the bank again. A business analyst must write the requirement that the customer-facing screen shows the expected arrival time for the chosen payment type, and that the screen does not offer instant payment when the receiver is not enrolled. Reconciliation across five systems. The operations team reconciles five different settlement streams every day. Fedwire settles in the master account, and the reconciliation is a match of the Fedwire messages to the master account statement. CHIPS settles in the prefunded balance account, and the reconciliation is a match of the CHIPS advices to the position. ACH settles in batches through the operator, and the reconciliation is a match of the batch totals to the settlement account. FedNow settles continuously in the master account, and the reconciliation must run continuously, not once a day, because the money moves at 3.00 am on a Sunday. RTP settles in the prefunded joint account, and the reconciliation is a match of the RTP messages to the joint account balance. A bank that cannot reconcile all five streams cannot prove that its customers' money is where it should be. The future. The direction of travel is convergence. The ISO 20022 migration means that Fedwire, CHIPS, FedNow and RTP all speak the same message language, and a payment hub can route between them with less translation. The reach of the instant networks grows every month, and the day will come when most consumer and small business payments in the United States are instant by default. The large-value systems will not disappear, because a 50 million dollar payment will always need the certainty of central bank money or the netting efficiency of CHIPS. The ACH will not disappear either, because payroll and direct debits are batch payments by nature, and there is no reason to send 4,800 payroll entries one by one. The bank that wins is the bank whose payment hub routes each payment to the right system, tells the customer honestly when it will arrive, and reconciles all five streams without manual work. Worked example, a corporate treasurer's day. At 8.00 am the treasurer releases the payroll file, 4,800 employees, 6.2 million dollars, by standard ACH, because the pay date is Friday and the money does not need to move today. At 11.00 am the treasurer pays a supplier 850,000 dollars by Same Day ACH, because the invoice is due today and the 2.45 pm window will settle it by 5.00 pm. At 2.00 pm the treasurer sends 2 million dollars by Fedwire to settle a securities trade, because the trade settles today and the amount is too large for the instant networks. At 4.00 pm the treasurer refunds a customer 400 dollars by RTP, because the customer complained and the refund needs to arrive now. Four payments, four systems, one payment hub, and the treasurer never had to think about which network to use, because the hub routed each one correctly. Last reviewed 8 November 2026.
