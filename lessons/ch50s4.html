Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Execution date: The date on which the bank executes a payment instruction, debiting the debtor account and sending the message onward.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

  1. What to do. List the main blocks of the message, name the field that carries the amount, and name the field that identifies the payment.

Correct result. The message is made of blocks, each carrying a part of the payment instruction. The amount field and the identification field are the two a receiving bank checks first, because without them it cannot process or reconcile the payment.

Why. A message is only useful if you can read it. The blocks tell the receiving bank what to do, and the amount and identification fields are what it checks first.

Show transcript

A corporation may need to make two domestic payments on the same day, for example a salary payment and a supplier payment, or two supplier payments to different cities. Instead of sending two separate messages, it can send one MT101 with two Sequence B blocks. A company in Mumbai, for example, may send one MT101 to HDFC Bank, instructing it to pay 250,000 rupees to a supplier in Pune and 180,000 rupees to a landlord in Delhi, both on 15 May 2026. The bank receives one message, validates it once, and then executes two separate domestic payments.

Each Sequence B block carries its own full details, so the two payments do not interfere with each other. The first Sequence B may carry reference SUP 2026 114, amount INR 250,000, beneficiary Shree Components Pvt Ltd, account 50200012345678, and remittance information for invoice INV 8812. The second Sequence B may carry reference RENT 2026 047, amount INR 180,000, beneficiary City Properties Ltd, account 50200098765432, and remittance information for May rent. The bank uses the reference in each block to report the result, so the corporation can reconcile each payment separately.

The two transactions may use different domestic payment systems. A payment of 2 million rupees to a supplier may go through the high value system, while a payment of 50,000 rupees to a consultant may go through the national electronic funds transfer system. The bank decides the system for each Sequence B based on the amount, the value date, the beneficiary bank, and the instruction in field 71A for charges and field 72 for sender to receiver information. The corporation does not need to send a different message type for each system, because MT101 can carry both instructions in one file.

The requested execution date in Sequence A applies to the message, but each Sequence B can carry its own value date if needed. In most domestic runs, both transactions share the same execution date, for example 15 May 2026, and the bank will execute both on that date if funds are available. If one transaction has a different value date, the bank will hold that payment until its own date, while it executes the other at once. A treasurer who sets the dates correctly can therefore pay a supplier today and schedule a tax payment for next week in the same message.

A real example from the United Kingdom shows the saving. A retailer with 30 stores sends one MT101 to Lloyds Bank each Friday, with two Sequence B blocks for domestic payments, one for 120,000 pounds to a logistics supplier in Leeds, and one for 45,000 pounds to a cleaning contractor in Bristol, both through Faster Payments. The bank executes both payments within two hours, and the retailer receives one MT940 statement showing both debits with their own references. The alternative, two separate messages and two separate validations, would take twice the treasury time and double the chance of a manual error.
