Two Domestic Transactions in One MT101

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain two domestic transactions in one mt101. A corporation may need to make two domestic payments on the same day, for example a salary payment and a supplier payment, or two supplier payments to different cities.

Why it matters. This matters because the message is the payment instruction. A wrong field, a missing element or a misunderstood format is a failed payment, a return, or a compliance breach.

Chapter objective: Learner explains MT101 use cases, multiple debit accounts, subsidiary payments, funds repatriation and the difference between Sequence A and Sequence B. Learner uses real examples with amounts, banks and message names to show how the topic works in practice.

Key learning points

  • Two domestic payments in one day: salary or two suppliers
  • Sequence B block and own full details
  • Two transactions using different domestic payment systems
  • Requested execution date in Sequence
  • Real example United Kingdom saving

Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Execution date: The date on which the bank executes a payment instruction, debiting the debtor account and sending the message onward.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

A corporation may need to make two domestic payments on the same day, for example a salary payment and a supplier payment, or two supplier payments to different cities. Instead of sending two separate messages, it can send one MT101 with two Sequence B blocks. A company in Mumbai, for example, may send one MT101 to HDFC Bank, instructing it to pay 250,000 rupees to a supplier in Pune and 180,000 rupees to a landlord in Delhi, both on 15 May 2026. The bank receives one message, validates it once, and then executes two separate domestic payments. Each Sequence B block carries its own full details, so the two payments do not interfere with each other. The first Sequence B may carry reference SUP 2026 114, amount INR 250,000, beneficiary Shree Components Pvt Ltd, account 50200012345678, and remittance information for invoice INV 8812. The second Sequence B may carry reference RENT 2026 047, amount INR 180,000, beneficiary City Properties Ltd, account 50200098765432, and remittance information for May rent. The bank uses the reference in each block to report the result, so the corporation can reconcile each payment separately. The two transactions may use different domestic payment systems. A payment of 2 million rupees to a supplier may go through the high value system, while a payment of 50,000 rupees to a consultant may go through the national electronic funds transfer system. The bank decides the system for each Sequence B based on the amount, the value date, the beneficiary bank, and the instruction in field 71A for charges and field 72 for sender to receiver information. The corporation does not need to send a different message type for each system, because MT101 can carry both instructions in one file. The requested execution date in Sequence A applies to the message, but each Sequence B can carry its own value date if needed. In most domestic runs, both transactions share the same execution date, for example 15 May 2026, and the bank will execute both on that date if funds are available. If one transaction has a different value date, the bank will hold that payment until its own date, while it executes the other at once. A treasurer who sets the dates correctly can therefore pay a supplier today and schedule a tax payment for next week in the same message. A real example from the United Kingdom shows the saving. A retailer with 30 stores sends one MT101 to Lloyds Bank each Friday, with two Sequence B blocks for domestic payments, one for 120,000 pounds to a logistics supplier in Leeds, and one for 45,000 pounds to a cleaning contractor in Bristol, both through Faster Payments. The bank executes both payments within two hours, and the retailer receives one MT940 statement showing both debits with their own references. The alternative, two separate messages and two separate validations, would take twice the treasury time and double the chance of a manual error.

Two domestic transactions in one MT101 is the simplest multi-transaction case, and it shows how the message works when no cross border leg is involved. A corporation sends one MT101 to its bank, and the bank executes two domestic payments from the same account, on the same execution date, to two different beneficiaries. The two payments are independent, and each has its own Sequence B, its own reference, its own amount, and its own remittance information. The bank debits the account once per transaction, or once for the total if its system supports a single debit, and it sends each payment through the domestic clearing system.

Consider a concrete example. Meridian Foods Ltd holds account GB29 HBUK 4012 3456 7890 01 at HSBC in London. On 12 May 2026 it sends an MT101 with reference PAYRUN 2026 0512 and execution date 120526. Sequence B 1, indexed 1/2 in field 28D, pays 45,000 pounds to a supplier in Berlin. Sequence B 2, indexed 2/2, pays 12,000 pounds to a consultant in Manchester. Field 50a in Sequence A names Meridian Foods Ltd. Field 25 in Sequence A names the Meridian account. Each Sequence B carries its own field 21 transaction reference, its own field 32B amount, its own field 59a beneficiary, and its own field 70 remittance information. The bank reads the two Sequence B blocks and creates two separate domestic payments.

The two payments may travel through different domestic systems even though they are in one MT101. The payment to the consultant in Manchester, in pounds, will normally go through Faster Payments if it is below the scheme limit, or through BACS if it is a regular bulk payment, or through CHAPS if it is high value and urgent. The payment to the supplier in Berlin, also in pounds in this example, will go through the same domestic system if the beneficiary bank is in the UK, or through the cross border route if the beneficiary account is in Germany. The MT101 does not dictate the clearing system. The bank decides the routing for each Sequence B based on the currency, the amount, the beneficiary bank, and the value date. The corporation only states the payment, and the bank routes it.

The value date and the execution date are different ideas, and an SME must keep them apart. Field 30 in Sequence A is the requested execution date, the date the bank should start processing the payment. The value date is the date the beneficiary bank credits the beneficiary account, and it depends on the clearing system. A Faster Payments payment executed on 12 May will normally reach the beneficiary on the same day. A BACS payment executed on 12 May will reach the beneficiary on 14 May, because BACS works on a three day cycle. The MT101 does not carry a value date per transaction, so the corporation must know the clearing cycle of the system the bank will use, and must set the execution date early enough to meet the supplier payment deadline.

The bank reports each transaction separately, even though they arrived in one message. The status report will list transaction 1, reference and amount, as executed, and transaction 2, reference and amount, as executed, with the value date and the charges for each. If one transaction fails, for example because the beneficiary account number is invalid, the bank reports that transaction as rejected and processes the other. The corporation reconciles the status report against its own payment run, transaction by transaction, and re-issues the failed payment in a new MT101 or through another channel. A treasury system that cannot reconcile at transaction level cannot use MT101 safely, because a single failed payment in a large run is easy to miss.

A common edge case is a duplicate Sequence B. If the sender system generates the same field 21 reference twice, the bank may reject the second transaction as a duplicate, or it may execute it twice if its duplicate detection is weak. The sender must therefore generate a unique field 21 for each Sequence B, and the bank must check the reference against recently processed transactions. Another edge case is a transaction whose amount exceeds the debit account balance when combined with the other transactions in the run. The bank checks the total of all Sequence B amounts against the available balance, not each transaction in isolation, and it will reject the whole run or the later transactions if the total exceeds the balance. The corporation should therefore check the total before sending the message.

For a business analyst, the requirement for two domestic transactions in one MT101 is straightforward to write, but it must cover the routing decision, the value date, and the reporting. The requirement should state that the system sends one MT101 with two Sequence B blocks, that each block carries its own beneficiary, amount, and remittance information, that the bank routes each payment through the appropriate domestic system, that the system records the value date per transaction from the bank status report, and that the system flags any transaction that is rejected so the treasurer can re-issue it. A system that treats the MT101 as one payment, rather than as a container for many payments, will fail the reconciliation.
