Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Mandate: An authorisation given by a debtor to a creditor to collect payments from the debtor account by direct debit.

BACS is the workhorse of UK retail payments. It processes Direct Debits and Direct Credits, and it moves more payments by volume than any other UK system. Salaries, pensions, utility bills, council tax, mortgage payments and most regular business-to-business payments travel by BACS. It is not fast, and it is not designed to be fast. It is designed to be cheap, predictable and reliable, and it settles in central bank money through the Bank of England. A payments SME who cannot explain the BACS three-day cycle cannot explain how most UK money moves. The three-day cycle is the heart of BACS. Day 1 is the input day, when the submitting bank sends its payment file to BACS. Day 2 is the processing day, when BACS sorts the file, validates the sort codes and account numbers, and prepares the settlement. Day 3 is the entry day, when the money is credited to the beneficiary and debited from the payer. The value date is Day 3. A salary file submitted on Monday is paid on Wednesday. There is no way to speed this up within BACS itself. If a customer needs the money on Tuesday, the payment must go by Faster Payments or CHAPS. Direct Debit is the pull mechanism. The payer gives the originator a mandate, and the originator pulls the money on the due date. The mandate is the legal authority, and it is held by the originator, not by the bank. AUDDIS is the message that sets up a new Direct Debit instruction at the payer's bank. ADDACS is the message the bank sends when an instruction is amended or cancelled. ARUDD is the report of unpaid Direct Debits, the ones that bounced because the account had insufficient funds or was closed. The Direct Debit Guarantee protects the payer: if an error is made, the payer gets an immediate refund from their bank, and the bank recovers from the originator. Direct Credit is the push mechanism, often called BACS Direct Credit. The payer's bank sends a credit to the beneficiary's account. Salaries and pensions are the classic use. The file contains one credit per beneficiary, and the originator's bank submits it as a bulk file. The beneficiary sees a single credit on Day 3, with a reference the originator chose. Direct Credit has no guarantee equivalent to the Direct Debit Guarantee, because the risk sits with the payer, not the payee. File submission is done through BACSTEL-IP, the secure internet channel, or through a bureau that submits on behalf of smaller originators. A service user number identifies the originator, and every file is signed and validated before BACS accepts it. The practical cut-off is late evening on Day 1, typically around 22:30, but each bank sets its own earlier cut-off for its customers, often in the afternoon. A file that misses the cut-off rolls to the next cycle, and the entry day moves by one working day. Bank holidays shift the whole cycle, so a file submitted before a long weekend can take five calendar days to arrive. What goes wrong in BACS is usually a file problem, not a system problem. A sort code that does not exist causes the payment to reject at validation. An account number with the wrong number of digits fails the modulus check. A file submitted with a duplicate reference can double-pay, so originators must keep a strict control on file numbering. An unpaid Direct Debit appears on the ARUDD report, and the originator must decide whether to re-present it, contact the payer, or write it off. Re-presenting a Direct Debit that the payer has cancelled is a breach of the scheme rules and will draw a complaint. For a business analyst, BACS requirements are about dates, files and reports. The acceptance criteria must state the input day, the processing day and the entry day for every payment type. A test must prove that a file submitted before the cut-off is paid on Day 3, and a file submitted after the cut-off is paid on Day 4. The requirements must name the reports the operations team will use: the input report, the ARUDD report, the ADDACS report, and the settlement report. The design must handle the bank holiday calendar, because a payment due on a bank holiday is paid on the next working day, and a customer who expects their salary on a Friday will notice if it arrives on a Tuesday.
