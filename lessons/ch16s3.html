Key terms

A return happens when a payment has been sent and the funds have moved, but the receiving bank cannot credit the beneficiary and sends the money back. The most common reasons are that the beneficiary account is closed, that the account number does not match the name, that the beneficiary bank cannot identify the account, or that the beneficiary has instructed the bank to refuse the payment. The receiving bank sends a return message, using a pacs.004 payment return in ISO 20022, which carries the original reference, the UETR, the amount returned, and the reason code.

The funds travel back through the chain, and each bank in the chain must process the return and pass it on, until the funds reach the sending bank, which credits the sender's account. Returns take time, because the funds must travel back through the same chain they came through, and each bank adds its processing time. A payment that took two days to go out can take two to five days to come back, and the customer must be told that the return is in progress and when it is expected. The amount returned may be less than the amount sent, because banks in the chain may deduct their charges from the return, and because a currency conversion on the way back may use a different rate.

The analyst must design the return handling to match the return to the original payment by UETR, to calculate the amount actually returned, to identify any deduction, and to notify the customer with the amount and the reason. The customer who sent ten thousand dollars and receives nine thousand nine hundred and fifty dollars back needs to know why fifty dollars is missing, and the answer must be in the notification, not discovered by the customer when they check their balance. Returns also create a reconciliation task, because the sending bank must match the returned funds to the original payment and to the customer account.

If the return arrives with a different reference, or if the amount is different, the reconciliation team must investigate and allocate the funds manually. The analyst must specify the matching rules, the handling of partial returns, and the escalation for returns that cannot be matched. A return that sits unallocated in a suspense account is a customer whose money is missing, and the longer it sits, the angrier the customer becomes. The return process is therefore not just an operations task. It is a customer service task, and the analyst must treat it as one.
