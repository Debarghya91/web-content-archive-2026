A suspicious activity report in the United States and a suspicious transaction report elsewhere are the same idea: when a bank sees something it cannot explain, it tells the financial intelligence unit. The United States requires a SAR to be filed with the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, FinCEN, within 30 calendar days of detecting a suspicious transaction, with a 30-day extension if no suspect is identified. The United Kingdom requires a suspicious activity report to the National Crime Agency, and operates a consent regime under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002. India requires a suspicious transaction report to the Financial Intelligence Unit, FIU-IND, under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002. The deadlines differ, but the discipline is the same: detect, investigate, decide, file, and keep the evidence. The United Kingdom consent regime deserves attention because it changes what the bank can do with the money. When a bank has knowledge or suspicion that property is criminal, it must make a disclosure to the National Crime Agency and request consent, known as a Defence Against Money Laundering, before dealing with the property. The agency has 7 working days to respond. If it refuses consent, a 31-day moratorium period begins, during which the bank must not deal with the property, and the moratorium can be extended by a court order up to a total of 186 days. If the agency does not respond in 7 working days, consent is deemed to have been given. The bank must therefore have a workflow that tracks the 7-day clock, the 31-day clock, and any extension, because a payment released during a moratorium is a criminal offence. The narrative is the part of the report that decides whether it is useful. A good narrative answers who, what, when, where, why and how. Who are the parties, including the account holders and any third parties named in the payment. What happened, with the amounts, the dates, the channels and the payment references. When the activity occurred and when it was detected. Where the money went, including the destination accounts and countries. Why it is suspicious, with the specific red flags and how they compare to the customer expected behaviour. How the funds moved, including the sequence of payments and any layering. A narrative that says the activity was unusual, without saying what was unusual about it, will be returned or ignored, and the bank will have filed a report that protects nobody. Tipping off is prohibited in every regime, and it is the rule that operations staff most often get wrong. The bank must not tell the customer that a report has been filed, or that an investigation is under way. The prohibition covers the customer, their family, and anyone else who might warn them. The practical problem is that the customer will ask why their payment is delayed, and the staff must have a script that explains the delay without revealing the report. The script must be written, trained and tested, because an ad hoc answer in a branch or on a phone call is where tipping off happens. The same care applies to the audit trail: the report and the investigation file must be held in a system with restricted access, not in a shared drive. The decision not to file is as important as the decision to file, and it must be documented. When an alert is investigated and the activity is explained, the file must record the explanation, the evidence, and the name of the person who decided. A file that says no further action, without saying why, is not a decision, it is an absence of one, and an examiner will treat it as such. The quality assurance process samples the no-file decisions at a higher rate than the filed reports, because a missed report is a bigger risk than a weak one. The sampling must cover all teams and all alert types, and the results must be reported to the money laundering reporting officer. The consequences of failing to file are severe and they are public. Regulators have imposed fines of hundreds of millions of pounds and dollars for systemic failures in suspicious activity reporting, and the enforcement actions name the specific failures: alerts closed without investigation, reports filed late, narratives that contained no useful information, and a backlog of unreviewed alerts. The enforcement action also usually requires the bank to appoint an independent monitor, which is expensive and intrusive. The lesson for a payment firm is that the reporting obligation is not a back-office chore, it is a regulatory duty with a deadline, and the deadline is measured from detection, not from when the team gets around to it. For a business analyst, the reporting work is a workflow with hard clocks. The alert must be investigated inside the triage service level. The decision to file or not must be recorded with a reason and a named decision maker. The report must be drafted from a template that forces the who, what, when, where, why and how. The filing must be tracked against the deadline, with an escalation if the deadline is at risk. The acceptance criteria must include a test where a suspicious pattern is investigated and filed inside 30 days, a test where a UK consent request is tracked through the 7-day and 31-day clocks, a test where a no-file decision is documented with evidence, and a test where a tipping-off scenario is handled with the approved script.
