What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain from gross payments to net positions. Every payment has a gross value, which is the full amount instructed by the payer.

Why it matters. By netting first, a bank needs a much smaller balance, and the cost of holding that balance falls.

Chapter objective: Learner explains clearing, bilateral netting, multilateral netting, and the role of a clearing house, and calculates net settlement positions from a set of gross payment obligations. Learner demonstrates this skill using realistic banking examples, explaining each step clearly and using correct terminology throughout.

Key learning points

  • Gross payment value
  • Gross settlement liquidity
  • Customer experience
  • Ten million and eight million of payments
  • Liquidity saving
  • Credit risk remaining after netting

Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Liquidity: Cash or assets that a bank can use immediately to meet payment obligations. Banks manage liquidity by forecasting inflows and outflows, funding nostro and settlement accounts, and using central bank...

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Practise this lesson

Exercise

Work out the net position between two banks from their gross payment flows.

Bank A sends 100 payments worth EUR 10 million in total to Bank B, and Bank B sends 80 payments worth EUR 8 million to Bank A.

  1. What to do. Calculate the gross flows in each direction, then calculate the net position and state what settles and what the customer sees.

Correct result. Gross flows are EUR 10 million from Bank A to Bank B and EUR 8 million from Bank B to Bank A, a total of EUR 18 million exchanged. The net position is EUR 2 million owed by Bank A to Bank B, and only that EUR 2 million settles. Each customer payment still reaches its recipient in full. Netting only changes how the banks settle with each other behind the scenes.

Why. Netting cuts the liquidity needed from EUR 18 million of gross flows to a EUR 2 million net settlement, which is why banks net before they settle.

Every payment has a gross value, which is the full amount instructed by the payer. If Bank A sends one hundred payments worth ten million in total to Bank B, and Bank B sends eighty payments worth eight million to Bank A, the gross flows are ten million in one direction and eight million in the other. The net position is the difference between these two gross flows. In this example Bank A owes Bank B a net amount of two million, even though the total value of payments exchanged was eighteen million. Netting reduces the amount that actually needs to move at settlement, which is the core benefit of the whole process. This reduction matters because settlement uses central bank money, which is scarce and expensive to hold in large amounts. If banks had to settle every gross payment separately, they would need far more liquidity sitting in their central bank accounts. By netting first, they only need enough liquidity to cover the net difference. The same logic applies at scale. In a system with twenty banks exchanging thousands of payments, the total gross value might run into billions, while the total net settlement might be only a fraction of that amount. Netting therefore makes the payment system far more efficient. It is important to understand that netting does not cancel any individual payment. Each customer payment still reaches its intended recipient in full, and each recipient sees the full amount credited to their account. This separation between the customer leg and the interbank leg is a key concept in payments, and it appears again in card clearing, in cross border settlement and in securities settlement.
