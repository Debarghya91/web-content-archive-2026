Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

Exception: A payment that cannot be processed normally and needs manual handling, investigation or repair. Exceptions arise from missing data, failed screening, account errors or system faults.

Practise this lesson

Exercise

Work out what the beneficiary receives for a $10,000 payment under OUR, SHA and BEN.

Use the charge arrangement rules in this lesson and the correspondent and beneficiary bank fees named in the lesson.

  1. What to do. Calculate the amount credited under each of the three arrangements and state who pays each fee.

Correct result. Under OUR the sender pays all charges and the beneficiary receives the full amount. Under SHA the sender pays the sending charges and the beneficiary pays the receiving charges. Under BEN the beneficiary pays all charges and receives the amount less all fees.

Why. The charge arrangement decides who pays and how much arrives. A customer who was promised the full amount and receives less will complain, and the complaint lands on the analyst.

SWIFT Universal Confirmation is the rule that a bank receiving a cross border payment must confirm what happened to it. The rule applies to every supervised financial institution that receives an MT103 or a pacs.008, whether or not the bank is a member of the gpi service, and it has been mandatory since 22 November 2020. The receiving bank reports the outcome to the SWIFT Tracker, so the bank that sent the payment can see that it arrived, was rejected, or is being held, instead of guessing. Universal Confirmation closes the loop that tracking depends on: a tracker that never hears the ending cannot tell the customer the payment is complete.

The confirmation must state one of four outcomes, and it must arrive quickly. The bank reports that the payment was credited to the beneficiary, rejected, placed on hold, or transferred to an agent outside SWIFT, and for a credit it reports the amount credited and the value date. SWIFT measures each institution on confirming at least 80 percent of its weekly payment traffic within two business days of the value date, and a reported hold counts inside the window as well as a credit. Compliance is monitored through a traffic light score visible to the wider community, so late or missing confirmations are seen. The deadline is a service rule set by SWIFT, not a target each bank may set for itself.

Universal Confirmation is what makes gpi tracking trustworthy from end to end. Each bank in the chain updates the Tracker as the payment passes through, and the updates are linked by the Unique End to End Transaction Reference, known as the UETR, which stays with the payment from the sending bank to the beneficiary bank. The final confirmation is the update that matters most, because it turns a payment that is merely in transit into a payment that is known to have arrived. Without it, the Tracker view stops at the last intermediary, the ordering bank cannot answer the customer question about where the money is, and the payment stays open in the ordering bank systems. Confirmations travel through the Universal Confirmations API, through the trck.001 message, or through the Basic Tracker for banks with small volumes, and the MT199 message, used for tracker status in the past, was deprecated for this purpose in November 2025.

The confirmation flow follows the payment flow. The ordering bank sends the payment and the intermediary banks forward it, each updating the Tracker as they handle it. The beneficiary bank receives the payment, checks the beneficiary account, credits the amount, and then sends the confirmation, stating the outcome and, for a credit, the amount and value date. The Tracker updates to show the confirmed status, and the banks that handled the payment can see the ending without sending queries to each other. The beneficiary bank confirmation is distinct from the intermediary updates: intermediaries report progress, while only the final bank can report the outcome, which is why the rule places the duty on the receiving bank.

When a bank does not confirm, the consequences fall on the tracker, on the ordering bank, and on the silent bank itself. The Tracker shows the payment as unconfirmed, which the ordering bank and its customer can see, and the ordering bank must chase the missing status, often by sending a query message to the bank that should have confirmed. The payment remains an open item in the ordering bank reconciliation, creating manual exception work until the status is known. The bank that failed to confirm falls short of its service obligation, its traffic light score reflects the gap, and counterparties that depend on its confirmations can see the pattern. A payment held for a genuine reason, such as a compliance review or a missing beneficiary detail, should be reported as held rather than left in silence, because a reported hold can be managed and a silent one cannot.

A tracked payment shows the rule working as intended. A supplier payment sent on a Monday is credited to the beneficiary on Tuesday, the beneficiary bank confirms the credit the same day, and the ordering bank customer sees a confirmed arrival in the tracker instead of an open item. For a business analyst, the design work is to make confirmation automatic: generate the confirmation from the posting event, monitor the two business day window daily, queue unconfirmed items for same day follow up, and test all four outcomes, including the hold and the transfer outside SWIFT. A bank that confirms promptly gives its customers certainty, keeps its reconciliation clean, and keeps its community standing green. A bank that stays silent pays for it in queries, exceptions, and trust.
