Key terms

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

Scheme: A card or payment network that sets the rules, standards and fees for transactions between issuers and acquirers.

A cheque is a paper instruction, but the clearing system that processes it no longer needs the paper. Cheque conversion is the set of techniques that turn a paper cheque into electronic data at the earliest possible point, so that the image and the data travel through clearing instead of the physical document. There are two distinct ideas here, and the analyst must not confuse them. Truncation means the cheque is scanned at the bank of first deposit, the image and the data go forward for clearing, and the paper stops moving. Conversion means the cheque details are used to create a different kind of payment altogether, usually an electronic debit, and the cheque itself is treated as the authorisation for that debit.

Truncation is now the normal model in most cheque clearing systems. In the United States, the Check 21 law, effective in 2004, allowed banks to exchange cheque images and to create a substitute cheque, a paper print of the image with the same legal standing as the original, so that the original could stop moving. In India, the Cheque Truncation System, introduced in 2010, captures the image and the magnetic ink data at the presenting bank and clears on the image. In the United Kingdom, the Image Clearing System, rolled out in 2018 and 2019, lets banks exchange cheque images and clear a cheque by the next working day, where the old paper exchange took several days. In each case the image carries the legal weight, the data carries the routing and amount, and the paper stays at the bank where it was deposited.

Conversion to an electronic debit is a different mechanism, used mainly in the United States through the ACH network. When a business receives a cheque by post for a bill, it can convert the cheque into an ACH debit under the accounts receivable conversion rules, using the routing number and account number printed on the cheque. When a customer pays by cheque at a shop, the cheque can be converted at the point of purchase into an electronic debit, with the paper cheque voided and returned to the customer. When a customer authorises a payment by telephone, the cheque details given on the call can be converted into an electronic debit. In each case the cheque is the authorisation, the ACH debit is the payment, and the customer must be told that the cheque will be converted and will not be presented as paper.

The data captured from a cheque is small but exact. The magnetic ink line at the bottom carries the routing number, the account number and the cheque number, and the image carries the amount, the date, the payee name and the signature. The capturing bank reads the magnetic line automatically, enters or reads the amount, and creates a clearing record that combines the data with the image. The amount is the field most likely to be wrong, because it is handwritten, so the capturing process includes amount verification, and a mismatch between the written amount and the figures is resolved under the scheme rules before the record is sent. The image must be clear enough for the paying bank to verify the signature and the payee, because the image is what the paying bank sees when it decides to pay or return the cheque.

Returns work on the image too. If the paying bank decides not to pay, because the account has insufficient funds, the signature does not match, or the cheque is stale, it returns the item electronically with a return reason, and the image travels back with the return record. The depositor sees the return quickly, because no paper has to travel back, and the funds that were provisionally credited are withdrawn. A provisional credit must never be presented as final funds before the return window has closed.

A worked example shows the full path. A customer deposits a cheque for 850 pounds at a branch on Monday morning. The branch scans the cheque, reads the magnetic line, verifies the amount, and sends the image and data into the Image Clearing System the same day. The paying bank receives the image, checks the signature and the balance, and decides to pay. The funds are credited on Tuesday, the next working day, and the paper cheque never leaves the depositing bank. If the paying bank had returned the cheque unpaid, the return would have arrived electronically, the credit would have been withdrawn, and the customer would have been told the reason. The cheque is still a paper instrument for the customer, but for the clearing system it has been electronic since the moment it was scanned.
