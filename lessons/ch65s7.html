What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain worked example: a customer pays EUR 240 through a PISP. Claire Dubois, a customer of Meridian Bank in Lyon, buys EUR 240 of kitchen goods from an online retailer called Maison Nord.

Chapter objective: Learner explains open banking, PSD2 and PSD3, including the roles of PISPs, AISPs and PIISPs, strong customer authentication, verification of payee and fraud information exchange, and traces a customer payment through a PISP end to end. Learner applies this skill in realistic banking scenarios, with clear examples and correct terminology throughout.

Key terms

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

Clearing: The process of exchanging payment instructions between banks and calculating what each bank owes the others.

PISP: Payment initiation service provider, a licensed provider that initiates payments from a customer account with the customer consent, under open banking rules.

Show transcript

Claire Dubois, a customer of Meridian Bank in Lyon, buys EUR 240 of kitchen goods from an online retailer called Maison Nord. At checkout she chooses pay by bank, and the retailer PISP, a licensed provider called PayBridge, shows her a list of banks. She picks Meridian Bank, and PayBridge sends an API call to Meridian Bank asking to start a payment of EUR 240 to Maison Nord, with the retailer account number and the order reference. Claire sees a consent screen that names PayBridge, states the amount EUR 240, names the payee Maison Nord, and explains that PayBridge will start one payment and will not read her account history. She agrees, and the consent is recorded with a time stamp. Meridian Bank now runs its fraud checks before it asks Claire to authenticate. The bank checks her device, her location in Lyon, and her payment history, and it sees that she has never paid Maison Nord before, which raises the risk slightly. It then runs verification of payee, comparing the name Maison Nord with the name on the account PayBridge supplied. The check returns a mismatch, because the account is held in the name of Maison Nord SARL Trading, and the trading name does not match the registered name exactly. The bank shows Claire a warning that the name does not fully match, displays the registered name, and asks her to confirm that she still wants to proceed. She checks the retailer website, sees the legal name in the footer, and confirms. Claire now completes strong customer authentication on the Meridian Bank app, using her fingerprint and a one time code, which are two independent factors. The bank links the authentication to the amount EUR 240 and to the payee, so the approval cannot be reused for another payment. Once authentication succeeds, Meridian Bank executes the credit transfer, debiting EUR 240 from Claire account and crediting the Maison Nord account through the SEPA Instant route, because the retailer asked for an instant payment. PayBridge receives a confirmation through the bank API and tells the retailer that the payment has started, and the retailer shows Claire an order confirmation. The whole journey takes under a minute. The ledger entries tell the same story in accounting terms. Meridian Bank debits Claire customer account with EUR 240 and credits its internal settlement account with EUR 240, then settles the EUR 240 through the clearing system to the retailer bank. The retailer bank credits the Maison Nord account with EUR 240 and debits its own settlement account. If the fraud check had blocked the payment, no debit would have happened, and PayBridge would have received a rejection with a reason code. A business analyst should note that the mismatch warning, the consent record, and the authentication record are all stored, because they are the evidence if Claire later disputes the payment. A product owner should note that the warning wording decided the outcome, because a confusing warning would have lost the sale, and a weak warning would have let a fraud through.
