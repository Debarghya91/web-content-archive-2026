Why Payment Testing Is Different

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain payment testing. Payment testing is different because every test case touches value, and value cannot be undone with a simple refresh.

Why it matters. When a payment system sends a pacs.008 for 125,000 pounds to the wrong beneficiary account, the bank must start a recall, contact the receiving bank, and explain the error to the customer.

Chapter objective: Learner plans payment testing and certification, including test cases, test data, simulators, and scheme certification, and writes a test plan for a new payment feature. Learner practises this skill on realistic banking cases, explaining the reasoning clearly and using correct terminology throughout the work.

Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Charge bearer: A code that states who pays the charges for a payment. In ISO 20022 the values are DEBT for debtor, CRED for creditor, SHAR for shared and SLEV for service level.

Value date: The date on which funds become available to the beneficiary and on which interest starts to accrue. It is the date used for settlement and for nostro posting.

  1. What to do. Compare the instruction, the settlement record and the statement, name the break type, and state the corrective action.

Correct result. A break is found by matching amount, currency, date and reference across the three sources. The root cause is a timing difference, an amount difference, a missing entry, or a duplicate, and the corrective action follows the cause.

Why. Reconciliation is the last line of defence. A break that is not investigated is money that cannot be proved.

Payment testing is different because every test case touches value, and value cannot be undone with a simple refresh. When a retail website shows the wrong price, the customer can abandon the basket. When a payment system sends a pacs.008 for 125,000 pounds to the wrong beneficiary account, the bank must start a recall, contact the receiving bank, and explain the error to the customer. A tester therefore checks not only that the screen looks correct, but that the amount, currency, value date, charges and beneficiary details are exactly right in the message that leaves the bank. A payment system also depends on many external parties, and that dependence makes testing harder. A single customer payment from NatWest in London to DBS in Singapore may pass through SWIFT, a correspondent bank, a clearing system and the beneficiary bank. Each party applies its own validation rules, cut off times and sanctions screening. A test that passes inside one bank can still fail at the next hop because a mandatory field is missing or a code is invalid. Good payment testing therefore covers the full chain, not only the internal application. Time is another reason payment testing is special, because payments are bound by strict deadlines. A CHAPS payment for 2.4 million pounds for a house purchase must settle on the agreed value date, and a SEPA Instant payment must reach the beneficiary within ten seconds. If a system is slow, the payment misses its window and the customer faces penalties or a failed property completion. Testers must therefore test performance, cut off handling, queueing and end of day processing, not only functional correctness of a single transaction. Payment testing also carries regulatory and audit weight that ordinary testing does not carry. Regulators expect banks to prove that controls work, that sanctions screening blocks prohibited payments, and that customer funds are protected. Auditors ask for test evidence, defect logs and sign off records. A tester who writes a vague test result such as tested okay creates a risk for the whole bank. Clear evidence, with transaction references, amounts and expected results, is part of the job, and it protects both the customer and the institution. Finally, payment testing is different because the cost of a production defect is measured in money, reputation and regulatory action. In 2018 a major UK bank sent duplicate payments after a system change, and thousands of customers received money twice. The bank had to recover the funds, pay compensation and report the incident to the regulator. That incident started with a testing gap. A payment tester who finds a duplicate generation defect in a test environment saves the bank from that outcome, and that is why this work demands care, precision and professional scepticism. A worked example shows why the evidence standard is so high. Suppose the bank changes the charge calculation on SEPA payments. The test pack must include a 10,000 euro payment with a 12 euro charge, and the expected result must state the exact debit of 10,000 euros, the exact credit of 9,988 euros, the charge bearer code SHAR in the pacs.008, and the ledger entries on both sides. The tester must capture the pacs.008 message, the pacs.002 acceptance, and the ledger posting as evidence. If any one of those three is missing, the test is not proven, and an auditor will send it back. That discipline is what separates payment testing from ordinary software testing. Failure in payment testing is measured in real money and real regulatory consequences. A duplicate payment defect that reaches production can move millions before anyone notices, and the bank must then recover the funds, compensate customers, and explain the failure to the regulator. A sanctions screening defect can let a prohibited payment through, which carries fines and possible loss of the correspondent relationship. For a business analyst, the practical lesson is to write requirements that state the exact expected message, the exact ledger entries, and the exact error behaviour for every failure, because vague requirements produce vague tests, and vague tests let defects through.
