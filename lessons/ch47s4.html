Key terms

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Aggregator: A payment provider that lets many small merchants accept card payments under the aggregator master merchant account, without each merchant holding its own acquirer contract.

Settlement: The final movement of funds between banks to discharge obligations created by cleared payments. Settlement is usually made in central bank money or through correspondent accounts.

A payment gateway is a service that accepts payment instructions from a bank or a merchant and forwards them to the correct scheme. A corporate customer might send a file of 10,000 salary payments to the bank gateway, which validates the file, converts it to the scheme format, and delivers it onward. A merchant might send card and instant payment requests through the same gateway. The gateway hides the complexity of many schemes behind a single interface, which simplifies the bank internal systems. The bank still owns the customer relationship and the regulatory responsibility, even though the gateway performs the technical routing.

An aggregator goes further than a gateway, because it holds the scheme membership and the settlement relationship on behalf of smaller participants. A small bank or a fintech company might connect to an aggregator rather than joining a scheme directly, because direct membership requires capital, certification, and a settlement account. The aggregator sends the payments under its own membership and settles with the participant separately. A fintech sending USD 200,000 a day through an aggregator depends entirely on that aggregator for its service, so the contract must define limits, settlement timing, and the procedure if the aggregator fails or the relationship ends.

Third-party services supply specialist functions that a bank cannot build efficiently, such as sanctions screening, fraud detection, foreign exchange pricing, and address validation. A screening provider might check 50,000 payments a day against lists that change hourly, and return a result in under 200 milliseconds. A foreign exchange provider might quote a rate for a EUR to USD conversion that is valid for thirty seconds. These services are usually consumed through an application programming interface, with a contract that defines the request format, the response format, the timeout, and the error codes. The bank must test the service under load before launch, because a slow provider becomes the slowest step in the whole payment.

Service levels and penalties make the dependency explicit, and they must be negotiated before the service goes live. A gateway contract might promise 99.95 percent availability and a response time under one second for ninety-five percent of requests. If the provider misses the target, the contract might require a service credit or a root cause report within five business days. The bank should also demand a test environment that mirrors production, so that changes can be tested before they reach customers. A provider that offers no test environment is a risk, because the bank cannot verify a new version without risking live payments.

Failover planning is essential, because a third-party failure can stop a bank payment service completely. The bank should know what happens if the gateway is unavailable for ten minutes, for one hour, or for a full day. The plan might include a secondary provider, a direct scheme connection held in reserve, or a manual process for urgent payments above a threshold, such as GBP 50,000. The failover must be tested at least twice a year, with a realistic scenario and a measured recovery time. A plan that has never been tested is only a document, and the first real failure is the worst time to discover that it does not work.
