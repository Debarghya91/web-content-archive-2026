Key terms

Remittance information: Information that explains what a payment is for, such as invoice numbers, contract references or salary month.

Sanctions screening: Checking payment parties, countries and references against sanctions lists published by governments and international bodies.

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Transcript and full text

CBPR+ is SWIFT's programme to move cross-border payments from MT messages, the FIN format banks have used for decades, to ISO 20022 MX messages. It is part of a global migration: the UK, the euro area and the United States are all moving their domestic high-value systems to ISO 20022 on a similar timetable. The reason is data. An MT103 carries the ordering customer in field 50a and the beneficiary in field 59a, with limited structured fields and a great deal of free text. A pacs.008 carries the same information in structured, labelled elements, with room for full addresses, structured remittance information and a unique end-to-end reference. Structured data can be screened, validated and reconciled by machine. Free text cannot. The migration runs through a coexistence period that started in March 2023. During coexistence, MT and MX messages run in parallel, and a bank must be able to send and receive both formats. A pacs.008 arriving from one correspondent may need to go out as an MT103 to the next correspondent, because that correspondent is not yet ready for MX. The original plan was to end the use of MT for most cross-border payments in November 2025, but parts of the timeline were deferred, including the MT101 relay cutover, and the end date now differs by message type. A bank must track the SWIFT timeline per message type, not assume a single cutover date, because the date for an MT103 is not the same as the date for an MT202. Translation between the formats is governed by SWIFT's published translation rules in the CBPR+ usage guidelines. The rules state exactly how each MT field maps to its MX element and back again. Structured data maps cleanly: a structured debtor name and address in pacs.008 becomes field 50a in MT103 without loss. Unstructured data does not map cleanly, and that is where the problems start. Free-text address lines, long remittance references and special instructions have no exact equivalent, so the translator must truncate, reformat or drop them, and the receiving bank gets a degraded version of the original message. Truncated data is the most common operational failure in the migration. An MT103 field 70, remittance information, allows four lines of 35 characters, a total of 140 characters of free text. A pacs.008 can carry 140 characters of structured remittance information, and it can also carry additional structured data that has no MT equivalent at all. When a pacs.008 is translated to an MT103, the extra data is cut. If the cut removes the invoice number the beneficiary needs to reconcile the payment, the beneficiary cannot match the payment to the invoice, and the payment goes into an exception queue. The practical mitigation is to use structured remittance information wherever possible, to keep the full data in the pacs.008 for the banks that can receive it, and to warn the customer when a corridor still runs on MT and their reference may be shortened. The migration forced changes across the whole payment stack. Message validation had to accept both formats and reject messages that fail either set of rules. The sanctions screening engine had to understand both formats, because a name screened in an MT103 field 50a and the same name in a pacs.008 debtor element must produce the same result. Reconciliation had to match the MT and MX legs of the same payment, using the UETR as the link. Archiving had to store both formats. Staff had to be trained to read both formats, because an operations analyst investigating a payment may see an MT103 from one bank and a pacs.008 from another. The cost of getting it wrong is concrete: a payment rejected because a mandatory MX field is missing, or a payment delayed because the translation dropped the information the next bank needed. The deferral of the November 2025 cutover is itself a lesson. SWIFT deferred the MT101 relay cutover because a significant number of banks and corporates were not ready, and forcing the date would have broken live payment flows. The deferral does not mean the migration has stopped. It means the coexistence period is longer, and banks must fund and staff dual-format processing for longer than they planned. A business analyst should treat the published timeline as a moving target and design for coexistence, not for a single big-bang date. For a business analyst, the migration work is a set of testable requirements. The payment hub must process both MT and MX for the same corridors, translate between them using the published rules, and log what was truncated in each translation. The UETR must be carried in both formats so the payment can be tracked across the format change. There must be a fallback rule for a correspondent that cannot receive MX, and a cutover plan per corridor with a named owner and a rollback step. The acceptance criteria must include a test where the same payment is sent as MT and as MX and produces the same business outcome, and a test where a pacs.008 with a long structured remittance is translated to MT103 and the truncation is logged and visible to operations.
