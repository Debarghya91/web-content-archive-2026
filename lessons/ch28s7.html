Workshop Facilitation: Running a Payments Discovery Workshop

What you will learn

Your objective. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain workshop facilitation: running a payments discovery workshop. A discovery workshop brings all the stakeholders into one room, or one video call, for a focused session to agree on requirements, and it is the fastest way to resolve the questions that stall a payments project.

Why it matters. The analyst plans the workshop like any other deliverable.

Chapter objective: Learner applies business analysis techniques to a payments project, writing requirements, user stories, acceptance criteria, and a traceability matrix for a real payment feature. Learner applies this skill in realistic banking scenarios, with clear examples and correct terminology throughout.

Key learning points

  • Discovery workshop all stakeholders into one room
  • Practical technique
  • Analyst framing the trade-off with data, not choosing a side
  • Workshop-agreed classification for every requirement
  • Sign-off: formal step

Key terms

Reconciliation: The process of comparing records from two sources, such as a bank statement and an internal ledger, to confirm they agree.

Capture: The step in a card transaction where the merchant collects the authorised amount from the issuer, usually when goods are shipped.

Cut-off: The latest time a bank or payment system accepts a payment instruction for processing on a given value date.

Exercise

Route the payment described in this lesson and justify the route.

Use the routing rules, the correspondent chain and the charges in this lesson.

  1. What to do. Name each bank in the chain, the message used on each leg, and the charges deducted at each step.

Correct result. The route follows the correspondent chain from the debtor agent to the creditor agent, with each intermediary deducting its charge and passing the remainder on. The message on each leg carries the information the next bank needs.

Why. Routing is a business decision with a cost. The wrong route is slower, more expensive, or fails.

Show transcript

A discovery workshop brings all the stakeholders into one room, or one video call, for a focused session to agree on requirements, and it is the fastest way to resolve the questions that stall a payments project. The analyst plans the workshop like any other deliverable. A clear objective, a timed agenda, a list of decisions that must be made, and pre-reading sent two days in advance. A typical half-day agenda covers the As-Is process in the first hour, the pain points in the second, the To-Be process in the third, and the decisions and actions in the final thirty minutes. The analyst facilitates the workshop, which means keeping the discussion on track, making sure every voice is heard, and preventing any one person from dominating. A practical technique is to give each participant a fixed turn to speak, and to capture every point on a visible board so people can see their input recorded. When the conversation drifts into technical detail, the analyst parks the point in a list of open items and returns to the agenda. When two participants disagree, the analyst restates both positions neutrally and asks what evidence would settle the question. Conflicting requirements are normal in payments, because different teams optimise for different things. Operations may want a 2 in the afternoon cut-off so the team can finish reconciliation by 5 in the afternoon, while the business team wants a 5 in the afternoon cut-off to win corporate customers. The analyst does not choose a side, but frames the trade-off with data. Moving the cut-off to 5 in the afternoon adds 8,000 payments a day and requires two extra staff, while keeping it at 2 in the afternoon loses an estimated 40 corporate accounts a year. Presenting the trade-off in numbers lets the sponsor decide on facts rather than on who argued loudest. Prioritisation is handled with a simple framework that everyone in the room can use. The MoSCoW method sorts requirements into Must have, Should have, Could have and Will not have this time. A must-have for a UPI integration is the ability to raise a collect request; a should-have is the merchant dashboard; a could-have is a downloadable monthly report; and a will-not-have is support for recurring mandates in the first release. The workshop agrees the classification for every requirement, which stops scope creep later when someone asks for a could-have to be treated as a must-have. The workshop ends with a clear set of outputs, not with a vague sense of agreement. The analyst produces a decision log recording each decision, who made it and when, an action list with owners and dates, and a draft requirements list with the agreed priorities. These are circulated within 24 hours while the discussion is still fresh in everyone's mind. Participants have two working days to raise corrections, and silence after that period is treated as agreement, which prevents the workshop's decisions from being reopened weeks later. Sign-off is the formal step that turns workshop outputs into a baseline. The analyst sends the requirements document to the business sponsor, the operations head, the compliance lead and the IT architect, and each one signs to confirm that the document reflects what was agreed. Once signed, the requirements are frozen for the current release, and any change needs a change request with an impact assessment. This discipline protects the project from endless rework, and it protects the analyst, because the signed document is the record of what was agreed and by whom.
