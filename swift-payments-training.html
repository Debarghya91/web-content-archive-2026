SWIFT Payments Training

SWIFT carries the messages behind most cross-border payments. This track teaches SWIFT the way working BAs need it: read MT messages field by field, explain correspondent banking and nostro accounts, tell serial from cover, handle charges and FX, track payments with gpi, and explain the migration from MT to ISO 20022 under CBPR+. No prior SWIFT knowledge is assumed.

What Is SWIFT and How Does It Work?

SWIFT is a member-owned cooperative that provides the secure messaging network banks use to exchange payment instructions and other financial messages. It is not a bank, and it does not move money. When a bank sends an MT103, SWIFT delivers that message to the receiving bank securely and reliably. The money itself moves separately, through correspondent bank accounts and settlement systems. SWIFT's network is called SWIFTNet, and every institution on it is identified by a BIC, the Business Identifier Code.

SWIFT Network vs Payment Settlement

The distinction between messaging and settlement is the single most important idea in cross-border payments. SWIFT moves messages. Settlement moves money, and it happens through correspondent accounts: bank A holds an account with bank B, and the payment is settled by debiting and crediting those accounts. A payment can be messaged in seconds and settle hours later, or it can fail to settle even though the message was delivered perfectly. A BA who confuses the two will misdiagnose every delayed payment.

MT103 Explained With a Practical Example

The MT103 is the Single Customer Credit Transfer: the message a bank sends to move a customer's payment to another bank. Its fields tell the whole story. Field 20 is the sender's reference, the unique identifier for the payment. Field 32A gives the value date, currency and amount. Field 50 names the ordering customer. Field 59 names the beneficiary customer, with the account number and name. Field 70 carries the remittance information, what the payment is for. Field 71A states the charges: OUR means the sender pays all charges, SHA means charges are shared, BEN means the beneficiary pays them.

Example: a customer in Mumbai pays a supplier in Frankfurt EUR 50,000. The Indian bank sends an MT103 with field 20 as its reference, 32A as the value date and EUR 50,000, 50 as the customer, 59 as the supplier with its IBAN, 70 as the invoice number, and 71A as SHA. The receiving bank reads those fields, credits the supplier, and applies its own charges under the SHA instruction. Read the full field-by-field guide in MT103 Explained.

MT202 and MT202 COV Explained

The MT202 is the General Financial Institution Transfer: a bank moving its own money to another bank, for example to settle with a correspondent or fund a nostro account. The MT202 COV is the cover payment: it carries the settlement for a customer payment whose instruction travelled separately as an MT103. The cover carries the underlying customer details so that screening and compliance can still be performed on the settlement leg, which was the gap the COV was created to close. Read MT202 Explained and MT202 COV Explained.

Correspondent Banking and Nostro/Vostro Accounts

Most banks cannot hold accounts in every currency in every country, so they hold accounts with correspondent banks that can. A nostro account is an account a bank holds with another bank, in that bank's books, in a foreign currency: "our account with you". A vostro account is the same account seen from the other side: "your account with us". When bank A pays bank B's customer in euros, the money moves by debiting A's nostro account with B and crediting the customer's account. Correspondent banking is the plumbing of cross-border payments, and its costs, cut-off times and account balances shape how fast a payment can settle. Read Correspondent Banking Explained.

Serial vs Cover Payments

There are two ways to send a cross-border payment. In a serial payment, the MT103 travels from bank to bank along the chain, and each bank passes the funds to the next. In a cover payment, the customer instruction (MT103) goes directly to the beneficiary's bank, while the funds travel separately through a cover payment (MT202 COV) between the correspondent banks. Cover payments are faster for the instruction and give the beneficiary's bank full information upfront, but they need a separate settlement leg that must be matched to the instruction. Serial payments are simpler to trace but slower, because each bank in the chain must process the message and the funds in turn.

SWIFT gpi and Payment Tracking

SWIFT gpi (Global Payments Innovation) added tracking and transparency to cross-border payments. Every gpi payment carries a UETR, a Unique End-to-End Transaction Reference, which identifies the payment across every bank in the chain. Any bank in the chain can look up the payment's status, the fees deducted at each step, and the FX applied, and the end customer can see where the payment is. The gpi Tracker turns a cross-border payment from a black box into a traceable journey. Read SWIFT gpi Explained and UETR Explained.

SWIFT and ISO 20022 Migration

Under CBPR+ (Cross-Border Payments and Reporting Plus), SWIFT's cross-border payments migrated from MT messages to ISO 20022 MX messages, starting in November 2022, with MT messages for cross-border payments phased out by November 2025. The MT103 maps to a pacs.008, the MT202 to a pacs.009, and the MT202 COV to a pacs.009 with the underlying customer details carried in the message. The migration matters for BAs because every mapping, every test scenario and every exception workflow built on MT fields had to be rebuilt for the richer ISO 20022 data. The full story is in the ISO 20022 Training Course.

Payment Investigations and MT103 Field Analysis

When a cross-border payment is delayed, the investigation starts with the MT103. Field 20 gives the reference to trace. Field 32A confirms the value date and amount. Field 59 confirms the beneficiary details, and an error here is the most common reason for a payment sitting in a repair queue. Field 70 shows what the payment was for, and field 71A shows who should have borne the charges, which explains a short payment. The investigator then checks the gpi status by UETR, the pacs.002 status reports if the payment has migrated to ISO 20022, and the correspondent account balances. Most delays resolve to one of four causes: incomplete or incorrect beneficiary details, a repair queue hold, a cut-off missed, or a compliance screen.
