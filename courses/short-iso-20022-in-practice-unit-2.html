Lesson 2.1: From instruction to interbank message

From instruction to interbank message

A payment begins when a customer instructs its bank, usually with a pain.001. The bank receives the instruction and runs its own checks: is the account valid and funded, does the instruction carry the fields the bank needs, and does anything in it need a closer look. If the checks pass, the bank accepts the instruction and builds the interbank message, a pacs.008, which carries the payment to the next bank in the chain. Each bank in the chain does the same in turn: receive, check, accept, and send onward. The last bank in the chain is the beneficiary bank, which credits the beneficiary account. The customer never sees the pacs.008, and the beneficiary never sees the pain.001. Each message belongs to one leg of the journey, and the payment only moves as fast as the slowest check on the way.

Worked example. A London company pays a Singapore supplier USD 25,000 for invoice INV-8841. The company sends a pain.001 to its London bank. The bank checks the account and the instruction, accepts it, and sends a pacs.008 onward. The payment travels through the correspondent chain, each bank checking and forwarding it, until the Singapore bank credits the supplier. The supplier sees the invoice reference on its statement and matches the receipt without a phone call.

Try it yourself.

  1. Draw the journey as four boxes: customer, sending bank, banks in the middle, beneficiary bank.
  2. Write which message belongs to each leg: pain.001 from customer to bank, pacs.008 between banks.
  3. List three checks a bank runs before it accepts an instruction.
  4. Explain in two sentences why the beneficiary never sees the pain.001.

Quiz 2.1.1. Which message starts a customer credit transfer?





Why: The customer instructs its bank with a pain.001.

Quiz 2.1.2. What does the sending bank do after it receives the pain.001?





Why: The bank checks the instruction, accepts it, and builds the interbank pacs.008.

Quiz 2.1.3. Which bank credits the beneficiary account?





Why: The beneficiary bank credits the beneficiary account at the end of the chain.

Quiz 2.1.4. Why does a payment move only as fast as the slowest check?





Why: Every bank in the chain receives, checks and accepts before forwarding, so any slow check delays the payment.

Tool in action: Payment Lifecycle Simulator

What you do. Open the Payment Simulator on paymentstraininghub.com and pick the Payment Lifecycle Simulator. The payment is already set up: USD 25,000 from a London corporate to a supplier in Singapore, reference INV-8841, end to end reference LON-SIN-25000-01. Choose a rail, a speed, and optionally inject a failure, then press Start, or press Step to move through the stages one at a time.

What the tool shows. The payment travels through eight stages: initiation, payment acceptance, payment processing, correspondent receipt, settlement finality, beneficiary credit, notification and reconciliation. At each stage you see the time, the message exchanged, the validation result, the debits and credits, and the status change, plus what comes in, what goes out, what data is used, which system it touches, what happens on failure, and how it recovers.

What the run returns. In the fast case shown, the beneficiary is credited about 41 minutes after initiation. The debrief makes the key point: acceptance is not settlement, and settlement is not beneficiary credit. Each stage is a distinct event with its own message and status. You can also click a stage to jump to it, but only to a stage already reached or the next one, because a payment cannot be credited before it is settled.

Try it yourself.

  1. Run the simulator with the default payment and watch the status change at each stage.
  2. Write down the message exchanged at the initiation stage and at the settlement finality stage.
  3. Run it again with a failure injected and note what repair the payment needs before it can move again.
  4. Change the rail selector and note which stages drop out on other rails.

Lesson 2.2: Correlating with UETR and references

Correlating with UETR and references

A payment produces several messages, and an investigation means proving which messages belong to the same payment. Three references do that work. The UETR, the unique end to end transaction reference, is created once and carried on every message about the payment, so it is the strongest link. The end to end reference is chosen by the customer, usually an invoice number, and travels with the payment so the beneficiary can match it. The instruction identifier is assigned by the instructing party for its own instruction, and the message identifier identifies one single message. When messages arrive, match them in this order: UETR first, then the end to end reference, then the instruction identifier. If the UETR matches but the amount does not, stop and investigate, because something has changed on the way. If two messages share an end to end reference but have different UETRs, they are two different payments about the same invoice, not one payment.

Worked example. An analyst has three files about a payment of EUR 6,200: a pain.001, a pacs.008 and a pacs.002. All three carry the same UETR. The pain.001 and the pacs.008 both show the end to end reference INV-5520 and the amount EUR 6,200. The pacs.002 shows status ACCP for the same UETR. The analyst can now tell the customer the payment was accepted, and can prove it with three messages that all point to the same payment.

Try it yourself.

  1. Take a set of sample messages and list the UETR on each one.
  2. Check whether the end to end reference matches across the set.
  3. Write down what you would conclude if the UETR matched but the amount differed.
  4. Explain in two sentences why a shared invoice reference alone does not prove two messages are one payment.

Quiz 2.2.1. Which reference is the strongest link between messages about one payment?





Why: The UETR is created once and carried on every message about the payment.

Quiz 2.2.2. Who chooses the end to end reference?





Why: The customer chooses the end to end reference, usually an invoice number.

Quiz 2.2.3. What does the instruction identifier identify?





Why: The instruction identifier is assigned by the instructing party for its own instruction.

Quiz 2.2.4. Two messages share an invoice reference but have different UETRs. What does that mean?





Why: Different UETRs mean different payments, even when the invoice reference matches.

Lesson 2.3: Value dates and cut-offs in general terms

Value dates and cut-offs in general terms

Every payment has a value date, the date on which the funds become available to the beneficiary. It also has an execution date, the date the sending bank is asked to act. The two are not always the same: a payment instructed today can carry a value date in the future. Banks also have cut-off times, the latest time they accept instructions for same-day processing on a given rail. An instruction that arrives after the cut-off is processed on the next business day, and its value date moves with it. Business days matter too: a payment instructed late on a Friday may not be processed until Monday, and public holidays in any country on the route can delay it further. Never guess a cut-off time or a value date rule for a specific rail. The rule depends on the scheme, the rail and the banks involved, so check the current scheme rulebook for the payment in front of you.

Worked example. A company instructs a payment on a Friday afternoon, after its bank's cut-off for that rail. The bank accepts the instruction but processes it on the next business day, Monday, and the value date becomes Monday. The customer expected the money to arrive on Friday and calls to ask where it is. The analyst checks the instruction time against the cut-off, sees it arrived after the cut-off, and explains that the payment is on track for Monday value. No message failed. The calendar did the work.

Try it yourself.

  1. Write one sentence each for value date, execution date and cut-off time.
  2. List three things that can push a value date later: a missed cut-off, a weekend, and a public holiday on the route.
  3. Take a payment instructed late on a Friday and state which day you would expect processing, without naming a specific time.
  4. Write the sentence you would say to a customer whose payment missed the cut-off.

Quiz 2.3.1. What is the value date?





Why: The value date is the date the funds become available to the beneficiary.

Quiz 2.3.2. What is a cut-off time?





Why: A cut-off is the latest time for same-day processing on a given rail.

Quiz 2.3.3. An instruction arrives after the cut-off. What happens?





Why: After the cut-off, processing moves to the next business day.

Quiz 2.3.4. Where do you find the cut-off time and value date rules for a specific rail?





Why: Rules depend on the scheme, the rail and the banks involved, so check the current scheme rulebook.
