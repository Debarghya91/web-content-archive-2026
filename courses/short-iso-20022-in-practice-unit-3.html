Lesson 3.1: Returns with pacs.004

Returns with pacs.004

A return starts when a bank cannot complete a payment as instructed. The beneficiary bank, or a bank in the middle of the chain, sends a pacs.004 back along the route the payment came by. The return carries the original instruction identifier and the UETR so every bank can match it to the original payment, plus a return reason code that says why the money came back. The most useful reason codes describe the account: the account number is wrong, the account is closed, or the account cannot be credited. When a return arrives, do three things in order. First, match it to the original payment by UETR. Second, read the reason code and write it down in plain words. Third, compare the returned amount with the original amount, and if they differ, find out why before you speak to the customer. A return is not a mystery. The reason code is the answer, and the UETR is the proof.

Worked example. A payment of EUR 4,100 is sent to a supplier, but the account number in the instruction has two digits transposed. The beneficiary bank cannot find the account, so it sends a pacs.004 with the reason code for an incorrect account number and the original UETR. The sending bank matches the return, credits its customer, and the customer corrects the account number and sends the payment again. The whole investigation took one reason code and one matched UETR.

Try it yourself.

  1. Write the three steps to follow when a return arrives: match by UETR, read the reason code, compare the amounts.
  2. List three account-related reasons a payment might be returned.
  3. Take a pacs.004 sample and find the reason code and the original UETR.
  4. Write the sentence you would say to a customer whose payment was returned for a wrong account number.

Lesson 3.2: Cancellations with camt.056

Cancellations with camt.056

A cancellation starts with a camt.056 from the bank that wants the payment stopped. The request names the payment by its original instruction identifier and UETR and gives a reason, such as a customer request or an instruction sent by mistake. The bank that receives the request checks where the payment is. If the payment has not yet been credited to the beneficiary, the bank can usually stop it and confirm the cancellation. If the payment has already been credited, the bank cannot take the money back by cancelling, and it replies that cancellation is not possible. The customer must then ask the beneficiary for the money back, which is a commercial matter, not a message. The reply to a camt.056 is the only proof of the outcome. Until you have seen it, the payment is neither cancelled nor safe, and you should say so plainly to anyone who asks.

Worked example. A company sends EUR 7,300 to the wrong supplier and asks its bank to stop the payment. The bank sends a camt.056 with the UETR and the reason. The beneficiary bank checks and finds the payment was credited that morning, so it replies that cancellation is not possible. The company must now contact the wrong supplier and ask for the money back. The camt.056 did its job by asking, and the reply did its job by giving the true answer.

Try it yourself.

  1. Write the two outcomes a camt.056 can have: confirmed cancellation, or not possible.
  2. Explain in two sentences why a credited payment cannot be cancelled by message.
  3. List the two identifiers a camt.056 uses to name the payment.
  4. Write the sentence you would say to a customer whose cancellation was refused because the payment was already credited.

Quiz 3.2.1. Which message requests a cancellation?





Why: camt.056 is the cancellation request.

Quiz 3.2.2. What proves whether a payment was cancelled?





Why: Only the reply to the cancellation request proves the outcome.

Quiz 3.2.3. Why can a credited payment not be cancelled by message?





Why: Once credited, the money is in the beneficiary account and a cancellation message cannot remove it.

Quiz 3.2.4. Cancellation is not possible. What should the customer do next?





Why: Recovery from the beneficiary is then a commercial matter, not a message.

Lesson 3.3: RJCT handling and who bears the loss

RJCT handling and who bears the loss

RJCT is the status code for a rejected payment, and it appears in a pacs.002 when a bank refuses to process a payment. A rejection is not the same as a return. A rejection stops the payment before it moves onward, while a return sends money back after it has moved. When you see RJCT, read the reason code first: it names the problem, such as an invalid field, a failed check, or a closed account. Then decide what can be fixed. A wrong field can be corrected and the payment sent again. A failed compliance check cannot be fixed by retyping, and the payment must not be resent until the underlying issue is resolved. The hardest case is a return that arrives after the beneficiary was already credited. The money is in the wrong place, and someone must recover it. Who bears the loss depends on the facts and on the scheme rules: which bank made the error, what the scheme rulebook says, and whether the funds can be recovered from the beneficiary. Do not guess at liability. Record the facts, check the current scheme rulebook, and escalate through your bank's own process.

Worked example. A payment of EUR 12,000 is credited to a beneficiary account by mistake, because the account number in the instruction belonged to a different customer with a similar name. Two days later the error is found and a pacs.004 returns the funds, but the beneficiary has already spent part of them. The banks must now work out recovery from the beneficiary and, for any shortfall, who bears the loss under the scheme rules. The analyst writes down the facts, the messages and the timeline, and escalates. She does not promise the customer a refund date, because she does not know one.

Try it yourself.

  1. Write one sentence explaining the difference between a rejection and a return.
  2. List three things to do when you see RJCT: read the reason code, decide what can be fixed, and do not resend until the issue is resolved.
  3. Write down the three questions that decide who bears a loss: which bank made the error, what the scheme rulebook says, and whether the funds can be recovered.
  4. Write the sentence you would say to a customer when you do not yet know who bears the loss.
