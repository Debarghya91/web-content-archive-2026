Unit 0: Onboarding

What a payments BA does, how this course works, and the tools of the trade.

Lesson 0.1: What a payments BA does

A business analyst in payments sits between the business and the technology team. The business knows what it needs, for example fewer failed payments or clearer status updates for customers. The technology team knows how to build software, but it needs a precise description of what to build. The BA turns the business need into clear, testable requirements. That is the core of the job, and everything else in this course supports it. A payments BA also needs enough domain knowledge to ask the right questions. You do not need to operate a settlement system or write code, but you do need to know how a payment moves, which messages carry it, and where exceptions arise. Without that knowledge you cannot tell a good requirement from a vague one. Day to day, a BA runs workshops, interviews stakeholders, writes requirements and user stories, draws process flows, answers questions from developers and testers, and supports testing. Success is simple to judge. If the team builds the right thing the first time, with few surprises late in the project, the BA has done the job well.

Worked example. A bank wants customers to see the status of a payment online instead of calling support. The BA talks to operations to learn which statuses exist, to engineering to learn which data is available, and to compliance to learn what may be shown to a customer. The BA then writes the requirements, maps each status value to the message that carries it, such as a pacs.002 payment status report, and works with testers on real cases, including a payment that was returned. One short document, agreed by all three teams, prevents weeks of rework later.

Try it yourself.

  1. List three teams a payments BA works with, and write one question you would ask each team.
  2. Write one sentence, in your own words, describing what a BA does.
  3. Think of a payment you made recently and list everyone involved in moving it.
  4. Write down one thing you most want to learn in this course.

Lesson 0.2: How this course works

This course has five units, numbered 0 to 4, and you should work through them in order because each unit builds on the one before it. The recommended pace is one week per unit at 3 to 4 hours per week, which is enough for a working professional to make steady progress without falling behind. Every lesson follows the same shape. First comes the theory, then a worked payments example, then a short hands-on exercise, and finally a quiz of four questions. The exercises matter as much as the reading, because business analysis is a practical skill and you learn it by writing requirements, not only by reading about them. Keep a notebook, on paper or on screen, and keep every exercise in it. Your final assignment in Unit 4 will draw on that notebook. Quizzes are marked as you go. Pass all four questions in every lesson of a unit to complete that unit. Progress is stored in this browser only, so it stays on this device. There is nothing to submit. If you finish a unit, move on; if you struggle with a quiz, re-read the lesson and try again.

Worked example. A learner sets aside 30 minutes each weekday evening. On Monday she reads the theory and the worked example. On Tuesday she does the exercise and writes it in her notebook. On Wednesday she takes the quiz, re-reads anything she missed, and retakes it. By Friday she has finished one lesson, and by the end of the week she has finished the unit. After five weeks she has a notebook full of practice requirements, flows and message maps, and a finished assignment to show for it.

Try it yourself.

  1. Block 3 to 4 hours per week in your calendar for the next five weeks.
  2. Start a course notebook and label it with the course name and today.
  3. Read the three lessons of Unit 0 in order.
  4. Try one quiz, read the feedback, and retake any question you miss.

Lesson 0.3: The tools of the trade

A BA has a small set of tools, and each one answers a different question. A business requirements document, or BRD, records the agreed business need in one place. User stories slice the work into small pieces a team can build in a sprint. Acceptance criteria state when a story is done, in testable terms. A process flow shows the sequence of steps in a process, including decisions and exceptions. A message map ties each step of a flow to the message that carries it, such as a pacs.008 or a pacs.002. A glossary keeps terms consistent so that everyone means the same thing by the same word. A good BA picks the lightest tool that answers the question at hand. Over-documenting wastes time and nobody reads it; under-documenting causes rework because the team guesses. The skill is judgement: enough detail to build and test from, and no more. This course teaches each tool in turn, and the final assignment asks you to use them together.

Worked example. A team is building a return handling flow. The BRD records why returns matter and what is in scope. User stories slice it into pieces, such as matching a return to its original payment. The process flow shows the steps from receiving the return to updating the status. The message map shows that the return arrives as a pacs.004 payment return and the new status is reported by a pacs.002 payment status report. The glossary defines return, reversal and recall so nobody mixes them up. Five tools, one shared understanding.

Try it yourself.

  1. List the five tools in this lesson and write one sentence on what each one is for.
  2. Pick a simple payment and sketch its flow in five boxes.
  3. Write one user story for checking a payment status.
  4. Start a glossary with five payments terms and a short definition for each.

Quiz 0.3.1. What does a BRD record?





Why: The BRD is the single agreed record of what the business needs.

Quiz 0.3.2. What does a message map tie to a flow step?





Why: The map shows which message carries each step that crosses a boundary.

Quiz 0.3.3. Why keep a glossary?





Why: One shared definition per term stops misunderstandings between teams.

Quiz 0.3.4. Which tool shows the sequence of steps in a process?





Why: A process flow shows steps, decisions and exceptions in order.
