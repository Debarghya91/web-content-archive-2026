Unit 1: BA foundations in payments

The BA role in a payments team, stakeholders, how a payment moves, and the questions to ask.

Lesson 1.1: The BA in a payments team

A payments team brings together several roles, and the BA sits in the middle of them. The product owner decides what to build next. Engineers build it. Testers check it. Operations teams run it day to day. Compliance checks it against the rules. Support answers customers when something goes wrong. The BA connects these roles by keeping one shared understanding of what is being built and why. When an engineer asks what should happen in an unusual case, the BA finds the answer from the business and records it. When operations says a proposed design will be hard to run, the BA carries that concern back to the product owner. In agile teams this happens in backlog refinement, sprint planning and demos: the BA clarifies stories, answers questions as work progresses, and checks the finished work against the requirement. In a more traditional project the BA writes the requirements document first and supports the team through build and test. Either way the BA owns clarity, not decisions. The BA does not set business policy alone; the BA records the decision, who made it, and the reason for it, so the team can rely on it later.

Worked example. During sprint planning the engineers ask what should happen when a payment arrives with no matching account. Nobody in the room knows. The BA takes the question to the operations team, who say the payment must go to an exceptions queue with the reason recorded. The BA writes that rule into the story, adds an acceptance criterion for it, and tells the testers so they can test it. A question that could have become a defect in production becomes one clear line in a story.

Try it yourself.

  1. Draw a team of six roles and place the BA in the middle, with a line to each role.
  2. Write three questions that only the BA can answer in that team.
  3. Write down one decision a BA should not make alone, and who should make it.
  4. Note one way a BA helps during a demo.

Quiz 1.1.1. In backlog refinement the BA mainly does what?





Why: Refinement is where the BA makes sure every story is clear enough to build.

Quiz 1.1.2. Who decides business policy?





Why: The business decides; the BA records the decision and its reason.

Quiz 1.1.3. What does the BA own above all?





Why: The BA owns the shared understanding of what must be built.

Quiz 1.1.4. At a demo the BA mainly does what?





Why: The demo is where the BA checks what was built against what was asked for.

Lesson 1.2: Who the stakeholders are

Stakeholders are the people affected by a change, and a payments change has many of them. Customers want speed, clarity and a way to know where their money is. Operations teams want work they can actually do: clear exceptions, sensible queues, and no step that needs information they do not have. Compliance wants controls that stop bad payments and an audit trail that proves it. Risk wants limits and monitoring. Finance and treasury want correct settlement and reconciliation. Engineers want precise rules with no gaps. Support wants answers they can give to customers. Scheme representatives and correspondent banks sit outside the firm but still shape what is possible. Each group cares about something different, and their needs often conflict. The BA finds the stakeholders early, learns what each one cares about, and brings the conflicts into the open. A trade-off that is discussed and recorded is manageable; a trade-off discovered after launch is a defect. The most expensive stakeholder to miss is the quiet one, often operations, whose needs only appear when the system is live.

Worked example. A team is building a payment status enquiry feature. Customers want instant answers at any hour. Operations wants fewer phone calls but also wants every enquiry logged so patterns can be spotted. Compliance wants only permitted data shown on screen. Engineering wants one simple lookup. The BA writes down all four needs, agrees that logging stays in scope while instant answers cover only the most recent payments, and records the trade-off in the requirements. Nobody is surprised later.

Try it yourself.

  1. List six stakeholders for a status enquiry feature.
  2. Write one concern for each stakeholder you listed.
  3. Pick two conflicting concerns and write how you would handle the conflict.
  4. Note one stakeholder that teams often forget.

Quiz 1.2.1. Why identify stakeholders early?





Why: A missed stakeholder means requirements found late, which means rework.

Quiz 1.2.2. What do operations teams mainly care about?





Why: Operations lives with the system daily, so exceptions must be workable.

Quiz 1.2.3. What does the BA do with conflicting needs?





Why: Conflicts are brought into the open, agreed, and recorded.

Quiz 1.2.4. Which stakeholder cares most about what data may be shown?





Why: Compliance decides what customer data may be displayed.

Lesson 1.3: How a payment moves

A payment can travel by several instruments. A credit transfer moves money on the payer instruction. A direct debit pulls money under an agreed mandate. A card payment authorises a purchase against a card account. Each instrument travels over a rail, which is the system that carries it: a domestic clearing system, a real-time system, a card scheme, or a chain of correspondent banks for cross-border payments. Two words matter most. Clearing is the exchange of payment instructions between banks and the sorting out of who owes whom. Settlement is the movement of funds between accounts, usually at a central bank or a settlement agent, which makes the payment final. Clearing prepares; settlement completes. A single payment can pass through several systems, and every hand-off is a place where something can go wrong: a missing field, a failed check, a wrong account. The BA does not need to operate any of these systems, but must know the journey in general terms, because every requirement, flow and message map in this course follows that journey. If you cannot say where a payment is at each step, you cannot write a requirement for it.

Worked example. A customer sends money to a supplier abroad. The instruction leaves the bank and travels through correspondent banks, each of which checks the details it needs. Clearing sorts out who owes whom between the banks in the chain. Settlement moves the funds between settlement accounts so the payment is final. The beneficiary bank credits the supplier, and status messages travel back along the chain so each bank knows what happened. One instruction, several systems, and a status update needed at every hand-off.

Try it yourself.

  1. List four payment instruments.
  2. Sketch the journey of a credit transfer in six steps.
  3. Mark on your sketch where clearing happens and where settlement happens.
  4. Note two hand-offs where errors could occur.

Quiz 1.3.1. What is clearing, in general terms?





Why: Clearing exchanges and sorts instructions; settlement moves the funds.

Quiz 1.3.2. What is settlement?





Why: Settlement is the final movement of funds between settlement accounts.

Quiz 1.3.3. Which payment is started by the payer sending money?





Why: A credit transfer is instructed by the payer to move money to the payee.

Quiz 1.3.4. Why does the BA need to know the payment journey?





Why: Every hand-off is a place a requirement must cover.

Lesson 1.4: The questions a BA asks at each step

A BA works by asking questions, and good questions are specific, written down, and chased until they are answered. At initiation the BA asks who pays whom, how much, in what currency, and by when. At validation the BA asks what checks run and what happens when a check fails. At clearing the BA asks which rail carries the payment and what data travels with it. At settlement the BA asks when the payment becomes final and what happens if funds are short. At exceptions the BA asks who is told, how the problem is fixed, and what a reasonable wait looks like, and where a rule is uncertain the BA writes check the current scheme rulebook instead of guessing. A vague question gets a vague answer, so the BA rewrites until the question has one clear meaning. Every question goes into a log with four columns: the question, the owner, the date asked, and the answer. A log with an empty answer column is a risk list, and the BA reviews it at every team meeting until it is empty.

Worked example. A team is designing a return flow. The BA asks who can request a return, which reason codes exist, which message carries the return, what status is reported back, and what happens if the original payment cannot be found. The answers: the return travels as a pacs.004 payment return, the status is reported by a pacs.002 payment status report, and an unmatched return goes to operations as an exception. Five questions, one complete flow, no guessing.

Try it yourself.

  1. Pick one payment step and write five questions about it.
  2. Start a questions log with the columns: question, owner, date asked, answer.
  3. Rewrite one vague question to make it specific.
  4. Write one question you would ask compliance about a new feature.

Quiz 1.4.1. At validation a BA should ask what?





Why: Validation rules and failure behaviour are core requirements.

Quiz 1.4.2. Why keep a questions log?





Why: A log turns open questions into a visible list that gets closed.

Quiz 1.4.3. For a return, which message carries it?





Why: pacs.004 is a payment return.

Quiz 1.4.4. What makes a good BA question?





Why: Specific written questions get clear answers and can be chased.
