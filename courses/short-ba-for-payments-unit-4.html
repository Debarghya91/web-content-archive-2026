Unit 4: Final assignment

Write a BRD for a payments feature, with acceptance criteria and a process flow.

Lesson 4.1: The final assignment brief

Your final assignment is to write a BRD for one payments feature, and you may choose which one. Option A is a return handling flow: your system receives payment returns and must match them, update statuses, and raise exceptions for anything it cannot match. Option B is a status enquiry flow: customers ask where a payment is, and your system must answer with the current status or a clear message when the payment cannot be found. Pick the one that interests you more; both are judged the same way. Your deliverable has two parts. First, a BRD with background, objectives, scope in and out, requirements with IDs, assumptions, open questions, and acceptance criteria. Second, a process flow with a message map. Use everything from Units 1 to 3: stakeholder needs, testable requirements, Gherkin scenarios, flows with numbered steps, and the four messages pacs.008, pacs.002, pacs.004 and camt.056 where they apply. Work over one week at 3 to 4 hours. Keep it realistic: one feature, not a whole system. Your work is judged against the review checklist in Lesson 3, not against a model answer.

Worked example. A learner chooses the return handling flow. Her scope is inbound pacs.004 returns for credit transfers only. Out of scope is cancelling payments, which is a camt.056 cancellation request and a different flow, and handling returns for card payments, which is a different product. Her BRD headings are background, objectives, scope, stakeholders, requirements, assumptions, acceptance criteria. Her flow starts with receiving the pacs.004 and ends with either an updated status or an exception for operations. One feature, one page of scope, and she can start writing.

Try it yourself.

  1. Choose your feature: return handling or status enquiry.
  2. Write one paragraph on why you chose it.
  3. List your BRD headings.
  4. Sketch your flow in six boxes.

Lesson 4.2: Writing your BRD

Start from scope, because scope decides everything else. Write what is in and what is out, then write the requirements, one idea each, with IDs. For a return flow, the requirements cover receiving a pacs.004, matching it to the original payment, updating the status, reporting the status by pacs.002, and raising an exception for an unmatched return. For a status enquiry, they cover validating the reference, looking up the payment, showing the current status, handling not found, and logging the enquiry. Add acceptance criteria in Gherkin for the main stories, covering the happy path and at least one error case. Draw the flow with numbered steps and build the message map beside it. List your assumptions and open questions honestly; a BRD that pretends to know everything is less trustworthy than one that says what it does not know. Then re-read the whole document and strike out every vague word. If you need a real failure to write about, use an investigation as your source material: a worked exception shows you exactly which requirements the system is missing.

Writing your BRD

  1. Source failure. A pacs.008 rejected at validation, EUR 18,400.00, SEPA Credit Transfer, status RJCT, is the source material.
  2. R1. When a pacs.008 fails validation, the system must record the failed field and the reason.
  3. R2. When a payment is rejected, the system must report the rejection by pacs.002 so the sender knows.
  4. Scope first. Write what is in and what is out, then write the requirements.
  5. Requirements. One idea each, with IDs, in plain language, and testable.
  6. BRD sections. Add Gherkin acceptance criteria, the numbered flow and message map, and list assumptions and open questions honestly.

Lesson 4.3: Reviewing your own work

Review your BRD twice: once for completeness and once for clarity. The completeness check asks whether the scope is clear and bounded, whether every requirement has an ID and is testable, whether acceptance criteria cover the happy path and the errors, whether the flow covers the main path and the exceptions, whether every flow step that crosses a boundary has a message, whether the gaps are closed for not found, unmatched and failure cases, and whether assumptions and open questions are listed. The clarity check asks whether the language is plain, whether any vague word survives, and whether a colleague who has not seen the work could read it and build from it. Read your BRD aloud and fix any sentence you stumble on; if you cannot say it clearly, it is not written clearly. If you can, ask a colleague to read it. A fresh reader finds what you cannot see, because you know what you meant and they only know what you wrote. Fix the three weakest items, then stop. A BRD is finished when it is complete and clear, not when it is long.

Worked example. A learner runs the checklist against her return handling BRD. Completeness finds a hole: her BRD has no step for an unmatched pacs.004, so a return with no matching original payment would vanish. She adds a requirement to raise an exception for the operations team and a flow branch to match. Clarity finds a vague sentence: the system must handle returns quickly. She rewrites it to name the matching rule and the exception behaviour. Two fixes, and her BRD now passes the checklist it will be judged against.

Try it yourself.

  1. Run the checklist against your BRD.
  2. Fix the three weakest items you find.
  3. Read your BRD aloud and fix any sentence you stumble on.
  4. Note what you would improve next time.
