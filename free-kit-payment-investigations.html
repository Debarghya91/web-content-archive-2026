Preview: the three-check method

Use this for any missing payment before raising an investigation or resending anything. Run the checks in order. Most cases are solved by the third check.

Check 1: the status report. Read the pacs.002 for the payment's UETR. ACSC means accepted and settled: the money moved between banks, so the problem is the account credit, not the payment. RJCT means it never left: read the reason code and fix that. ACCP or ACSP means it is still in process: do not resend, because a resend risks a duplicate. PDNG means it is waiting: find out what it is waiting for and who owns the wait.

Check 2: the value date against the cut-off. Read the value date in the pain.001 and compare it with the scheme cut-off table for that corridor. A payment entered after cut-off settles in the next cycle, usually the next business day. The beneficiary is right to say nothing arrived, and the customer is right to say the money left the account. Both are true, and the payment is not lost. The fix is a cut-off warning at entry time, not an investigation.

Check 3: the cover leg. In a serial payment, the instruction and the funds travel together through the chain. In a cover payment, the customer instruction goes directly to the beneficiary bank while the funds travel separately through a cover payment between correspondent banks. The debit on the customer's statement proves only that the first leg moved. Read the cover leg status under the same UETR. If the cover leg is missing or rejected, the beneficiary bank has the instruction but no funds, and that is the cause.

After the three checks. If all three checks pass and the payment is still missing, raise an investigation to the next bank in the chain, quoting the UETR, the latest status, the value date, and the cover leg status. An investigation that quotes all four gets an answer. An investigation that says only "payment missing" gets a request for more information.

Preview: investigation checklists

Missing payment checklist. UETR confirmed. Latest pacs.002 status and reason code read. Value date checked against the cut-off table. Cover leg status checked. Sanctions queue checked. Clearing cycle confirmed. Account posting checked with the camt.054. Only then raise an investigation, quoting all of the above.

Duplicate payment checklist. Both credits identified with their UETRs. Same UETR on both means one payment processed twice. Different UETRs means two separate payments. Second payment stopped before further processing. Beneficiary bank notified. Return arranged through a pacs.004. Cause found: usually a retry after a timeout without an idempotency key.

Delayed payment checklist. Value date and cut-off confirmed. Clearing cycle confirmed. Liquidity at the next bank checked. Sanctions hold checked. Queue position confirmed. Customer told the realistic arrival time, not a guess.

Returned payment checklist. Return reason code read. Original payment found by UETR. Customer told the funds are returning, not that the payment failed. Original instruction corrected if the reason code points to the instruction. Resubmission uses the same UETR so tracking stays continuous.

Decision tree: failed and delayed payments

Start: a payment is reported as failed or delayed. Step 1: read the latest pacs.002 for the UETR. If the status is RJCT, read the reason code and go to the fix for that code: AC01 means the account is wrong, correct it and resubmit; AM05 means it is a duplicate, stop and do not resubmit; RC01 means the bank identifier is wrong, correct the routing. If the status is ACSC, the payment settled between banks, so the problem is the account credit: check the camt.054 and the account posting. If the status is ACCP or ACSP, the payment is in process: check the value date against the cut-off, then check the queue, then check the sanctions hold, then check liquidity at the next bank. If the status is PDNG, find out what the payment is waiting for and who owns the wait. Step 2: if no pacs.002 exists at all, the payment may never have been sent, or the status report may have been lost. Check the hub log for the UETR: if the pacs.008 was sent, ask the next bank for a status; if the pacs.008 was never sent, the payment is stuck at initiation, and the cause is in the validation or the queue. Step 3: if the payment is in process past its expected settlement time, escalate to operations with the UETR, the latest status, the value date, and the cover leg status. Do not resend while the status is ACCP or ACSP.

The requirement that prevents the next one. Every payment must have a visible status at all times, and a payment that is past its expected settlement time must appear in an exception queue with an owner. A payment that is invisible is a payment that will be reported missing by the customer.

Decision tree: cover leg failures

Start: the beneficiary bank has the instruction but no funds. Step 1: confirm the payment method. If it is a serial payment, the instruction and the funds travel together, so a missing credit means the chain broke at an intermediary: read the status from each bank in the chain. If it is a cover payment, the customer leg and the cover leg are separate: read the cover leg status under the same UETR. Step 2: if the cover leg is missing, the funds never left the correspondent: check the correspondent's liquidity and whether the cover payment was ever instructed. If the cover leg was rejected, read its reason code. Step 3: if the cover leg settled but the beneficiary bank still has no funds, the cover payment went to the wrong account or the wrong correspondent: check the cover payment's creditor agent against the beneficiary bank's settlement account. Step 4: tell the customer the truth: the instruction arrived, the funds are waiting on the cover leg, and the expected arrival time is the cover leg settlement time, not the instruction time.

The requirement that prevents the next one. Both legs of a cover payment must be visible under one UETR, in one view, so operations can see the cover leg status without a manual investigation. A cover payment tracked as two separate payments will always be investigated as if it were lost.

Decision tree: sanctions holds

Start: a payment has no status and no rejection, and the customer says it is lost. Step 1: check the sanctions screening queue for the UETR. If the payment is in the queue, it is held for manual review, not lost and not rejected. Step 2: find out why it was held: a name that partially matches a listed entity, a country on a watch list, or a purpose code that needs review. Step 3: check how long it has been held and whether the review has an owner and a deadline. A hold without a deadline is a payment that will sit until the customer complains. Step 4: tell the customer the payment is under review, and give a realistic time for the review to complete. Do not tell the customer the payment failed, and do not resend it, because a resend will be held for the same reason.

The requirement that prevents the next one. A held payment must produce a status visible to operations within an agreed time, and the customer must be told the payment is under review rather than left to assume it failed. A hold that is invisible to operations and to the customer is indistinguishable from a loss.

Decision tree: duplicate payments after a timeout

Start: two credits appear for the same amount on the same day. Step 1: pull both pacs.008 messages and compare the UETRs. Same UETR means one payment processed twice: a duplicate. Different UETRs means two separate payments, and the second may be legitimate. Step 2: if it is a duplicate, stop any further processing of the second payment immediately, and notify the beneficiary bank. Step 3: arrange the return of the duplicate funds through a pacs.004, referencing the original UETR. Step 4: find the cause. The usual cause is a retry after a timeout without an idempotency key: the channel timed out, the user retried, and the first instruction had actually been processed. The fix is idempotency: the same instruction carries the same key, and the receiving system returns the original result instead of creating a second payment.

The requirement that prevents the next one. Every payment instruction must carry an idempotency key, and a retry with the same key must return the original result, not create a second payment. The retry path must be tested explicitly, including the timeout case, because the happy path never creates a duplicate.

Decision tree: status mismatches across systems

Start: two systems disagree about a payment. The hub says sent, the core says never received. Or the pacs.002 says settled, but the account was never credited. Step 1: identify the two systems and the exact point where they disagree, using the UETR as the common key. Step 2: check the queue between them: a message can sit in a queue, or be delivered while its acknowledgement is lost, so the sender marks it sent and the receiver never processes it. Step 3: check the reconciliation between the two systems: a payment present in one and missing in the other is the gap, and the gap needs a named owner. Step 4: decide which system is right, from the evidence, not from seniority: the queue log, the acknowledgement log, and the account posting are the evidence. The system that claims the payment was sent must show the acknowledgement. The system that claims it was never received must show the missing entry in its own log.

The requirement that prevents the next one. A reconciliation job must run on a schedule, not on demand, and must flag any payment present in one system and missing in another, with a named owner for the gap. Two systems that can disagree and never notice will be discovered by customers, not by operations.

Decision tree: reconciliation breaks and orphan returns

Start: a return arrives with no original payment to match it, or a settlement does not match the messages. Step 1: read the return's references: UETR, TxId, EndToEndId, and the original instruction reference. Match on the UETR first, then on the EndToEndId, because a return may carry a different TxId but must carry the same UETR. Step 2: if no match is found, check whether the original payment was processed under a different reference, for example after a system migration or a manual repair that changed the reference. Step 3: if the original truly cannot be found, the return is an orphan: money with no case. Hold it in a suspense account, assign an owner, and investigate the reference chain across both systems. Do not apply an orphan return to the wrong payment. Step 4: for a reconciliation break, compare the three records separately: the message log, the settlement record, and the account posting. The break is where the three disagree, and the fix belongs to whichever record is wrong, not to the other two.

The requirement that prevents the next one. Every return must be matchable to its original payment by at least two independent references, and every settlement must reconcile to the messages and to the account postings. A return that cannot be matched is not a minor exception. It is money with no owner, and it must be treated as one.

Worked examples

Example 1: The payment that left the account and never arrived. A customer calls on day two. The debit is on the statement, the beneficiary says nothing arrived. Run the three checks: the pacs.002 shows ACCP, so the payment is in process; the value date is Tuesday because the instruction missed the Monday cut-off; the cover leg is waiting for liquidity at the correspondent. The payment settles on Wednesday. No resend, because a resend would pay the supplier twice. The full case study is in the BA Starter Kit.

Example 2: The duplicate that was almost resent a third time. A beneficiary is credited twice for the same invoice. Operations sees the second credit, assumes the first payment failed, and prepares to send it a third time. The UETR on both credits is the same, which proves it is one payment processed twice after a timeout retry. The third payment is stopped, the duplicate is returned through a pacs.004, and the idempotency requirement is written the same day.

Example 3: The sanctions hold that looked like a loss. A payment to a new supplier has no status for three days. The customer is told the payment is lost and a replacement is prepared. The screening queue shows the payment held for a partial name match, with no owner and no deadline. The replacement would have been held for the same reason. The hold is cleared, the payment settles, and the queue is given an owner and a deadline the same week.
