SWIFT Payments: The Complete Guide

SWIFT is the messaging network banks use to instruct cross-border payments. When a bank in one country pays a bank in another, it almost always sends a SWIFT message. SWIFT does not move money: it carries the instruction, and the money moves separately through correspondent accounts. This guide explains how SWIFT works, the MT messages that matter, how a cross-border payment actually travels, what charges and tracking mean, what goes wrong, and how the network is moving to ISO 20022.

What SWIFT is, and what it is not

SWIFT (the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication) is a member-owned cooperative that runs a secure messaging network for financial institutions. Over 11,000 institutions in more than 200 countries use it. A SWIFT message is an instruction, not a transfer of funds: it tells a bank to pay, to report, to confirm, or to investigate. The funds themselves move by debiting and crediting accounts that banks hold with each other, called nostro and vostro accounts.

Three common misunderstandings. SWIFT is not a payment system: it has no central account and settles nothing. SWIFT is not a bank: it holds no customer funds. And SWIFT is not the only way to send a cross-border payment: regional systems, bilateral links and instant-payment corridors exist, but SWIFT remains the default for bank to bank cross-border traffic. Read the full introduction in SWIFT payments explained.

The MT messages that matter

SWIFT MT (FIN) messages are identified by a three-digit category and number. The ones a payments professional meets most are in Category 1 and 2 for customer and bank transfers, Category 9 for cash management, and the gpi tracking messages.

Each message has fixed fields with strict formats: field 20 is the transaction reference, field 32A is the value date, currency and amount, fields 50 and 59 are the ordering customer and the beneficiary, field 70 is remittance information, and field 72 is sender-to-receiver information. A single wrong character in a field can reject the message or send the funds to the wrong place. See the field detail in MT103 explained and MT202 explained.

How a cross-border payment actually travels

A cross-border payment moves in one of two ways. In a serial payment, the instruction passes from bank to bank along a chain: debtor bank to intermediary to creditor bank, each debiting and crediting the next. In a cover payment, the customer instruction travels directly from the debtor bank to the creditor bank, while the settlement funds travel separately through the correspondent chain as a cover payment. The cover method is faster for the instruction and is the norm for high-value payments, but it creates the reconciliation problem of matching a cover to its customer instruction.

The accounts that make this work are nostro and vostro. A nostro account is an account a bank holds with another bank, in that bank's currency. A vostro account is the same account seen from the other side: an account a bank holds for another bank. When Bank A pays Bank B in euros, Bank A debits its nostro account with Bank B, or Bank B credits Bank A's vostro account with it. Liquidity, cut-off times and account balances decide whether a payment can go out now or must wait. Read correspondent banking explained, nostro vs vostro and serial vs cover payments.

Charges, FX, and what the beneficiary receives

Every cross-border payment carries a charge instruction in field 71A: OUR, SHA or BEN. OUR means the ordering customer pays all charges, so the beneficiary receives the full amount. SHA means charges are shared: the sender pays its bank's charges and the beneficiary pays the rest. BEN means the beneficiary pays all charges, so the amount credited is the amount sent minus every charge along the chain. The charge instruction is a frequent source of disputes, because a beneficiary who expected the full amount receives less, and the difference is rarely explained on the statement. See OUR, SHA and BEN explained.

Where a payment changes currency, the FX rate and the party that converts it must be explicit. The rate is agreed at instruction time or applied by the converting bank, and the spread is a cost the customer rarely sees itemised. A payment that crosses currencies and correspondent chains can lose value at each step, which is why the full amount and the final credited amount are different numbers and both must be reconcilable.

gpi and UETR: tracking a payment

SWIFT gpi (Global Payments Innovation) added tracking and transparency to cross-border payments. Every gpi payment carries a Unique End-to-End Transaction Reference (UETR), a UUID that stays with the payment from the debtor bank to the creditor bank. Each bank on the chain confirms receipt and onward transmission, so the payment's status, the charges deducted, and the FX applied are visible to the sender and, where the bank exposes it, to the customer. The UETR is the single most useful field for investigations: give an operations team a UETR and they can trace the payment without asking each bank in turn. Read SWIFT gpi explained and UETR explained, and see a tracking failure worked through in UETR tracking across the full chain.

What goes wrong

Cross-border payments fail in a small number of repeatable ways. A message arrives without a Relationship Management Application (RMA) authorisation, so the receiving bank rejects it. A field 57A (account with institution) is missing, so the receiving bank does not know where to send the funds onward. A cover arrives before the customer instruction, or after it, and the two cannot be matched. A beneficiary name is truncated by the 35-character field limit and does not match the account. A payment is held for sanctions screening because a name matches a list entry. Each of these has a known investigation path, and each is cheaper to prevent at instruction time than to fix after the funds have moved.

Worked through in the exception scenarios: missing RMA authorisation, MT103 serial payment missing field 57A, cover arriving before the customer payment, customer payment arriving before the cover, multiple cover messages and reference reconciliation, and field 72 differences between MT103 and MT202 COV. The full set is in the payment exception scenarios.

MT to MX and CBPR+

SWIFT's CBPR+ programme retires MT103, MT202 and MT202 COV for cross-border payments and replaces them with pacs.008, pacs.009 and pacs.009 COV. The migration started in March 2023 with a coexistence period in which banks accept both formats. The change is not only a format change: ISO 20022 carries structured data that MT could not, so addresses, remittance information and party identifiers are richer, and screening and reconciliation improve. The risk is in the mapping: fields that were free text in MT must land in structured elements in MX, and anything that does not map cleanly is truncated or lost. Read MT to MX migration, MT to MX field mapping, MT103 vs pacs.008, and the worked mappings MT103 to pacs.008, MT202 to pacs.009 and MT101 to pain.001. The ISO 20022 side is covered in the ISO 20022 complete guide.

What a BA needs to know

A business analyst on a SWIFT project needs to read MT messages, trace a payment through the correspondent chain, and write requirements that survive contact with the network. That means knowing the fields that decide routing (56A, 57A, 58A), the fields that decide charges (71A) and value (32A), and the fields that cause the most exceptions (50, 59, 70, 72). It means writing mapping specifications from MT to MX with transformation rules for every field, and test scenarios that cover the serial and cover routes, the missing-field cases, and the RMA failure. See the Payments BA complete guide for the full BA view.

Official references

SWIFT publishes its message standards, usage guidelines and the CBPR+ migration timeline through SWIFT and its MyStandards portal. The ISO 20022 message definitions are published at iso20022.org. Scheme rules for a given payment (SEPA, TARGET, CHAPS, Fedwire) are published by the scheme owner. Always check the current version of the standard and the scheme usage guide before relying on a field rule.
