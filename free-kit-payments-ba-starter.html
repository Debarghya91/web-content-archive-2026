Preview: the end-to-end payment lifecycle

A customer payment passes through nine stages. Most incidents are a failure at one stage being misread as a failure at another. Read the stage, then the break.

1. Initiation. The customer or corporate system instructs the payment: amount, currency, value date, beneficiary, remittance. Break: an instruction with a past value date, a missing charge option, or an amount that breaches a limit. The payment never starts, and the customer is told it failed.

2. Validation and enrichment. The bank checks the account, the balance, the beneficiary details, and completes the instruction with routing data. Break: a valid-looking IBAN with a wrong check digit, or a beneficiary name that does not match the account. The payment is rejected here, before any money moves.

3. Sanctions and compliance screening. Names, countries and purpose codes are screened against sanctions and watch lists. Break: a name that partially matches a listed entity. The payment is held, not rejected, and no status is sent to the customer. A held payment looks exactly like a lost payment from the outside.

4. FX conversion. For a cross-currency payment, the amount is converted at an agreed rate. Break: the rate is applied to the wrong leg, or the FX is booked after the cut-off, so the value date slips a day.

5. Routing. The bank chooses the path: direct, through a correspondent, or through a clearing system. Break: the chosen correspondent has no liquidity in that currency at that hour, so the payment queues.

6. Messaging. The instruction travels as a message: MT103 or pacs.008 for a customer payment, MT202 or pacs.009 for a bank-to-bank movement. Break: the message is schema-valid but carries wrong business data, for example a value date that the receiving scheme will not accept. The message is accepted by the network and rejected by the next bank.

7. Clearing. The payment enters a clearing system that nets or queues it with other payments. Break: the payment misses the clearing cut-off. It is not lost. It settles in the next clearing cycle, usually the next business day.

8. Settlement. Money actually moves: accounts at the central bank or correspondent banks are debited and credited. Break: the sending bank has insufficient liquidity at settlement time. The payment waits, and the beneficiary sees nothing.

9. Reconciliation. Each bank matches the message, the settlement and the account posting. Break: the message says settled but the account was never credited, or the account was credited twice after a retry. This is where duplicates and orphans are found, usually days later.

The rule that resolves most missing-payment complaints: check the message status first, then the value date against the cut-off, then the cover leg. Three checks, in that order, before anyone raises an investigation or resends anything.

Preview: ISO 20022 message reference sheet

pain.001, Customer Credit Transfer Initiation. The customer tells its bank to move money. Sent by a corporate or retail customer system to its bank. The BA reads it to check the instruction: debtor, creditor, amount, value date, remittance, charge bearer. If the instruction is wrong here, every message after it inherits the error.

pacs.008, FI to FI Customer Credit Transfer. The bank-to-bank message that carries a customer payment. Sent by the debtor bank to the creditor bank, directly or through correspondents. The BA reads it to follow the payment, check the charges and the remittance data, and confirm which leg is stuck. The UETR in the pacs.008 is the key that links every later message about the same payment.

pacs.009, FI to FI Financial Institution Credit Transfer. A bank moving its own money, for example to settle with a correspondent or to fund a nostro account. No customer behind it, no customer charge option, no remittance information for a beneficiary. The BA who mistakes a pacs.009 for a pacs.008 will look for customer data that does not exist.

pacs.002, FI to FI Payment Status Report. The reply that says what happened: ACCP (accepted), ACSP (settlement in process), ACSC (settled), RJCT (rejected, with a reason code). The BA reads the pacs.002 before doing anything else in an investigation. ACSC is the only status that means the beneficiary was paid. ACCP means accepted for processing, nothing more.

pacs.004, Payment Return. A payment that was accepted and then returned, for example because the beneficiary account was closed after acceptance. The BA checks the return reason code and the original UETR, then tells the customer the funds are coming back, not that the payment failed.

camt.056, FI to FI Payment Cancellation Request. A request to stop a payment that is already in flight. It does not guarantee the payment stops. The BA tracks whether the cancellation was accepted or rejected, and whether the funds were already settled.

Read the full guides: pain.001, pacs.008, pacs.009, pacs.002, pacs.008 vs pacs.009, ISO 20022 Explained.

Preview: SWIFT MT vs MX

MT103 vs pacs.008. Both instruct a customer credit transfer. The MT103 carries the instruction in fixed fields with free-text addresses and remittance information. The pacs.008 carries the same instruction with structured addresses, structured remittance data, and a published schema. A payment that is an MT103 in one leg can be a pacs.008 in the next leg during migration, and the mapping between them is where data is lost.

MT202 vs pacs.009. Both move a bank's own money. The MT202 COV variant carries cover payment data for a customer payment that travelled separately. The pacs.009 carries the same bank-to-bank movement with structured data. The cover leg is the one most often missed in an investigation, because the customer instruction and the funds travel on different messages.

MT900 and MT910 vs camt.054. Debit and credit confirmations. The MT versions confirm a single debit or credit on an account. camt.054 does the same with structured data and a direct link to the original payment through the UETR.

MT940 and MT942 vs camt.053 and camt.052. Account statements and intraday reports. The MT versions are end-of-day and intraday account statements in fixed format. The camt versions carry the same reporting with structured entries, balances and references a reconciliation system can match automatically.

What migration changes for a BA. The messages get richer, but the failure modes get subtler. A message can pass schema validation and still fail business validation. A structured address can be truncated back to free text when it crosses into an MT leg. A field that exists in MX may have no MT equivalent, so the mapping workbook must record what is lost and the business must accept it in writing. Read the MT to MX Migration Guide and the MT to MX Field Mapping.

10 real-world payment scenarios

Each scenario gives the situation, what is actually happening, and the check that resolves it. These are the cases that fill an operations queue.

Scenario 1: The payment left the account, but the beneficiary never got it. A customer calls on day two. The debit is on the statement, the beneficiary says nothing arrived. Run three checks in order. First, the pacs.002 status report: ACSC means accepted and settled, RJCT means it never left. Second, the value date against the cut-off: a payment sent after cut-off settles the next business day, and the beneficiary is right to say nothing arrived yet. Third, the cover leg: in a serial payment the customer leg and the cover leg are two different movements, and the debit proves only the first one moved. Most missing-payment cases are solved by reading those three, not by resending the payment. Resending is how a customer gets paid twice.

Scenario 2: The value date is tomorrow, but the customer expected the money today. The instruction carried a value date one business day out, because the payment was entered after the cut-off. The system accepted the instruction and applied the next available value date, but nobody told the customer. The check is the value date in the pain.001 against the scheme cut-off table. The fix is a cut-off warning at entry time, not an investigation. The BA writes the requirement: if the requested value date cannot be met, the channel must say so before the customer confirms.

Scenario 3: The cover leg failed, but the customer leg succeeded. In a cover payment, the MT103 goes directly to the beneficiary bank while the funds travel separately through an MT202 COV between correspondents. The beneficiary bank received the instruction and is waiting for funds that never arrived, because the cover payment was rejected for insufficient liquidity. The customer sees a debit and no credit. The check is the cover payment status, not the customer instruction. The two legs must be tracked as one payment, using the same UETR, or operations will chase the wrong leg.

Scenario 4: A pacs.002 RJCT that does not mean the payment failed. A payment is rejected with reason code AC01, incorrect account number. The operations team treats it as a failed payment and tells the customer to send it again. But the rejection happened before settlement, so no money moved, and the original instruction can be corrected and resubmitted with the same UETR. The mistake is treating a pre-settlement rejection as a settled failure. The check is the status code and the reason code together: RJCT with AC01 means fix the account and resubmit; RJCT after ACSC would mean something else entirely, and is far rarer.

Scenario 5: Sanctions screening holds a payment without rejecting it. A beneficiary name partially matches a sanctions list entry. The payment is held for manual review. No pacs.002 is sent, no status reaches the customer, and the payment sits in a queue. From the outside it looks lost. The check is the screening queue for the UETR, not the messaging log. The BA designs the requirement: a held payment must produce a status visible to operations within an agreed time, and the customer must be told the payment is under review rather than left to assume it failed.

Scenario 6: A valid ISO 20022 message with wrong business data. A pacs.008 passes schema validation: the XML is well formed, every mandatory element is present, every value matches its data type. It is then rejected by the receiving bank because the value date is a bank holiday in the beneficiary country, or the currency is not supported on that corridor. Schema validation checks the shape of the message. Business validation checks whether the payment can actually happen. The BA must test both layers, and must know that a message passing the first layer can still fail the second.

Scenario 7: A duplicate payment after a timeout. A channel sends a payment instruction and waits for an acknowledgement. The acknowledgement is slow, the channel times out, and the user retries. The first instruction was actually processed. Now two payments exist for one instruction, and the beneficiary is paid twice. The fix is idempotency: the same instruction carries the same idempotency key, and the receiving system recognises the second submission as a repeat of the first instead of a new payment. The BA writes the acceptance criteria: a retry with the same key must return the original result, not create a second payment. Read the Idempotency in Payment APIs guide and the Duplicate Payments guide.

Scenario 8: The status says settled, but the account was never credited. The pacs.002 reports ACSC, so operations closes the case. The beneficiary still has no money. The settlement leg completed, but the account posting failed, or the credit went to the wrong account because of a mapping error in the beneficiary bank. The check is the camt.054 credit notification and the account posting, not the pacs.002 alone. ACSC means settlement completed between banks. It does not prove the beneficiary account was credited. The two must be reconciled separately.

Scenario 9: A return with no original payment to match it. A pacs.004 return arrives, but the operations team cannot find the original payment. The return references a UETR that does not exist in the local system, because the original payment was processed under a different reference after a system migration. The return becomes an orphan: money with no case. The check is the full reference chain (UETR, TxId, EndToEndId, and the original instruction reference) across both systems. The BA designs the requirement: every return must be matchable to its original payment by at least two independent references.

Scenario 10: The payment is stuck between two systems that disagree. The payment hub says the payment was sent. The core banking system says it was never received. The message sits in a queue between them, or it was delivered but the acknowledgement was lost, so the hub marked it sent and the core never processed it. This is an eventual consistency problem: two systems hold different views of the same payment, and neither is wrong from its own side. The check is the reconciliation between the hub log, the queue, and the core posting, using the UETR as the common key. The fix is a reconciliation job that flags any payment present in one system and missing in the other, and a clear owner for the gap.

20 interview questions for payments BAs

Short model answers. Each answer is two to four sentences: the definition, the reason it matters, and the check or action.

1. Explain ISO 20022 to a non-technical stakeholder. ISO 20022 is a common language for financial messages. Before it, banks used MT messages, which are short text blocks with limited data, so a beneficiary address was four lines of free text and every bank read it differently. ISO 20022 replaces that with structured data, so the address, the remittance information and the purpose are separate fields a system can read and reconcile automatically. The industry moved because regulators want better data for sanctions screening, corporates want automatic reconciliation, and banks want fewer errors.

2. A pacs.008 is rejected with reason code AC01. What happened and what do you do? AC01 means incorrect account number. The beneficiary bank could not find the account stated in the pacs.008, and the payment was rejected before settlement, so no money moved. Read the pacs.002 to confirm the reason code and the original references, check the creditor account in the pacs.008 against the customer instruction, then correct it and resubmit as a new pacs.008 with a new message identification but the same UETR so tracking stays continuous.

3. What is the UETR and why is it critical? The UETR is the Unique End to End Transaction Reference, a UUID generated by the first bank in the payment chain and carried unchanged by every bank that handles the payment. It is the single key that links the pacs.008, every pacs.002 status report, any pacs.004 return, and any camt.056 cancellation. Without it, each bank sees only its own leg and the payment becomes untrackable, which is why SWIFT gpi relies on it.

4. Walk me through the difference between pacs.008 and pacs.009. pacs.008 is a customer credit transfer: it moves money on behalf of a customer and carries customer data, the debtor, the creditor, the instructed amount, the charge option, and the remittance information. pacs.009 is a bank to bank credit transfer: it moves a bank's own money, with no customer behind it and no customer charge option. The test is simple: if there is a customer, it is pacs.008; if it is the bank's own money, it is pacs.009.

5. A customer payment is accepted (ACCP) but never reaches ACSC. How do you investigate? ACCP means accepted for processing, not settled. Check the UETR in the gpi tracker for the last recorded status and which bank holds the payment, then check whether a further pacs.002 was received and missed by looking at the hub logs. If no further status arrived, send an investigation to the next bank in the chain, and check whether the payment is stuck in a queue, held for sanctions review, or waiting for liquidity. Do not resend the payment, because that risks a duplicate.

6. How does a pacs.002 status flow work? When a bank sends a pacs.008, the receiving bank replies with a pacs.002. ACCP means the payment passed validation and was accepted. ACSP means it was accepted and settlement is in process. ACSC means settlement is complete. RJCT means the payment was rejected, with a reason code explaining why. The BA reads the status and the reason code together, because the same status with a different reason code means a different fix.

7. What is the difference between a serial payment and a cover payment? In a serial payment, the instruction travels from bank to bank along the chain, and each bank passes the funds to the next. In a cover payment, the customer instruction goes directly to the beneficiary bank while the funds travel separately through a cover payment between correspondent banks. The cover method is faster for the instruction but creates two legs that must be tracked as one payment, and the cover leg is the one most often missed in an investigation.

8. What is a nostro account, and why does it matter for settlement? A nostro account is an account a bank holds with another bank, usually in a foreign currency. Settlement of a cross-border payment happens by debiting and crediting these accounts, not by moving physical money. If the sending bank has insufficient balance in its nostro account at settlement time, the payment waits, even though the message was sent hours earlier.

9. What happens when a payment misses its cut-off? The payment is not lost. It is held for the next clearing or settlement cycle, usually the next business day, and the value date moves with it. The customer must be told at entry time if the requested value date cannot be met, because the gap between the instruction date and the value date is the most common source of "where is my money" complaints.

10. How do you handle a duplicate payment? First, prove it is a duplicate: same UETR, same amount, same value date, same beneficiary, two postings. Then stop any further processing of the second payment, notify the beneficiary bank, and arrange the return of the duplicate funds through a pacs.004. Then fix the cause, which is usually a retry after a timeout without an idempotency key, so the same instruction was processed twice.

11. What is idempotency, and why does it matter in payment APIs? Idempotency means that processing the same request twice has the same effect as processing it once. In a payment API, the caller sends an idempotency key with the instruction, and the receiving system stores the key with the result. If the same key arrives again, the system returns the original result instead of creating a second payment. Without it, any retry after a timeout risks a duplicate debit.

12. Explain the difference between clearing and settlement. Clearing is the exchange and sorting of payment instructions: working out who owes whom, and netting payments where the scheme allows it. Settlement is the actual movement of money between accounts at the central bank or correspondent banks. A payment can be cleared and still unsettled, and the gap between the two is where most delays sit.

13. What is a pacs.004, and when is it used? A pacs.004 is a payment return. It is used when a payment was accepted and then has to come back, for example because the beneficiary account was closed, the account details were wrong but discovered after acceptance, or the beneficiary bank cannot apply the funds. The return references the original payment through the UETR, and the BA checks the return reason code before telling the customer what happened.

14. How would you test an ISO 20022 migration? Test three layers. Schema validation: does the message match the published definition. Business validation: does the receiving system accept the business data, including value dates, currencies and character sets. And end to end: does a payment travel from pain.001 through pacs.008 to pacs.002 with the right status at each step, including every rejection reason, truncation cases, and duplicate handling. A migration test plan that only covers the happy path will miss the failures that happen in production.

15. What is truncation, and why does it matter in MT to MX migration? Truncation is the loss of data when a rich ISO 20022 message has to be forwarded as an MT message to a bank that has not migrated. Structured addresses collapse into free text, remittance information is cut, and fields that exist in MX have no MT equivalent. The BA's mapping workbook must record, field by field, what survives, what is truncated, and what is lost, and the business must accept the loss in writing.

16. What is the role of the Business Application Header, head.001? The Business Application Header travels with an ISO 20022 message and carries routing and processing information: who sent it, who should receive it, what type it is, and when it was created. It is separate from the business content of the message, which means a system can route and log a message from the header alone, without reading the payment details.

17. A customer reports a payment as missing. What are your first three checks? First, the pacs.002 status: ACSC means settled, RJCT means it never left, ACCP means it is still in process. Second, the value date against the cut-off: a payment sent after cut-off settles the next business day. Third, the cover leg: in a serial or cover payment, the debit proves only that the first leg moved. Only after those three checks do you raise an investigation.

18. How do sanctions screening delays differ from rejections? A rejection ends the payment: the instruction is refused and a status is sent. A screening hold pauses it: the payment sits in a queue for manual review, no status is sent, and the customer hears nothing. A held payment looks identical to a lost payment from the outside, which is why operations must be able to see the screening queue and why the customer must be told the payment is under review.

19. What is a camt.056, and does it guarantee a payment stops? A camt.056 is a payment cancellation request. It asks the next bank to stop a payment that is already in flight. It does not guarantee the payment stops: if settlement already completed, the cancellation is rejected and the funds must be recovered through a return instead. The BA tracks whether the cancellation was accepted or rejected, and whether the funds were already settled.

20. How do you write a good requirement for an exception handling flow? Start from the failure, not from the happy path. Name the failure, name who sees it, name how long it may sit before it is escalated, and name what the customer is told at each stage. Then add the acceptance criteria: the payment must appear in the exception queue within a defined time, the queue must show the UETR and the reason, and no payment may sit in the queue without an owner. A requirement that only describes the success case is not a requirement for an exception flow.

Solved case study: the payment that left the account and never arrived

The situation. On Monday, a corporate customer in Mumbai instructs a EUR 50,000 payment to a supplier in Frankfurt. The payment is debited from the customer's account on Monday. On Wednesday the supplier says nothing arrived. The customer calls the bank and asks where the money is. The relationship manager assumes the payment failed and asks operations to send it again.

The investigation. Operations reads the pacs.002 for the payment's UETR. The latest status is ACCP, accepted, with no ACSC and no RJCT. The payment is not failed and not settled. It is in process. Next, operations checks the value date in the pain.001 against the cut-off. The instruction was entered at 16:40 on Monday, after the 15:30 cut-off for the Frankfurt corridor, so the value date is Tuesday, not Monday. The payment entered the Tuesday clearing cycle. Then operations checks the cover leg. The payment travels as a cover payment: the instruction went directly to the beneficiary bank, and the funds travel separately through a correspondent. The cover payment is waiting for liquidity at the correspondent, which is why the beneficiary bank has the instruction but no funds. The beneficiary is right to say nothing arrived, and the customer is right to say the money left the account. Both are true.

The resolution. The payment settles on Wednesday once the correspondent funds the cover leg. The beneficiary is credited on Wednesday. No resend is needed, and a resend would have paid the supplier twice. Operations tells the customer the payment will arrive on Wednesday and explains the value date and the cover leg in plain language.

The requirement that prevents the next one. The channel must warn the customer at entry time when the requested value date cannot be met, showing the expected value date before the customer confirms. Operations must be able to see both legs of a cover payment under one UETR, so a missing cover leg is visible without a manual investigation. And no payment may be resent while its latest pacs.002 status is ACCP or ACSP.

What to practise next. Work through the same case with different facts: a payment rejected with AC01, a payment held for sanctions review, and a payment settled but never credited. System design notes

Idempotency prevents double payments. Every payment instruction carries an idempotency key. The receiving system stores the key with the result of the first processing. A retry with the same key returns the original result instead of creating a second payment. A retry without a key is a new payment. This is the single most important design rule in payment APIs, and the one most often skipped.

Retries must not duplicate a debit. A caller that times out does not know whether the instruction was processed. The safe retry is: resend with the same idempotency key, and read the result. The unsafe retry is: send a new instruction. The BA writes the acceptance criteria for the retry path, not just for the first submission, and tests the timeout case explicitly.

Surviving a downstream failure. When a downstream system is down, the payment must not be lost and must not be duplicated. The safe design is a durable queue: the instruction is written to the queue first, then processed, and the queue is the record of what is outstanding. A payment that is in the queue and not yet processed is visible to operations. A payment that was never written to the queue is lost, and no investigation can find it.

Eventual consistency when money is involved. Two systems can hold different views of the same payment for a short time: the hub says sent, the core says not received. That is acceptable only if the difference is temporary and reconciled. The design must include a reconciliation job that flags any payment present in one system and missing in the other, with a named owner for the gap. A system that can disagree with itself and never notice is not eventually consistent. It is broken.

Cover vs customer payment coordination. A cover payment has two legs that must be tracked as one payment. The shared key is the UETR. If the two legs are tracked separately, operations will chase the wrong leg, and the customer will be told the payment failed when only the cover leg is waiting. The requirement is one view of the payment, with both legs visible, under one reference.

Queues, timeouts and reconciliation. A queue holds work that is waiting. A timeout says the work may or may not have happened. Reconciliation proves what actually happened. The three belong together: the queue makes outstanding work visible, the timeout rule decides when to retry, and reconciliation catches whatever the first two missed. A payment system without reconciliation is a system that discovers its errors from customer complaints.
