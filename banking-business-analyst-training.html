Banking Business Analyst Training

Banking BAs live between business and technology: they run workshops, write requirements engineers can build from, map messages field by field, define test cases, and shepherd changes through delivery. Payments is the domain that trains those skills fastest, because every requirement touches messages, rules, timing and failure handling. This track teaches the BA craft through payments work: practical workshops, real artefacts, product ownership, and a capstone project, plus the AI skills now expected of BAs and POs.

Running a Requirements Workshop

Most requirements are born in a workshop, not in a document. A workshop brings the business users, the operations team and the technologists into one room, and the BA runs it: setting the objective, asking the open questions, walking the process end to end, and naming the pain points. The preparation matters as much as the session itself: a clear objective, the right attendees, and a pre-read so nobody arrives cold.

Inside the workshop, the BA listens for the requirement behind the request. A user who says "the system is slow" may mean the payment takes too long to reach the beneficiary, or the repair queue takes too long to clear, or the report arrives too late for reconciliation. Each of those is a different requirement, with a different fix. The BA writes it down at the level of precision a developer needs: the condition, the rule, the error code, the exception path.

The workshop ends with outputs, not just notes: draft requirements, an open questions log, and decisions recorded with names and dates. The most common failures are a missing agenda, the wrong attendees in the room, and decisions that were agreed verbally and never written down. The BA Practical Workshop chapter teaches the full method: preparation, facilitation, documentation and follow-up, with a worked example from a real payments change.

From Requirements to Delivery

Requirements are not the end of the work; they are the start of delivery. Every requirement is traced to the test that proves it and to the defect that breaks it. When a test fails, the BA traces it back: which requirement, which mapping, which rule. When a defect is found, it is triaged by impact on money: a defect that pays the wrong amount or pays twice is critical, and a defect in a display label is not.

Delivery ends with go-live: checklists, rollback plans and hypercare, where the BA watches the first live payments and answers the first live questions. After go-live, the work continues as product ownership: a backlog to prioritise, stakeholders to align, and the next change to shape. The Payment Product Management and Product Strategy chapters cover that side of the role, and the AI chapters cover the newest expectation: using AI to draft, analyse and review BA work, with governance, risk and control, and a human checking every output before it is used.

What good requirements look like

A good requirement is testable, unambiguous and traceable. Testable means a tester can prove it: the requirement states the condition, the expected result and the error when it fails. Unambiguous means two readers reach the same meaning: no "fast", no "user-friendly", no "as soon as possible", only numbers, limits, times and codes. Traceable means every requirement links forward to the test that proves it and backward to the business need that asked for it, so when a test fails or a stakeholder asks why, the answer is one lookup away.

Acceptance criteria are written in the same spirit, often as Given, When and Then: given a payment above the daily limit, when it is submitted, then it is rejected with error code LIMIT-01 and routed to the exceptions queue. The business rules catalogue holds the rest: limits, cut-off times, routing rules, charges, FX and validation, each with its owner and its effective date. The BA Practical Workshop and BA Practice Depth chapters teach this craft with worked examples, and the capstone project is where it is proved: a full BA pack on a realistic brief, written to the standard a real bank would accept.
