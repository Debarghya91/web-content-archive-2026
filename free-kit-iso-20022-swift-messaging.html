Preview: message reference

pain.001, Customer Credit Transfer Initiation. The customer instructs its bank to move money. Sent by a corporate or retail customer system to its bank. The BA checks the debtor, the creditor, the instructed amount, the value date, the remittance information, and the charge bearer. An error here is inherited by every message that follows. If the pain.001 is wrong, the pacs.008 will be wrong in the same way.

pacs.008, FI to FI Customer Credit Transfer. The bank-to-bank message that carries a customer payment. Sent by the debtor bank to the creditor bank, directly or through correspondents. The BA reads it to follow the payment, check the charges and the remittance data, and confirm which leg is stuck. The UETR in the pacs.008 is the key that links every later message about the same payment.

pacs.009, FI to FI Financial Institution Credit Transfer. A bank moving its own money, for example to settle with a correspondent or to fund a nostro account. No customer behind it, no customer charge option, no remittance information for a beneficiary. A BA who mistakes a pacs.009 for a pacs.008 will look for customer data that does not exist, and will misread the settlement.

pacs.002, FI to FI Payment Status Report. The reply that says what happened to a payment. Sent by the receiving bank back along the chain. The BA reads the pacs.002 before doing anything else in an investigation, because the status and the reason code together say whether the payment is in process, settled, or rejected, and what to do next.

camt.056, FI to FI Payment Cancellation Request. A request to stop a payment that is already in flight. It does not guarantee the payment stops. If settlement already completed, the cancellation is rejected and the funds must be recovered through a return. The BA tracks whether the cancellation was accepted or rejected, and whether the funds were already settled.

pacs.004, Payment Return. A payment that was accepted and then returned, for example because the beneficiary account was closed after acceptance. The BA checks the return reason code and the original UETR, then tells the customer the funds are coming back, not that the payment failed.

Preview: status code guide

ACCP, Accepted. The payment passed validation and was accepted for processing. It does not mean the beneficiary was paid. It does not mean settlement happened. A payment can sit at ACCP for hours if the next step is waiting for liquidity or a clearing cycle.

ACSP, Accepted, Settlement in Process. The payment was accepted and settlement has started. The money has not necessarily arrived. A payment at ACSP is in flight, and the correct action is to wait and watch, not to resend.

ACSC, Accepted, Settlement Completed. Settlement is complete between the banks. This is the only status that means the money moved. It still does not prove the beneficiary account was credited, because the account posting is a separate step that can fail on its own. Confirm the credit with the camt.054 notification or the account posting, not with the pacs.002 alone.

RJCT, Rejected. The payment was rejected, with a reason code that says why. Read the reason code, not just the status. RJCT with AC01 (incorrect account number) means fix the account and resubmit. RJCT with AM05 (duplication) means the payment was already processed and the second submission must be stopped. RJCT with RC01 (incorrect bank identifier code) means the routing is wrong. The same status with a different reason code means a different fix.

PDNG, Pending. The payment is waiting for something: a decision, a balance, a review. PDNG is not an error. It is a state. The BA finds out what the payment is waiting for, and who owns the wait, before telling the customer anything.

The rule. Never act on a status code alone. Read the status, the reason code, and the original references together. A status without a reason code is a headline without a story.

Preview: identifier guide

UETR, Unique End to End Transaction Reference. A UUID generated by the first bank in the payment chain and carried unchanged by every bank that handles the payment. It links the pacs.008, every pacs.002 status report, any pacs.004 return, and any camt.056 cancellation. Use the UETR when you need to track a payment across banks, when you raise an investigation, and when you reconcile a payment across systems. If a bank drops or changes the UETR, the payment becomes untrackable.

TxId, Transaction Identification. The identification assigned by the sending bank to one specific message, for example one pacs.008. It is unique to that message, not to the payment. Two messages about the same payment have different TxIds but the same UETR. Use the TxId when you need to identify one message, for example when a message was sent twice.

EndToEndId, End to End Identification. The identification assigned by the originating customer or system, carried through the chain. It is the customer's reference for the payment, and it is how the customer recognises the payment on their statement. Use the EndToEndId when you talk to the customer, and the UETR when you talk to another bank. The two identify the same payment from different sides.

Which one to use when. Talking to the customer: EndToEndId. Tracking across banks: UETR. Identifying one message: TxId. Reconciling across your own systems: UETR first, then EndToEndId as a second key, because a return or a status report may carry a different TxId but must carry the same UETR.

MT vs MX mapping pitfalls

Truncation is the trap most projects underestimate. Under coexistence, a bank receiving a rich ISO 20022 message may have to forward it as an MT message to a bank that has not migrated. Fields get shortened, structured addresses collapse into free text, and remittance information is cut. The mapping workbook must record, field by field, what survives, what is truncated, and what is lost, and the business must accept the loss in writing.

Structured addresses collapse. A pacs.008 carries the creditor address as separate elements: street, town, country, postcode. An MT103 carries the beneficiary address as four lines of free text. When the pacs.008 is mapped to an MT103, the structured address is flattened into those four lines, and anything that does not fit is dropped. The dropped part is usually the part sanctions screening needs, which is why a payment that passed screening as an MX message can be held as an MT message.

Remittance information is cut. A pacs.008 can carry structured remittance information the beneficiary can reconcile automatically. An MT103 carries remittance information in field 70, limited to four lines of 35 characters. Anything beyond that is lost, and the beneficiary cannot reconcile the payment automatically. The BA must record the remittance loss in the mapping workbook and warn the business that straight-through reconciliation will fail for those payments.

Fields with no equivalent. Some MX elements have no MT field at all. The purpose code, the structured remittance data, and the full party details are examples. When the mapping has no target field, the data is dropped, and the receiving bank never sees it. The BA must list every dropped field, say what it was used for, and get the business to accept the loss, rather than discovering it from a customer complaint.

Character set restrictions. Under CBPR+, messages are restricted to the SWIFT X character set. A name or address containing characters outside that set must be converted or the message is rejected. The conversion is lossy: a character that cannot be represented is replaced or dropped, and the name that arrives is not the name that was sent. The BA tests character set handling explicitly, with real customer names, not with test data that only uses plain letters.

Charge options do not map cleanly. An MT103 carries the charge option in field 71A: OUR, SHA or BEN. A pacs.008 carries the charge bearer as a structured element, but the scheme rules about who pays what can differ between the two formats. A payment that is OUR in MT can become SHA in MX if the mapping is wrong, and the beneficiary receives less than expected. The BA checks the charge mapping in both directions, not just one.

MT202 COV and the cover leg. The MT202 COV carries cover payment data for a customer payment that travelled separately. In MX, the cover leg is a pacs.009. The mapping must preserve the link between the customer payment and the cover payment, or the two legs become untraceable as one payment. The link is the UETR, and it must survive the mapping in both directions.

Schema-valid vs business-invalid examples

Example 1: A pacs.008 that passes the schema and fails the value date. The message is well formed: every mandatory element is present, every value matches its data type, the XML validates against the published definition. It is then rejected because the value date is a bank holiday in the beneficiary country. Schema validation checks the shape of the message. Business validation checks whether the payment can actually happen on that date. The BA tests both layers, and knows that passing the first layer proves nothing about the second.

Example 2: A pacs.008 with a valid IBAN and a wrong beneficiary. The IBAN passes its check digit, so schema validation and format validation both pass. The beneficiary name does not match the account holder, and the receiving bank rejects the payment. The message was valid. The business data was wrong. The BA checks the business data against the customer instruction, not just against the schema.

Example 3: A pacs.008 with a currency the corridor does not support. The message is well formed and the currency code is a valid ISO currency. The corridor between the two banks does not support that currency for this payment type, so the receiving bank rejects it. The schema cannot know which currencies a corridor supports. The BA checks the corridor rules in the scheme documentation, not in the message definition.

Example 4: A pacs.008 with an amount outside the scheme limit. The amount is a valid number, the currency is supported, the value date is a business day. The amount exceeds the scheme limit for that payment type, so the payment is rejected. The schema allows any amount. The scheme does not. The BA tests amounts at the boundary, not just in the middle of the range.

Example 5: A pacs.002 with a reason code that contradicts the status. A status report says ACCP, accepted, but carries a reason code that normally accompanies a rejection. The receiving bank's system generated the status and the reason code from different steps, and they disagree. The BA reads the status and the reason code together and treats a contradiction as a defect in the reporting, not as a fact about the payment. The payment is investigated from the UETR, not from the contradictory report.

The lesson. A message can be perfectly well formed and still wrong. The BA's job is to read the business data, not just to validate the format. Test the message against the scheme rules, the corridor rules, the cut-off table, and the customer instruction, in that order.

Worked examples: reading a real message

Worked example 1: Follow a pacs.008 from initiation to settlement. Start with the UETR. Find the pain.001 that started the payment and confirm the instructed amount, the value date and the beneficiary. Read the pacs.008 and confirm the same amount, the same value date, the same beneficiary, and the charge option. Read each pacs.002 in order: ACCP, then ACSP, then ACSC. If the chain stops at ACCP, the payment is waiting: check the queue, the sanctions hold, and the liquidity at the next bank. If the chain shows RJCT, read the reason code and fix that, not something else. If the chain shows ACSC, confirm the credit with the camt.054, because settlement between banks and credit to the beneficiary account are two different steps.

Worked example 2: Prove a duplicate from the messages. Two credits appear on the beneficiary account for the same amount on the same day. Pull both pacs.008 messages. If the UETR is the same on both, it is one payment processed twice: a duplicate caused by a retry after a timeout. If the UETR is different, they are two separate payments, and the second one may be legitimate. The UETR is the proof. Without it, the two credits are indistinguishable, and operations will argue about whether the customer was paid twice or paid for two different things.

Worked example 3: Find the missing cover leg. The customer instruction arrived at the beneficiary bank, but the funds did not. Pull the MT103 or pacs.008 for the customer leg, and confirm it arrived. Then pull the MT202 COV or pacs.009 for the cover leg, using the same UETR. If the cover leg is missing, the funds never left the correspondent. If the cover leg is present but rejected, read its reason code. The customer leg and the cover leg must be read together, under one UETR, or the investigation will chase the wrong leg.

Worked example 4: Read a rejection and decide the fix. A pacs.002 reports RJCT with reason code RC01, incorrect bank identifier code. The fix is in the routing: the creditor agent BIC in the pacs.008 is wrong, and the payment must be corrected and resubmitted. A different reason code would mean a different fix: AC01 means the account is wrong, AM05 means the payment is a duplicate, BE04 means the creditor account is closed. The reason code decides the fix. A team that resubmits without reading the reason code will submit the same error again.
