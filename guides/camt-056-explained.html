camt.056 Explained

By Debarghya Mondal · Last reviewed 10 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • The camt.056 is the FI to FI Payment Cancellation Request.
  • The camt.056 is sent between banks, not by customers.
  • A cancellation request is not the same as a return.

Reading time: about 10 min

What it is

The camt.056 is the FI to FI Payment Cancellation Request. It is an ISO 20022 message that one financial institution sends to another to ask for a payment instruction to be cancelled. The bank that sent the payment uses it when it discovers, after sending the instruction, that the payment should not have been sent, or should not go ahead as instructed.

The camt.056 is sent between banks, not by customers. A customer who wants to cancel a payment tells their own bank. The bank then decides whether a cancellation is possible and, if so, sends the camt.056 to the next bank in the chain. The customer never sees the camt.056 itself.

A cancellation request is not the same as a return. A return, sent as a pacs.004, is used when the beneficiary bank cannot credit the payment and sends the money back on its own initiative. A cancellation, sent as a camt.056, is a request from the sending bank to stop a payment that is already on its way. The difference matters because a cancellation can be refused, while a return is a statement of what has already happened.

The camt.056 is answered by a camt.029, the Resolution of Investigation message, which tells the requesting bank whether the cancellation was accepted or rejected. The two messages always work as a pair: the camt.056 asks, and the camt.029 answers.

Why it matters

Payments go wrong. A customer types the wrong account number. An operator enters the wrong amount. A payment is sent twice. A fraud is discovered after the payment has left the bank. In each case, the sending bank has a short window in which it can stop the payment before the money reaches the beneficiary. The camt.056 is the tool for that window.

The window is short because cross-border payments move quickly. A payment instructed in the morning can be credited to the beneficiary the same day, and once it is credited, the money belongs to the beneficiary. The sending bank cannot simply take it back. It must ask, and the beneficiary bank must ask the beneficiary. The camt.056 is how that request travels, and how quickly it is sent decides whether the money can still be recovered.

For operations teams, the camt.056 is a daily tool. Every bank that sends cross-border payments handles cancellation requests, both sending them and receiving them. A slow or badly formed cancellation request means the money is lost. A well-run cancellation process means most errors are caught before settlement.

For a business analyst, the camt.056 appears in exception handling requirements. A payment hub needs a cancellation workflow: who can request a cancellation, how the request is built, which bank it goes to, how the camt.029 response is processed, and what happens when the cancellation is rejected. The workflow has to be fast, because the window is short, and it has to be auditable, because a cancellation moves money.

How it works in a bank

The error is found. A customer calls to say the payment went to the wrong account. An operations analyst spots a duplicate. A fraud team flags a payment that has already been sent. Whoever finds the error raises a cancellation request in the payment hub, giving the original payment reference, the UETR, the amount, and the reason for the cancellation.

The bank checks whether cancellation is possible. The payment hub checks the status of the original payment. If the payment has not yet been sent to the next bank, it can simply be stopped internally and no camt.056 is needed. If the payment has been sent but not yet settled, a camt.056 can stop it. If the payment has already been settled and credited, the camt.056 becomes a recall request, which is a different situation with a lower chance of success.

The camt.056 is built and sent. The payment hub builds the camt.056 with the details of the original payment: the original message identification, the UETR, the amount, the currency, the debtor and creditor, and the reason for the cancellation. The reason uses a standard code, such as a duplicate payment, a wrong beneficiary, or a fraud suspicion. The camt.056 is sent to the next bank in the chain, the bank that received the original payment instruction.

The next bank acts or forwards. The bank that receives the camt.056 checks whether it still holds the payment. If it does, and the payment has not yet been forwarded or settled, it stops the payment and sends a camt.029 accepting the cancellation. If it has already forwarded the payment, it forwards the camt.056 to the next bank in the chain. The request travels down the chain until it reaches the bank that currently holds the payment.

The camt.029 answers. The bank that can act on the cancellation sends a camt.029 back up the chain. The camt.029 carries a status: RJCR (the cancellation request is rejected), ACSP (the cancellation request is accepted and the cancellation is pending), or ACCP (the cancellation request is accepted and the cancellation is completed). Each bank in the chain forwards the camt.029 back to the requesting bank, so the requesting bank learns the outcome.

The funds are returned. If the cancellation is accepted, the bank that holds the payment returns the funds to the requesting bank, usually as a pacs.004 return, and the requesting bank credits the money back to the customer. If the cancellation is rejected, the funds stay where they are, and the requesting bank must tell the customer that the payment cannot be stopped.

Worked example

A UK company, Harrow Engineering Ltd, instructs its bank to pay a supplier in Germany, Rheinmetall Components GmbH, EUR 25,000 for invoice INV-2026-2044. The payment is instructed at 10:00 on a Tuesday. At 10:20, Harrow's finance team realises the payment should have gone to a different supplier, and calls the bank to stop it.

The bank checks the payment status. The pacs.008 was sent to the correspondent bank at 10:05, but the correspondent bank has not yet settled it. The payment can still be cancelled. The bank builds a camt.056 with the original pacs.008 message identification, the UETR, the amount EUR 25,000, and the cancellation reason code for a wrong beneficiary. It sends the camt.056 to the correspondent bank at 10:25.

The correspondent bank receives the camt.056, checks its queue, and finds the payment has not yet been forwarded to the beneficiary bank. It stops the payment and sends a camt.029 with status ACCP, accepted and completed, back to Harrow's bank. The correspondent bank returns the EUR 25,000 to Harrow's bank, and Harrow's bank credits EUR 25,000 back to Harrow Engineering's account. The whole cancellation took less than an hour, and the money was recovered in full, because the request was sent before the payment settled.

If Harrow had called at 15:00 instead of 10:20, the outcome would have been different. By then the payment would have been settled and credited to the supplier's bank, and the camt.056 would have been a recall request. The supplier's bank would have had to ask Rheinmetall Components for consent to return the money, and Rheinmetall could have refused. The speed of the cancellation request is what decides whether the money comes back.

Technical detail

The camt.056 structure. The camt.056 carries an assignment (who is asking whom), the details of the original payment instruction (the original message identification, the original instruction identification, and the UETR), and the cancellation reason. The reason uses a standard code from the ISO 20022 external code list, such as DUPL for a duplicate payment, or a code for a wrong beneficiary or a fraud suspicion. The message also carries the amount and currency of the original payment, so the receiving bank can match it to the payment it holds.

Cancellation before settlement. Before settlement, the payment is still an instruction travelling between banks. The bank that holds it can stop it, and the funds have not yet moved. A cancellation at this stage is usually accepted, and the funds are returned in full. The main risk is timing: the request must reach the holding bank before that bank forwards or settles the payment. This is why cancellation requests are treated as urgent in operations.

Recall after settlement. After settlement, the payment has been credited to the beneficiary account, and the money belongs to the beneficiary. A camt.056 sent at this stage is a recall request. The beneficiary bank cannot take the money from the beneficiary's account without the beneficiary's consent, except in limited circumstances such as a proven fraud or a legal order. The beneficiary bank asks the beneficiary, and the beneficiary can refuse. A recall is therefore uncertain, and the requesting bank must tell the customer that the money may not come back.

camt.056 and camt.055. The camt.056 is the FI to FI cancellation request, sent between banks. The camt.055 is the Customer Payment Cancellation Request, which is the message a bank uses when the cancellation is initiated on the customer side. In practice, the customer's request to their own bank is handled internally, and the camt.056 is the message that travels between banks. Both are answered by a camt.029. A business analyst specifying a cancellation workflow needs to know which message is used at each step, because the two messages carry different information and go to different parties.

The camt.029 response codes. The camt.029 that answers a camt.056 carries one of three statuses. RJCR means the cancellation request is rejected, and the message carries a reason code explaining why. ACSP means the cancellation request is accepted and the cancellation is pending, usually because the holding bank needs to complete a check before it can confirm. ACCP means the cancellation request is accepted and the cancellation is completed, and the funds are being returned. A bank that receives an ACSP should expect a second camt.029 with ACCP or RJCR when the cancellation is finalised.

Common mistakes

Waiting too long to send the camt.056. The chance of a successful cancellation falls sharply once the payment settles. A cancellation request sent within minutes has a good chance of success. One sent hours later, after settlement, is a recall and may fail. Operations teams should treat a cancellation request as urgent and send the camt.056 as soon as the error is confirmed, not at the end of the day.

Sending a camt.056 for a payment that has not left the bank. If the payment is still inside the sending bank, it can be stopped internally. Sending a camt.056 to the next bank for a payment that bank never received creates confusion and a pointless investigation. The payment hub should check the payment status first and only send the camt.056 when the payment has actually left the bank.

Treating a recall as a cancellation. A cancellation stops a payment before settlement. A recall asks for money back after settlement, and it needs the beneficiary's consent. Telling a customer that a recall is a cancellation sets a false expectation. The customer should be told clearly whether the payment is being cancelled or recalled, and what the chances of recovery are in each case.

Not following up on an ACSP. An ACSP status means the cancellation is accepted but still pending. It is not a completed cancellation. A bank that files an ACSP and takes no further action may never learn that the cancellation later failed. The payment hub should track ACSP responses and chase the holding bank if a final ACCP or RJCR does not arrive within the agreed timeframe.

Sending a duplicate camt.056. If a camt.056 has already been sent for a payment, sending a second one creates a duplicate investigation and can confuse the holding bank. The payment hub should check whether a cancellation is already in progress before sending another request, and should use the camt.029 response to decide the next step, not send the same request again.
