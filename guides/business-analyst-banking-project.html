What a Business Analyst Does on a Banking Project

By Payments Training Hub · Last reviewed 18 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • A banking BA translates between business and technology: workshops in, requirements out, with the message detail engineers need and the business outcomes owners sign.
  • The work runs discovery, requirements, design support, testing, and go-live, and the BA stays involved through all of it.
  • The artefacts are BRDs, process flows, mapping documents, business rules, test cases and decision logs, and traceability ties them together.
  • Most project failures are requirements failures: missing exception paths, untested acceptance criteria, and decisions nobody recorded.

Reading time: about 6 min

What it is

A business analyst on a banking project is the person who turns a business need into a buildable, testable specification, and then stays with it through design, testing and go-live. The job is part investigator, part writer, part facilitator and part tester. In payments, the BA also needs domain depth: messages, schemes, cut-offs, charges, exceptions and reconciliation, because a requirement that ignores the domain will be wrong in ways that only surface in production.

The BA is not a note-taker and not a project manager. The BA owns the requirements: their completeness, their correctness, and their traceability to design and test. When a requirement is disputed, the BA resolves it with evidence. When a decision is made, the BA records it. When scope moves, the BA costs it.

Why it matters

Banking projects fail on requirements more than on code. A missed exception path becomes a production incident. An untested acceptance criterion becomes a UAT dispute. A decision made in a corridor and never recorded becomes a rebuild. The BA exists to prevent these, by making the implicit explicit before anyone builds.

The BA is also the project's memory. Programmes run for months or years, people rotate, vendors change, and scheme deadlines move. The requirements, the decision log and the traceability matrix are what keep the project coherent across all of that. A good BA leaves a project auditable: anyone can see what was decided, why, and what it affected.

How it works in a bank

Discovery. The BA runs workshops with operations, product, compliance, technology and sometimes the scheme or vendor. The output is an as-is picture: current flows, current messages, current pain, and the constraints nobody wrote down. Good discovery asks operations staff how the work actually happens, not how the procedure manual says it happens.

Requirements. The BA writes the BRD, user stories and acceptance criteria, the message catalogue and field mappings, and the business rules. Each requirement gets an ID. Each rule gets a condition and an outcome. Exception paths are specified with the same care as happy paths.

Design support. During design, the BA answers engineering questions, refines mappings, and challenges designs that do not meet the requirements. The BA attends design reviews as the voice of the requirement, not as an observer.

Testing. The BA writes or reviews test cases, supports system testing and UAT, triages defects (requirement gap, design error, or code defect), and manages the change requests that testing always produces. Certification with a scheme or a counterparty is often the BA's coordination work too.

Go-live and hypercare. The BA supports the release, watches the first live payments, handles the queries operations raise, and records the lessons. Hypercare findings feed back into requirements for the next release.

Stakeholders. The BA works with business owners who sign off, operations teams who live with the result, engineers who build it, testers who verify it, compliance teams who constrain it, and vendors and schemes who set external deadlines. Managing these relationships, and their conflicting priorities, is the job as much as writing documents.

Worked example

One week on a payments migration project, as an illustration:

Monday. A workshop on return handling: the BA walks operations through the to-be flow for pacs.004 returns, and surfaces a disagreement about who owns returns after 30 days. The decision is logged, with an owner and a date.

Tuesday. A mapping session with engineering: three MT103 fields have no clean MX equivalent, and the BA records the truncation decisions, with business sign-off, in the mapping document.

Wednesday. Test-case review: the BA finds four test cases that only cover the happy path, and adds exception cases for rejects, duplicates and cut-off breaches.

Thursday. Defect triage: of twelve defects, the BA classifies five as requirement gaps, writes the change requests, and gets the business owner to prioritise them before the next build.

Friday. A stakeholder demo: the BA shows the to-be flow with real message examples, takes three questions back as actions, and updates the decision log. The week ends with the requirements, the matrix and the log all current.

Technical detail

Traceability. Requirement to design to test case, in a matrix that is maintained, not written once and forgotten. When a requirement changes, the matrix shows the design and test impact immediately.

Decision logs. Date, decision, rationale, owner, and alternatives considered. A decision log ends repeat arguments and survives staff rotation.

Writing for two audiences. The same requirement must satisfy a business owner signing it off and an engineer building from it. Business outcomes up front, message and field detail in the body, acceptance criteria at the end.

Change control. Changes after sign-off get an impact note (scope, cost, schedule, test) and an approval. Undocumented change is the main cause of late-project chaos.

Common mistakes

Writing requirements without talking to operations. The procedure manual and the real process differ. The BA who only reads documents writes fiction.

Skipping exception paths. Happy-path requirements produce happy-path builds, and production is mostly exceptions.

Accepting "the vendor handles it". Vendor behaviour is a requirement like any other: specify it, test it, and record the gaps.

No decision log. Decisions re-argued every month burn the programme's time and credibility.

Gold-plating. Requirements beyond the business need add cost, risk and delay. If nobody can name the benefit, it is not a requirement.

Avoiding difficult stakeholders. The stakeholder who disagrees in the corridor and stays silent in the workshop will block sign-off later. Surface disagreement early, in the room.
