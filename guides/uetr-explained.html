UETR Explained

By Payments Training Hub · Last reviewed 09 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • The UETR (Unique End-to-end Transaction Reference) is a 36-character identifier that stays with a cross-border payment for its whole journey, from the ordering bank to the beneficiary bank.
  • The UETR is a UUID version 4.
  • The UETR is not a payment amount, an account number, or a customer reference.

Reading time: about 10 min

What it is

The UETR (Unique End-to-end Transaction Reference) is a 36-character identifier that stays with a cross-border payment for its whole journey, from the ordering bank to the beneficiary bank. It is assigned once, by the bank that first instructs the payment, and it never changes. Every bank in the chain carries the same UETR in its messages about that payment, so the whole journey can be stitched together from the separate messages each bank sends.

The UETR is a UUID version 4. It has five groups of hexadecimal characters separated by hyphens, in the pattern 8-4-4-4-12. An example looks like this: `6f8e2b1a-3c4d-4e5f-9a0b-1c2d3e4f5a6b`. The first group has 8 characters, then three groups of 4, then a final group of 12. The 13th character is always `4`, which marks it as a version 4 UUID, and the first character of the fourth group is `8`, `9`, `a`, or `b`, which marks the UUID variant. These fixed positions are how a system can tell a well-formed UETR from a random string.

The UETR is not a payment amount, an account number, or a customer reference. It carries no information about the payment itself. It is a random identifier whose only job is to be unique. Two payments never share a UETR, even if they are for the same amount, between the same two accounts, on the same day.

Why it matters

Before the UETR, a cross-border payment had no single identifier that every bank in the chain used. Each bank gave the payment its own reference: the ordering bank had one, the correspondent bank had another, and the beneficiary bank had a third. When a customer asked where their payment was, the ordering bank had to ask each bank in the chain, using that bank's own reference, and piece the answers together by phone and email. A payment investigation could take days.

The UETR fixed that by giving the payment one identity that every bank recognises. A bank that receives a status report, a return, or a cancellation request can match it to the original payment immediately, using the UETR, without searching by amount, date, or beneficiary name. That matching is what makes end-to-end tracking possible, and it is the foundation of SWIFT gpi.

For operations teams, the UETR is the fastest way to find a payment. When a customer calls about a payment, the first question is the UETR. With it, the operations analyst can pull the payment's full history in seconds: when it was sent, which banks handled it, what statuses were received, and whether it has been credited. Without it, the analyst is searching by amount and beneficiary name, which is slow and can match the wrong payment.

For a business analyst, the UETR is a design decision in almost every cross-border payments project. Any system that sends, receives, tracks, or investigates a payment needs to store the UETR, carry it through every message, and use it as the key for searching and reconciliation. A system that drops the UETR breaks tracking for every payment it touches.

How it works in a bank

The UETR is assigned at instruction. When a customer instructs a cross-border payment, the payment hub builds the pacs.008 and assigns a UETR. The hub generates a fresh UUID version 4 for each payment. It stores the UETR against the payment record in the core system, alongside the bank's own payment reference, the customer reference, the amount, and the value date. The UETR is assigned once and never reused, even if the payment is later cancelled and re-sent. A re-sent payment gets a new UETR.

The UETR is carried in the payment message. The pacs.008 carries the UETR in its own element. In the MT world, the MT103 and the MT202 carry the UETR in field 121. Every bank that forwards the payment copies the UETR into the next message unchanged. The UETR is not edited, shortened, or replaced at any point in the chain.

The UETR is carried in the status reports. Each bank in the chain sends pacs.002 status reports as the payment progresses: accepted, settlement in progress, settlement completed, credited, or rejected. Each pacs.002 carries the same UETR, so the tracker can link the status to the original payment. This is how the gpi tracker builds the journey view from messages sent by different banks on different days.

The UETR is carried in exceptions. If the payment is returned, the pacs.004 return carries the UETR. If the payment is cancelled, the camt.056 cancellation request carries the UETR, and the camt.029 response carries it too. If a case is raised through gpi case resolution, the case is linked to the UETR. Every message about the payment, in every direction, uses the same UETR.

The UETR is used for searching. The payment hub, the operations dashboard, and the customer-facing tracker all index payments by UETR. When a customer or a correspondent bank asks about a payment, the analyst searches by UETR and gets the full history. The UETR is also used in reconciliation: a status report with a UETR that matches no payment in the system is flagged immediately, because it means a message has arrived for a payment the bank does not know about.

The UETR survives the cover method. In a cover payment, the pacs.008 goes directly to the beneficiary bank while a separate pacs.009 moves the funds through the correspondent chain. Both messages carry the same UETR, so the instruction and the cover can be matched to each other. Without a shared UETR, the beneficiary bank could not link the cover funds to the customer instruction.

Worked example

A German exporter, Rheinmetall Components GmbH, pays a supplier in India, Apex Auto Parts Pvt Ltd, EUR 75,000 for a shipment of components. Rheinmetall instructs the payment through its bank in Frankfurt at 11:00 on a Wednesday.

The Frankfurt bank builds a pacs.008 and assigns the UETR `6f8e2b1a-3c4d-4e5f-9a0b-1c2d3e4f5a6b`. It stores the UETR against the payment record, together with its own payment reference, the customer reference INV-2026-1187, the amount EUR 75,000, and the value date Thursday. It sends the pacs.008 to its correspondent bank.

The correspondent bank receives the pacs.008, reads the UETR, and sends a pacs.002 with status ACSP and the same UETR. It forwards the payment to the beneficiary bank in Mumbai and sends a pacs.002 with status ACSC, again with the same UETR. The beneficiary bank receives the payment, credits EUR 75,000 (converted to INR) to Apex Auto Parts, and sends a pacs.002 with status ACCC and the same UETR.

On Friday, Rheinmetall's finance team calls the Frankfurt bank to ask whether the supplier has been paid. The analyst searches by the UETR and sees the full journey in seconds: sent Wednesday 11:00, accepted by the correspondent bank Wednesday afternoon, settled Thursday, credited to the beneficiary Friday morning. The analyst reads the ACCC status and confirms the payment arrived. Without the UETR, the same enquiry would have needed the Frankfurt bank to contact the correspondent bank, which would have had to contact the Mumbai bank, and the answer would have taken days.

Technical detail

The UUID version 4 format. A UETR is a UUID version 4, which is a 128-bit random number written as 32 hexadecimal characters in five groups. The pattern is 8-4-4-4-12, with hyphens between the groups, making 36 characters in total. The 13th character is always `4`. The first character of the fourth group is `8`, `9`, `a`, or `b`. A system that validates a UETR should check the length, the hyphen positions, the version digit, and the variant digit. A string that fails any of those checks is not a valid UETR and should be rejected or flagged for repair.

Where the UETR is carried. In ISO 20022, the UETR is carried in the pacs.008, the pacs.002, the pacs.004, the pacs.009, the camt.056, and the camt.029. In the MT messages, it is carried in field 121 of the MT103, the MT202, and the MT202 COV. In the MT103, field 121 sits in the user header block, block 3, not in the main text of the message. A bank that builds MT103 messages must put the UETR in the header, or the tracking chain breaks.

The UETR and the EndToEndId are different. The pacs.008 carries two references that are often confused. The EndToEndId is the reference the ordering customer gives the payment, such as an invoice number. It is passed through the chain unchanged, but it is not unique: two customers can use the same invoice number, and the same customer can reuse a reference. The UETR is assigned by the bank, is globally unique, and is never reused. The EndToEndId is for the customer's own reconciliation. The UETR is for tracking the payment across banks. A system must store both and must never use one where the other is needed.

The UETR and duplicate detection. Because a UETR is unique to one payment, a bank can use it to detect duplicates. If the bank receives two pacs.008 messages with the same UETR, the second one is a duplicate of the first and must not be processed again. This check is one of the simplest and most reliable duplicate controls in cross-border payments, and it depends entirely on every bank assigning a fresh UETR to each new payment and never reusing one.

What happens if the UETR is dropped or changed. If a bank in the chain fails to copy the UETR into the next message, the tracking chain breaks at that bank. Status reports from banks after the break cannot be linked to the original payment, and the tracker shows a gap. If a bank changes the UETR, the same break happens, and worse, the new UETR may collide with another payment. The payment hub must validate that the UETR is present in every outgoing message and must never generate a new UETR for a payment that already has one.

Common mistakes

Confusing the UETR with the EndToEndId. The EndToEndId is the customer's reference, such as an invoice number. The UETR is the bank-assigned tracking identifier. Searching the tracker by invoice number will not find the payment, and giving a customer the EndToEndId when they ask for the UETR leaves them unable to track the payment with another bank. Store both, label them clearly, and use each for its own purpose.

Reusing a UETR. A UETR belongs to one payment only. If a payment is cancelled and re-sent, the new payment needs a new UETR. Reusing the old one makes the tracker show two journeys under one identifier, confuses the status reports, and breaks duplicate detection. The payment hub should generate a fresh UUID for every new instruction, with no exceptions.

Dropping the UETR in the cover payment. In a cover payment, the pacs.008 and the pacs.009 must carry the same UETR. If the cover message is built without the UETR, the beneficiary bank cannot link the funds to the instruction, and the payment will be held for investigation. The payment hub must copy the UETR from the pacs.008 into the pacs.009 automatically, not leave it to manual entry.

Searching by amount instead of by UETR. When a customer asks about a payment, the fastest and most reliable search is by UETR. Searching by amount and beneficiary name is slow and can return several payments that look the same. Ask the customer for the UETR first, and show it on every payment confirmation the bank sends to the customer, so they have it when they call.

Assuming a UETR proves the payment was credited. A UETR proves the payment exists and can be tracked. It does not prove the beneficiary received the money. Only the ACCC status on the pacs.002 confirms the credit. A payment with a UETR can still be pending, rejected, or returned. Check the status, not just the reference.
