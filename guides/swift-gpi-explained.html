SWIFT gpi Explained

By Payments Training Hub · Last reviewed 09 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • SWIFT gpi (Global Payments Innovation) is a service that runs on top of the SWIFT network and gives banks and their customers end-to-end visibility of a cross-border payment.
  • gpi is not a new payment system and it is not a new message type.
  • gpi has four service levels, and a bank can adopt them in stages:

Reading time: about 11 min

What it is

SWIFT gpi (Global Payments Innovation) is a service that runs on top of the SWIFT network and gives banks and their customers end-to-end visibility of a cross-border payment. Before gpi, a cross-border payment disappeared into a chain of correspondent banks. The ordering bank could not see where the payment was, how long it would take, or what charges had been deducted. The customer asking "where is my payment" got no useful answer. gpi changed that by adding tracking, speed commitments, and fee transparency to the same SWIFT messages banks already used.

gpi is not a new payment system and it is not a new message type. It is a set of rules and services that gpi member banks agree to follow. The core of it is the UETR, the Unique End-to-end Transaction Reference, a 36-character identifier that stays with a payment for its whole journey. Every bank in the chain includes the UETR in its status reports, so the payment can be followed from the ordering bank to the beneficiary bank.

gpi has four service levels, and a bank can adopt them in stages:

  • gpi Tracker. The tracker is the shared service that shows the live status of a payment. It records every status update sent by the banks in the chain and presents the journey in one view: when the payment was sent, which banks handled it, what charges were deducted, and whether it has been credited to the beneficiary.
  • gpi Observer. The observer is a tool that lets a bank watch the quality of the payments it sends and receives, even for payments that pass through banks that are not gpi members. It measures whether banks in the chain are meeting gpi service levels, such as confirming credit promptly, and it highlights where a payment slowed down.
  • gpi payment confirmations. Under gpi, the bank that credits the beneficiary account must confirm that it has done so. The confirmation is sent as a pacs.002 status report with the status ACCC, which means the payment has been accepted and credited. This confirmation is what turns "the payment was sent" into "the payment arrived".
  • gpi case resolution. When something goes wrong, gpi provides a standard way to raise and resolve a case. The main cases are stop and recall requests, investigations into a payment that has not arrived, and requests for information. Cases are raised against the UETR and routed to the bank that holds the payment, which shortens the back and forth that used to happen by email and phone.

A gpi payment is still a SWIFT payment. It travels over the same network, uses the same pacs.008 instruction, and settles through the same nostro accounts. What gpi adds is the tracking, the confirmations, and the rules that make the journey visible and predictable.

Why it matters

Cross-border payments are the part of banking that customers complain about most. A corporate treasurer paying a supplier needs to know the payment arrived on the value date. An individual sending money to family needs to know it arrived in full. Without gpi, the ordering bank could only say the payment had been sent. It could not say where it was or when it would arrive, and it could not explain why the beneficiary received less than the amount sent.

gpi matters commercially because it turns that uncertainty into a service the bank can sell. A bank that offers gpi tracking can answer "where is my payment" in seconds, and it can show the customer the charges and the foreign exchange rate applied along the way. That transparency is now the expected standard for cross-border payments, and a bank that cannot offer it loses business to one that can.

For operations teams, gpi reduces the manual work of chasing payments. Before gpi, a payment investigation meant emails and phone calls to correspondent banks, often taking days. With gpi, the tracker shows the status, and a case can be raised through the standard gpi process. Fewer investigations means lower cost and faster resolution for the customer.

For a business analyst, gpi is a common requirement. A project to join gpi, to expose the tracker to customers through online banking, or to build payment confirmations into the operations dashboard all start from understanding these four service levels and the UETR. Interviewers test gpi because it shows whether a candidate understands how a cross-border payment actually moves, rather than just naming message types.

How it works in a bank

A bank joins gpi by signing the gpi rulebook and connecting its payment hub to the tracker. The rulebook sets the obligations: the bank must assign a UETR to every gpi payment, must send status updates as the payment progresses, must confirm when it credits a payment to a beneficiary, and must handle gpi cases within agreed timeframes. Joining is a project that touches the payment hub, the nostro and settlement teams, operations, and compliance, because every bank in the chain has to play its part.

The UETR is assigned. When a customer instructs a cross-border payment, the payment hub builds the pacs.008 and assigns the UETR. The UETR is a 36-character UUID that never changes for that payment. It is carried in the pacs.008 and in every status report that follows.

Status updates are sent. As the payment moves through the chain, each bank sends a pacs.002 status report with the UETR. The statuses are ACSP (accepted, settlement in progress), ACSC (accepted, settlement completed), ACCC (accepted and credited to the beneficiary), PDNG (pending), and RJCT (rejected). Each status update also carries the charges deducted by that bank and the foreign exchange rate applied, so the tracker can show the full cost of the journey.

The tracker shows the journey. The gpi tracker collects all the status reports for a UETR and presents them in one view. The ordering bank can see which bank currently holds the payment, how long each step took, and what charges were applied. The customer-facing version of this view is what the bank shows in online banking or in a payment status enquiry.

Credit is confirmed. When the beneficiary bank credits the beneficiary account, it sends a pacs.002 with status ACCC. That confirmation is the proof that the payment arrived. Under the gpi rulebook, banks must confirm credit promptly, and the confirmation is tracked so that banks that are slow to confirm are visible.

Cases are resolved through gpi. If a payment is stuck, missing, or needs to be stopped, the bank raises a gpi case. The case is linked to the UETR and sent to the bank that holds the payment. The case types include stop and recall, investigation of a payment that has not arrived, and requests for information about a payment. Each case type has a standard set of questions, so the receiving bank can act without a round of clarifying emails.

The observer watches quality. The gpi observer measures how well the chain is performing. It shows whether banks are confirming credit on time, whether status updates are being sent, and where delays happen. A bank uses the observer to hold its correspondents to account and to decide which correspondent to use for a corridor.

Worked example

A UK importer, Meridian Foods Ltd, pays a supplier in Singapore, Tan Trading Pte Ltd, SGD 120,000 for a shipment of goods. Meridian instructs the payment through its bank's corporate portal at 09:30 on a Monday. The bank is a gpi member.

The bank validates the instruction, runs sanctions screening, debits Meridian's GBP account, and converts the amount to SGD. It builds a pacs.008 with the debtor (Meridian Foods Ltd), the creditor (Tan Trading Pte Ltd), the amount SGD 120,000, the charge option SHAR, and the invoice number in the remittance information. It assigns a UETR and records it against Meridian's payment.

The bank has no direct relationship with the supplier's bank in Singapore, so the payment goes through a correspondent bank. The correspondent bank receives the pacs.008, screens it, and sends a pacs.002 with status ACSP. It forwards the payment to the supplier's bank and sends a pacs.002 with status ACSC when settlement between the banks is complete. The tracker now shows the payment has left the UK bank, passed the correspondent bank, and reached the supplier's bank.

The supplier's bank receives the payment, checks the beneficiary account, and credits SGD 120,000 to Tan Trading Pte Ltd. It sends a pacs.002 with status ACCC, confirming the credit. The tracker shows the full journey: sent 09:30 Monday, credited Tuesday morning Singapore time, with the charges of each bank listed separately. Meridian's treasurer checks the tracker in the corporate portal and sees the payment has arrived, without calling the bank. The whole journey took less than a day, and the supplier received the full SGD 120,000 because each bank charged its own customer under the SHAR option rather than deducting from the payment amount.

Technical detail

The four service levels in practice. A bank does not have to adopt all four at once. Most banks start with the tracker, because it delivers the customer-facing benefit. Payment confirmations come next, because they require the beneficiary bank to send the ACCC status reliably. Case resolution is usually the last to be adopted, because it needs operations teams to work to a standard process. The observer runs alongside, measuring the quality of the other three.

The status codes. The pacs.002 carries a group status and, where relevant, a transaction status. ACSP means the payment has been accepted and settlement is in progress. ACSC means settlement between banks is complete, but the beneficiary account may not yet be credited. ACCC means the beneficiary account has been credited. PDNG means the payment is pending, usually because it is held for a check. RJCT means the payment has been rejected. A payment that shows ACSC has settled between banks but may not yet be in the beneficiary's account. Only ACCC confirms the beneficiary received the money.

The rulebook obligations. The gpi rulebook requires member banks to use the UETR on every gpi payment, to send status updates, to confirm credit to the beneficiary, and to process gpi cases within agreed timescales. It also requires that charges are not deducted from the payment amount along the chain. Instead, each bank charges its own customer, so the beneficiary receives the full instructed amount. This is one of the main differences between a gpi payment and an ordinary SWIFT payment, where intermediary banks can deduct charges from the principal.

The tracker and the observer. The tracker is the operational view of a single payment. The observer is the analytical view across many payments. The observer shows a bank how its correspondents perform: which banks confirm credit quickly, which banks are slow to send status updates, and where payments in a corridor typically stall. A bank uses the observer data when it reviews its correspondent relationships and when it chooses which correspondent to route a payment through.

gpi and ISO 20022. gpi was designed for the MT message world and carried over into ISO 20022. The UETR is carried in the pacs.008, the pacs.002 status reports, and the pacs.004 return. The CBPR+ migration to ISO 20022 did not change the gpi service levels. It changed the message format that carries them.

Common mistakes

Treating ACSC as proof the beneficiary was paid. ACSC means settlement between banks is complete. It does not mean the beneficiary account was credited. Only ACCC confirms the credit. Telling a customer the payment arrived on the strength of an ACSC status is a common error, and it causes disputes when the beneficiary says the money is not there.

Assuming every bank in the chain is a gpi bank. The tracker can only show what gpi banks report. If a payment passes through a bank that is not a gpi member, that bank sends no status updates and the tracker shows a gap. The payment is not lost, but the visibility stops at the gap. Check whether all the banks in a corridor are gpi members before promising a customer full tracking.

Assuming gpi makes payments instant. gpi improves tracking and speed, but it does not remove the real constraints on a cross-border payment. Settlement still depends on nostro funding, cut-off times, time zones, and business days in both countries. A gpi payment instructed late on a Friday will still arrive on Monday. gpi tells you where the payment is, it does not teleport the money.

Assuming gpi removes all charges. gpi makes charges visible, it does not remove them. Each bank in the chain still charges for its service. The difference is that under gpi the charges are shown separately and are not deducted from the payment amount, so the beneficiary receives the full instructed amount. The sender still pays, and the total cost can still be significant.

Forgetting the confirmations. A bank that sends gpi payments but does not send ACCC confirmations when it receives them is breaking the rulebook and degrading the service for every other bank. Confirmations are not optional. They are the part of gpi that proves the payment arrived, and they need to be built into the payment hub as an automated step, not left to manual operations.
