Payment Reconciliation Explained

By Payments Training Hub · Last reviewed 18 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • Reconciliation matches three sources: clearing confirmations, settlement entries, and ledger postings. A payment is finished only when all three agree.
  • Nostro reconciliation matches the bank's own records against the account statement from its correspondent bank, usually a camt.053.
  • A break is any mismatch: amount, timing, missing entry, or duplicate. Breaks age, escalate, and must be cleared with a root cause, not forced.
  • The UETR and EndToEndId are the match keys that work across banks; internal identifiers do not.

Reading time: about 6 min

What it is

Reconciliation is the control that proves a payment actually happened the way the bank recorded it. The bank matches three sources against each other: the clearing confirmations (usually pacs.002 status reports), the settlement entries (from the central bank or the nostro account statement, usually a camt.053), and its own ledger postings. If all three agree on amount, currency, value date and reference, the payment is reconciled. If they do not agree, there is a break, and someone must investigate.

Nostro reconciliation is the same discipline applied to the bank's accounts held at other banks. The correspondent bank sends a statement of the nostro account. The bank matches every statement entry against its own payment records. Anything unmatched on either side is a break.

Why it matters

Reconciliation is the control that catches errors other controls miss. A payment can clear, settle and credit the beneficiary, and still be wrong in the ledger. A charge can be deducted that nobody expected. A duplicate can post twice. A payment can settle a day late and nobody notices until the customer complains. Reconciliation finds these, because it compares independent records of the same event.

Breaks also carry risk and cost. An unmatched debit may mean money left the bank that should not have. An unmatched credit may mean the bank owes a customer money it has not recognised. Ageing breaks draw audit findings, because an old break usually means a control failed and nobody acted. For a BA, reconciliation matters because most payment requirements end at reconciliation: a feature is not done until its entries reconcile.

How it works in a bank

Reconciliation runs daily, usually overnight after the business day closes. The cycle is:

1. Collect the sources. Clearing confirmations, settlement entries, nostro statements (camt.053, with camt.052 intraday reports and camt.054 notifications where used), and the ledger extract.

2. Auto-match. The reconciliation engine matches entries on keys: amount, currency, value date, and reference, with the UETR and EndToEndId as the strongest keys because they travel across banks. Most entries match automatically. Banks set tolerances for small differences caused by rounding or FX conversion, and the tolerance itself is a controlled parameter, not a guess.

3. Breaks. Anything unmatched becomes a break, classified by type: amount break, timing break (value date differs from posting date), missing entry (one side has it, the other does not), or duplicate. Each break gets an owner and an age.

4. Investigation and clearing. The team works breaks oldest first: check the messages, check the statement, check the ledger, find the cause, post the correction or chase the counterparty, and clear the break with the root cause recorded.

5. Suspense and ageing. Entries that cannot be matched yet sit in suspense accounts, which must be controlled and aged. A growing suspense balance is a warning sign. Breaks age in buckets, and the oldest buckets escalate to management.

Worked example

A company pays a supplier 25,000 euros through a correspondent bank, with charges shared (SHA).

The sources. The clearing confirmation (pacs.002) shows ACSC for 25,000 euros. The nostro statement (camt.053) shows a debit of 25,000 euros. The ledger shows a debit of 24,950 euros to the company account. The three sources disagree by 50 euros: a break.

The investigation. The reconciliation analyst checks the charge detail on the payment. The intermediary bank deducted its 50 euro charge under the SHA arrangement, so the ledger posting of 24,950 already reflects the charge, while the clearing and settlement records show the gross 25,000. The cause is identified: a legitimate charge, posted in the wrong place for matching purposes.

The clearing. The analyst posts the 50 euro charge to the correct charge account, re-runs the match, and all three sources agree. The break is cleared with the root cause recorded: intermediary charge under SHA, not an error.

A second, simpler break: a payment with value date Monday posts to the ledger on Tuesday because of a cut-off. On Monday night it appears as a timing break. On Tuesday it matches and clears on its own. Timing breaks are normal; the discipline is to confirm they clear, not to force them.

Technical detail

Match keys. The UETR is the globally unique reference that tracks a payment across all banks and systems, and the EndToEndId travels from debtor to creditor. These are the keys that match across institutions. The InstrId identifies the instruction within one bank only, and the TxId identifies a transaction within one system. Matching on internal identifiers across banks fails.

Value date vs booking date. The value date is when funds are available and interest starts. The booking date is when the entry posts. They differ across cut-offs, weekends and time zones, and most timing breaks come from this gap. Requirements must state which date each report uses.

Statements. The camt.053 account statement is the standard nostro statement. Intraday reporting uses camt.052, and debit/credit notifications use camt.054. A BA writing reconciliation requirements must know which statement the bank receives, its frequency, and its cut-off.

Tolerances. Auto-match tolerances exist for rounding and FX differences. They are set, approved, and audited. A tolerance that is too wide hides real breaks; one that is too narrow floods the team with noise.

Common mistakes

Forcing matches. Clearing a break by adjusting an entry without finding the cause hides the error and guarantees it recurs.

Clearing breaks without a root cause. If the cause is not recorded, the same break returns next month, and the pattern stays invisible.

Ignoring small breaks. Small breaks compound, and a pattern of small breaks usually signals a systematic error in charges, FX, or rounding.

Matching on amount only. Amounts repeat. Without reference keys, the engine matches the wrong entries and creates false comfort.

Letting suspense accounts grow. Suspense is a parking place, not a destination. An ageing suspense balance means breaks are being stored, not solved.
