Payment Status State Machine Explained

By Payments Training Hub · Last reviewed 09 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • A payment status state machine is the formal model of the lifecycle of a payment.
  • A typical payment state machine has these states:
  • - Received.

Reading time: about 13 min

What it is

A payment status state machine is the formal model of the lifecycle of a payment. It defines the states a payment can be in, the transitions that move it from one state to another, and who is allowed to make each move. Every payment in a bank follows the same state machine, from the moment it is received to the moment it reaches a final state.

A typical payment state machine has these states:

  • Received. The payment instruction has arrived at the bank and been recorded, but no checks have run yet.
  • Validated. The payment has passed the bank's validation checks: the account and beneficiary details, the amount, the currency, the value date, and the sanctions and fraud screening.
  • Queued. The payment has passed validation and is waiting to be sent, usually because it is held for a cut-off time, a value date, or a batch.
  • Sent. The payment instruction has left the bank and is travelling to the next bank or clearing system.
  • Settled. The funds have been settled between banks and the payment has reached the beneficiary bank.
  • Rejected. The payment failed validation or a control and will not be sent. This is a final state.
  • Returned. The payment was sent but came back, because the beneficiary bank could not credit it. This is a final state.
  • Recalled. The payment was settled and then returned at the request of the sending bank, through a cancellation or recall. This is a final state.

A payment can also sit in holding states along the way, such as in repair or on hold for a compliance review. These are not separate destinations. They are states the payment passes through on its way to one of the states above.

The state machine matters because it gives every payment a single, unambiguous status at any moment. A customer asking where their payment is, an operations analyst working a repair queue, and a reconciliation system matching payments to settlements all need to know the same thing: what state is this payment in. The state machine is the answer.

Why it matters

A payment without a clear state is a payment no one can account for. If a payment can be "in progress" without further detail, the operations team cannot tell whether it is waiting for a cut-off, held for a sanctions check, or stuck in a failed system. The customer cannot be told when it will arrive. Reconciliation cannot tell whether the payment has settled. The state machine removes that ambiguity by forcing every payment into one defined state at all times.

The state machine also protects the bank from invalid actions. A payment that has been rejected must not be sent. A payment that has already settled must not be sent again. A payment that is in repair must not be settled before the repair is complete. These rules are enforced by the state machine: a transition that is not allowed is refused by the system, no matter who requests it. Without the state machine, these protections depend on people remembering the rules, and people forget.

For reconciliation, the state machine is the foundation. The reconciliation team matches payments to settlements by state: a payment in the sent state should have a matching settlement, a payment in the settled state should have a matching credit, and a payment in the returned state should have a matching return of funds. A payment whose state does not match the settlement records is an exception, and it needs investigation.

For a business analyst, the state machine is one of the first things specified in a payment hub project. The analyst defines the states, the allowed transitions, the events that trigger each transition, and who is authorised to make each move. The state machine then drives the operations screens, the customer-facing status, the reconciliation reports, and the audit trail. A hub built without a clear state machine will have payments in undefined states, and the operations team will spend its time working around the gaps.

How it works in a bank

The payment enters the state machine. When a payment instruction arrives, through online banking, a file, or an API, the payment hub creates a payment record and sets its state to received. The record carries the payment details, the state, and a history of every state change, with the time and the actor that made it.

Validation moves it forward or rejects it. The hub runs its validation checks. If the payment passes, the state changes to validated. If it fails a check that cannot be repaired, the state changes to rejected, which is final. The customer is told the payment was rejected and why. If it fails a check that can be repaired, the payment moves to a repair state, and from there back to validated when the repair is complete, or to rejected if the repair cannot be done.

The payment waits in the queue. A validated payment moves to queued. It waits there for its value date, its cut-off time, or its batch. A payment instructed after the cut-off waits until the next business day. A payment with a future value date waits until that date. The queued state is normal, not a problem. It means the payment is ready and waiting for its turn.

The payment is sent. When its time comes, the hub sends the payment instruction to the next bank or clearing system, and the state changes to sent. The hub records the time of sending, the message reference, and the UETR. The customer can now be told the payment has been sent, and the tracker can show the payment's journey.

Settlement completes the journey. When the funds settle between banks and the beneficiary bank confirms the credit, the state changes to settled. In a gpi payment, the ACCC status on the pacs.002 is the event that moves the payment to settled. Settlement is the normal final state for a successful payment. A payment in the settled state is done, and it should not move again except in the case of a recall.

Exceptions move the payment to a final state. A payment that is sent but cannot be credited is returned by the beneficiary bank, and the state changes to returned. A payment that is settled and then recalled at the sending bank's request changes to recalled. A payment that fails validation changes to rejected. Returned, recalled, and rejected are all final states. A payment in a final state does not move again.

Who can move a payment. The payment hub moves a payment automatically through the normal transitions: received to validated, validated to queued, queued to sent, and sent to settled. Operations staff move a payment out of repair and can reject a payment that cannot be fixed. Compliance staff can hold a payment for review and release it when the review is complete. The beneficiary bank moves a payment to returned. The sending bank requests a recall, which moves a settled payment to recalled. Each transition records who made it, so that the history is auditable.

Worked example

A UK company, Harrow Engineering Ltd, instructs a payment of EUR 50,000 to a supplier in Germany on a Monday at 09:00. The payment's journey through the state machine is recorded as follows.

09:00, received. The payment instruction arrives through the corporate portal. The hub creates the payment record and sets the state to received.

09:01, validated. The hub runs its checks: the beneficiary IBAN is valid, the amount is within limits, the account has sufficient funds, and the sanctions screening passes. The state changes to validated.

09:01, queued. The payment is ready, but the EUR cut-off for same-day settlement is 14:00 and the payment is batched for the 11:00 clearing. The state changes to queued.

11:00, sent. The batch is released. The pacs.008 is sent to the correspondent bank, and the state changes to sent. Harrow's finance team can see in the portal that the payment has been sent.

14:30, settled. The correspondent bank settles with the supplier's bank, the supplier's bank credits the beneficiary account, and a pacs.002 with status ACCC arrives. The state changes to settled. The payment is complete.

The same payment could have taken a different path. If the IBAN had been invalid, the payment would have moved from received to a repair state, then to validated after the repair, and then to queued. If the sanctions screening had hit, the payment would have moved to a hold state, then to validated if cleared, or to rejected if the hit was confirmed. If the supplier's bank had been unable to credit the payment, it would have moved from sent to returned. The state machine covers all of these paths, and at every moment the payment is in exactly one state.

Technical detail

Allowed transitions. The state machine defines which transitions are allowed. The normal path is received, validated, queued, sent, settled. From received, a payment can go to validated, to a repair or hold state, or to rejected. From validated, it can go to queued or to rejected. From queued, it can go to sent or back to a hold state. From sent, it can go to settled or to returned. From settled, it can go to recalled, and to nothing else. A transition that is not in the list is refused. The system must enforce this, not just document it, so that a payment cannot be settled from the rejected state, or sent twice from the sent state.

Terminal states. Rejected, returned, recalled, and settled are terminal states. A payment in a terminal state does not move again. Settled is terminal in the normal case, with the single exception of a recall, which moves it to recalled. A payment hub should treat terminal states as locked, so that no further action can be taken on the payment except reporting and audit. A payment that moves out of a terminal state is a sign of a control failure.

Holding states. Repair and compliance hold are not destinations. They are states a payment passes through on its way to a final outcome. A payment in repair should move to validated when the repair is complete, or to rejected when the repair cannot be done. A payment on hold should move to validated when the review clears it, or to rejected when the review confirms a problem. A payment must not sit in a holding state indefinitely. Each holding state needs an age limit and an escalation rule, so that a payment that has been held too long is noticed.

Events and transitions. Each transition is triggered by an event. The received to validated transition is triggered by the validation checks passing. The queued to sent transition is triggered by the cut-off or batch release. The sent to settled transition is triggered by the settlement confirmation or the ACCC status. The sent to returned transition is triggered by a pacs.004 return. The settled to recalled transition is triggered by a successful camt.056 recall. Naming the event for each transition makes the state machine precise and testable, and it lets the operations team understand exactly what happened to a payment.

Status and sub-status. The state is the high-level position of the payment. A sub-status gives more detail. A payment in the sent state might have the sub-status "awaiting settlement confirmation" or "with correspondent bank". A payment in a hold state might have the sub-status "sanctions review" or "fraud review". The sub-status is useful for operations, but it must not replace the state. A payment must always have a state, and the state must always be one of the defined values. Reporting and reconciliation use the state, not the sub-status.

Mapping to message statuses. The payment's internal state and the external message statuses are related but not identical. A pacs.002 with status ACSP means the payment has been accepted and settlement is in progress, which usually maps to the sent state. A pacs.002 with status ACCC means the payment has been credited, which maps to settled. A pacs.002 with status RJCT maps to rejected or returned, depending on when it arrives. The payment hub must map each incoming message status to the correct internal state, and must refuse a mapping that would be an invalid transition.

Common mistakes

Letting a payment sit in an undefined state. A payment that is not in any defined state cannot be reported, reconciled, or investigated. Every payment must be in exactly one state at all times, from the moment it is received to the moment it reaches a terminal state. If a payment falls out of the state machine because of a system error, it must be flagged immediately and put back into a defined state by an operations analyst.

Allowing invalid transitions. A payment that has been rejected must not be sent. A payment that has already settled must not be sent again. A payment in a terminal state must not move. If the system allows these transitions, duplicates and misdirected payments will follow. The state machine must be enforced in the system, not just written in a document. A transition that is not allowed should be refused automatically, with an error that says which rule was broken.

No audit trail of state changes. Every state change must record the time, the event that triggered it, and who or what made it. Without that history, the bank cannot explain to a customer why a payment was delayed, cannot investigate a dispute, and cannot prove to a regulator that its payments are controlled. The audit trail is part of the state machine, not an optional extra.

Confusing the internal state with the external status. The payment's internal state and the status shown to the customer or sent in a message are related but not the same. A payment in the sent state may be shown to the customer as "sent", but the beneficiary bank's system may show it as "received". The internal state is the bank's own record. The external status is what the bank tells others. Mixing the two up causes confusion, especially when a payment is in a holding state that the customer should not see in detail.

Treating sent as settled. A payment in the sent state has left the bank, but it has not necessarily arrived. It can still be delayed, held, or returned. Telling a customer a payment has arrived because it has been sent is a common error, and it causes disputes when the beneficiary says the money is not there. Only the settled state, confirmed by the ACCC status, means the beneficiary has been credited.
