Correspondent Banking Explained: How Banks Pay Each Other Across Borders

What it is

Correspondent banking is the system that lets banks move money across borders when they do not have a direct relationship with each other. A bank in one country holds an account with a bank in another country. That account is called a nostro account. The bank holding the account is the respondent bank. The bank that holds the account for them is the correspondent bank.

When a customer of the respondent bank wants to pay someone in the correspondent bank's country, the respondent bank instructs the correspondent bank to make the payment from the nostro account. The correspondent bank debits the nostro account and pays the beneficiary. This is how most cross-border payments work.

Correspondent banking is the backbone of international payments. Without it, a bank could only pay beneficiaries in countries where it has its own branch or subsidiary. With it, a bank can reach almost every country in the world.

Why it matters

Almost every cross-border payment you make passes through correspondent banking. When you pay a supplier in another country, your bank almost certainly uses a correspondent bank to settle the payment. Understanding correspondent banking is essential for anyone working in international payments, trade finance, treasury, or transaction banking.

Correspondent banking also carries risk. It is expensive, slow, and exposed to financial crime risk. Banks have been reducing their correspondent relationships in recent years, a trend called de-risking. This makes it harder and more expensive for some countries to access the global payment system. Understanding why correspondent banking is changing is important for any payments professional.

How it works in a bank

The basic chain

A company in Kenya wants to pay a supplier in Japan. The Kenyan company banks with Equity Bank in Nairobi. The supplier banks with MUFG in Tokyo. Equity Bank does not have a relationship with MUFG. Equity Bank holds a US dollar nostro account with its correspondent bank, Citibank in New York. MUFG holds a US dollar nostro account with its correspondent, also a bank in New York.

Equity Bank sends an MT103 to MUFG. The MT103 instructs MUFG to pay the supplier. Equity Bank also sends an MT202 COV through the correspondent chain to move the funds. Citibank debits the Equity Bank nostro account and sends the funds to MUFG's correspondent, which credits MUFG. MUFG receives the MT103 and the funds, matches them, and credits the supplier.

The payment chain is: Equity Bank, Citibank New York, MUFG's correspondent, MUFG Tokyo. Four banks are involved in a single payment. Each bank charges a fee. Each bank screens the payment. The payment takes two to three days.

Nostro and vostro

The same account has two names depending on whose perspective you take. From the respondent bank's perspective, the account it holds with the correspondent is a nostro account, meaning "our account with you". From the correspondent bank's perspective, the same account is a vostro account, meaning "your account with us". It is the same account, seen from two sides.

A bank's treasury team manages its nostro accounts. Treasury must keep enough money in each nostro account to settle the day's payments. If a nostro account runs out of money, payments stop. Treasury monitors nostro balances throughout the day and moves funds between accounts as needed. This is called nostro funding or liquidity management.

The payment chain in practice

A cross-border payment typically passes through three to five banks. Each bank in the chain performs checks. It verifies the payment instruction. It screens the sender and beneficiary against sanctions lists. It checks for fraud indicators. It verifies the account details. If anything fails, the payment stops and goes to an exceptions queue.

Each bank also takes a cut. The charge option in the payment instruction decides who pays. Under OUR, the sender pays all charges. Under SHA, the sender and beneficiary share charges. Under BEN, the beneficiary pays all charges. Intermediary bank charges are deducted from the payment amount as it travels through the chain.

Worked example

A textile exporter in Bangladesh, Dhaka Textiles, receives USD 200,000 from a buyer in the United States. The buyer banks with Bank of America in New York. Dhaka Textiles banks with Dutch-Bangla Bank in Dhaka.

Bank of America sends an MT103 to Dutch-Bangla Bank. The MT103 instructs Dutch-Bangla Bank to credit USD 200,000 to Dhaka Textiles. Bank of America also sends an MT202 COV through the correspondent chain to move the funds. Dutch-Bangla Bank holds a US dollar nostro account with its correspondent, Standard Chartered in New York. Bank of America sends the funds to Standard Chartered, which credits the Dutch-Bangla Bank nostro account. Dutch-Bangla Bank receives the MT103 and the funds, matches them, and credits Dhaka Textiles.

The chain is: Bank of America, Standard Chartered New York, Dutch-Bangla Bank Dhaka. Three banks, two days, and the exporter receives the funds.

Technical detail

The key messages in correspondent banking are:

  • MT103, the customer credit transfer, which carries the payment instruction to the beneficiary bank.
  • MT202, the bank-to-bank transfer, which moves funds between banks.
  • MT202 COV, the cover payment, which moves funds and carries the customer details so every bank can screen the payment.
  • MT900, the confirmation of debit, which tells a bank that its account has been debited.
  • MT910, the confirmation of credit, which tells a bank that its account has been credited.
  • MT950, the statement message, which gives a bank the statement of its nostro account.
  • MT940, the customer statement message, which gives a customer the statement of their account.

The key accounts are:

  • Nostro account. An account a bank holds with a correspondent bank in another country, usually in the correspondent's local currency.
  • Vostro account. The same account, seen from the correspondent bank's perspective.
  • Settlement account. An account a bank holds with a central bank, used to settle payments in that currency.

Under ISO 20022, the MT103 maps to the pacs.008, the MT202 maps to the pacs.009, the MT900 and MT910 map to the camt.054, and the MT950 maps to the camt.053.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing nostro and vostro. They are the same account from different perspectives. Nostro is "our account with you". Vostro is "your account with us". If you get them mixed up, your reconciliation will be backwards.
  • Underfunding a nostro account. If a nostro account runs out of money, payments stop. Treasury must forecast the day's payment volume and fund the account in advance. A nostro funding failure is an operational incident.
  • Ignoring the cost of the chain. Each bank in the chain charges a fee. A payment through four banks can cost significantly more than the customer expects. Always explain the charges to the customer.
  • Assuming the payment is instant. Correspondent banking is not instant. A payment through multiple banks takes time. Set customer expectations correctly.
  • Forgetting the sanctions risk. Every bank in the chain screens the payment. If any bank finds a sanctions hit, the payment stops. A payment can be delayed for days while a sanctions alert is investigated.

How it is changing

Correspondent banking is shrinking. Banks have been closing correspondent relationships because the cost of compliance is high and the revenue is low. This is called de-risking. Smaller banks and banks in higher-risk countries are finding it harder to maintain correspondent relationships.

At the same time, new alternatives are emerging. Faster payment systems are connecting across borders. ISO 20022 is making payments more transparent and easier to process. Central bank digital currencies and new settlement networks may reduce the need for correspondent banking in the future. But for now, correspondent banking remains the foundation of cross-border payments. Any payments professional needs to understand it deeply.
