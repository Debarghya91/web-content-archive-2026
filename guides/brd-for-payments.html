How to Write a BRD for a Payments Project

By Payments Training Hub · Last reviewed 18 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • A payments BRD is a contract between business and engineering: it must pin down messages, fields, rules, timing and failure paths, not just intent.
  • Most payments BRDs fail in the same places: missing field mappings, no exception paths, and acceptance criteria nobody can test.
  • Structure the BRD as objective and scope, as-is and to-be flows, message catalogue, field mapping, business rules, exception paths, non-functional requirements, acceptance criteria, and dependencies.
  • Give every requirement an ID and trace it to design and test cases, or changes will quietly drop requirements.

Reading time: about 6 min

What it is

A BRD, a business requirements document, states what a change must do, in enough detail that engineers can build it and testers can verify it. In payments, that bar is higher than in most domains. A requirement that says "the system must accept ISO 20022 payments" cannot be built. A requirement that says "the system must accept pacs.008 messages conforming to the CBPR+ usage guidelines, validate the creditor IBAN, and reject invalid instructions with a pacs.002 carrying RJCT and the applicable reason code" can be built, tested and signed off.

The BRD is not a design document and it is not a vendor manual. It states the business need, the rules, and the outcomes. It names the messages and fields involved, because in payments the message is the requirement: if a field is not specified, it will be guessed, and guesses in payments become production defects.

Why it matters

Payments failures trace back to vague requirements more often than to bad code. A missing field mapping becomes a truncated remittance. An unhandled reject becomes a payment stuck in a queue. A wrong cut-off becomes a value-date error that costs a customer interest. Each of these is cheap to prevent in the BRD and expensive to fix in production, where it needs a code change, a test cycle, a release window and often a client communication.

The BRD also protects the BA. When scope is disputed, when a vendor claims a behaviour was never requested, or when a defect is argued as a change, the BRD is the record of what was agreed. A BRD with IDs, versions and sign-off turns arguments into lookups.

How it works in a bank

A payments BRD is usually built through workshops with operations, technology, compliance, and sometimes the scheme or vendor. The BA drafts, circulates, and revises through review cycles until the business owner signs off. The sections that matter most are:

Objective and scope. What changes, what does not, and what success looks like. Scope boundaries matter: "in scope: pacs.008 receipt and validation; out of scope: pacs.009 generation" prevents months of drift.

As-is and to-be flows. Process flows showing the current state and the target state, with the systems, messages and handoffs named at each step.

Message catalogue. Every message in scope, its direction, its version or usage guideline, and its purpose. For example: pacs.008 inbound from the clearing system, pacs.002 outbound on acceptance and rejection.

Field mapping. Source field, target field, transformation rule, and default value, row by row. This is where MT to MX work lives, and where truncation and data loss decisions are recorded.

Business rules. Numbered rules with conditions and outcomes: validation rules, routing rules, charge rules, cut-off rules, and duplicate checks.

Exception paths. Every reject, return, repair, recall and timeout the change must handle, with the message, the reason code, the queue, and the owner for each one.

Non-functional requirements. Volumes, timing, availability, audit, and reporting needs, stated as numbers the design must meet.

Acceptance criteria. Testable conditions, usually in given/when/then form, one per requirement or per rule.

Dependencies and assumptions. Other programmes, vendor deliveries, scheme deadlines, and anything the requirements assume to be true.

Worked example

A bank is adding CBPR+ validation for inbound pacs.008 messages. One BRD extract:

Requirement REQ-014. The system must validate each inbound pacs.008 against the CBPR+ usage guidelines before posting. Mapping row: MT103 field 52a (ordering institution) maps to pacs.008 DbtrAgt (debtor agent), with the BIC carried without truncation. Business rule BR-07: if the creditor IBAN fails the modulus check, reject the instruction and return a pacs.002 with status RJCT and the applicable reason code, and route the item to the repair queue with the validation error attached. Acceptance criteria: given a pacs.008 with an invalid creditor IBAN, when the message is received, then a pacs.002 with RJCT is sent, the item appears in the repair queue with the error, and no ledger posting is made.

Note what the extract does: it names the message, the field, the rule, the failure message, the queue, and the observable outcome. An engineer can build from it. A tester can write a test case from it. A reviewer can challenge it precisely.

Technical detail

Requirement IDs and traceability. Every requirement gets a stable ID (REQ-001 onwards). A traceability matrix maps each requirement to the design element that implements it and the test case that verifies it. When a requirement changes, the matrix shows everything affected.

Mapping conventions. Each mapping row carries source, target, rule, and default. Rules must state what happens on missing data, on overlong data, and on invalid data. "Map as available" is not a rule.

Versioning. The BRD is versioned, and changes after sign-off go through change control with impact notes. Undocumented changes to a signed BRD are how scope creep and audit findings happen.

Separating rules from specs. Business rules (what the bank has decided) live apart from message specs (what the scheme requires). When a scheme updates its guidelines, only the spec section changes, not the business logic.

Common mistakes

Requirements without message detail. "Support ISO 20022" is a headline, not a requirement. Name the messages, the versions, and the fields.

No exception paths. If the BRD only describes the happy path, the build will only handle the happy path. Exceptions are requirements, not afterthoughts.

Acceptance criteria nobody can test. "The system must work correctly" cannot be tested. Given/when/then criteria with observable outcomes can.

Copying vendor documents as requirements. A vendor manual describes what a product does. A BRD describes what the bank needs. They are different documents.

No traceability. Without IDs and a matrix, a late change drops a requirement silently, and nobody finds out until UAT or production.

Scope creep by "also handle". Every "also handle" in a workshop is a scope decision. Record it, cost it, and get it approved, or say no.
