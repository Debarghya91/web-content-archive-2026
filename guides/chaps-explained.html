CHAPS Explained

What it is

CHAPS (Clearing House Automated Payment System) is the UK's high value, same day payment system. It is a real time gross settlement system operated by the Bank of England. It is used for large payments where same day finality matters: house purchases, business to business payments, tax payments, and financial market settlements.

There is no upper limit on a CHAPS payment. There is no lower limit set by the scheme, although banks typically use CHAPS for payments above 10,000 pounds because of the cost. CHAPS is operated by the Bank of England and is one of the largest high value payment systems in the world by value.

Why it matters

CHAPS is where the money is. Although it processes far fewer payments than Faster Payments or Bacs, the total value settled through CHAPS each day is enormous, running into hundreds of billions of pounds. A single CHAPS payment can be worth more than a whole day of Faster Payments volume.

For a business analyst, CHAPS matters because it is the system that handles the payments where failure is most expensive. A house purchase that does not settle on the day means a family cannot move in. A business payment that misses its value date means a contract is breached. The operational discipline around CHAPS, cut-offs, liquidity, and contingency, is the highest in UK payments.

How it works in a bank

Direct participants. Banks that are direct participants in CHAPS hold a settlement account at the Bank of England and connect directly to the Bank's RTGS infrastructure. They send and receive CHAPS payments in real time. Smaller institutions access CHAPS through a direct participant.

The payment flow. A customer instructs a CHAPS payment, often in a branch or through corporate online banking, because of the value involved. The bank validates the instruction, checks the account and the balance, runs fraud and sanctions checks, and debits the sender. The bank then sends the payment to the Bank of England's RTGS system. The Bank of England moves the funds from the sending bank's settlement account to the receiving bank's settlement account immediately, in full. The receiving bank credits the beneficiary. Settlement is final and irrevocable the moment it happens.

Liquidity. Because each payment is settled in full immediately, the sending bank must have enough funds in its Bank of England settlement account at the moment the payment is processed. If it does not, the payment waits in a queue. The bank manages this with intraday liquidity, which it can borrow from the Bank of England against collateral. The bank's treasury team monitors CHAPS liquidity throughout the day, because a queue of high value payments waiting for funds is a serious operational issue.

Cut-off. CHAPS has a cut-off time, currently 6pm for bank to bank payments, with customer cut-offs earlier in the day (typically 3pm to 5pm depending on the bank). A payment submitted after cut-off waits until the next business day. There is no weekend or public holiday CHAPS service.

Worked example

A customer buys a house for 650,000 pounds. Completion day is Friday.

The customer's solicitor instructs a CHAPS payment from the solicitor's client account to the seller's solicitor. The bank validates the instruction, checks the funds, debits the solicitor's account, and sends the payment to the Bank of England. The Bank of England moves the funds immediately. The seller's solicitor's bank credits the seller's solicitor within minutes. The seller's solicitor confirms receipt, the keys are released, and the customer moves in that day.

If the payment had been sent through Faster Payments instead, it would have been limited by the bank's own Faster Payments limit, which is usually well below 650,000 pounds for a single payment. If it had been sent through Bacs, it would have taken three days. CHAPS is the only rail that can move this amount with same day finality.

A second example. A company pays 2 million pounds in corporation tax to HMRC. The payment must arrive on the due date or the company faces penalties and interest. The company instructs a CHAPS payment in the morning. The bank settles it through the Bank of England the same day. The company has proof of settlement, and the tax is paid on time.

Technical detail

Settlement. CHAPS settles in central bank money through the Bank of England's RTGS system. Each payment is settled individually, in full, in real time. There is no netting. Settlement is final and irrevocable.

Messages. CHAPS uses ISO 20022 messages. The customer credit transfer is a pacs.008. The bank to bank transfer is a pacs.009. Status reports use pacs.002. The Bank of England's RTGS system is being renewed, and CHAPS is migrating fully to ISO 20022 under the Bank's RTGS Renewal Programme.

Value date. A CHAPS payment instructed and settled on a business day has a value date of that same day. There is no value dating to a future day for a standard CHAPS payment, although banks may accept instructions in advance and release them on the requested day.

Cost. CHAPS payments carry a fee, typically 20 to 35 pounds for a personal customer, and a negotiated fee for corporate customers. This is much higher than Faster Payments, which is usually free for personal customers. The fee reflects the real time settlement, the liquidity cost, and the operational handling.

Contingency. Because CHAPS is systemically important, the Bank of England operates contingency arrangements. If the primary RTGS system is unavailable, payments can be processed through a backup system. Banks must also have their own contingency plans, including manual procedures for critical payments.

Common mistakes

Using CHAPS for a low value payment. CHAPS is expensive and operationally heavy. A 500 pound payment should go through Faster Payments, not CHAPS. A business analyst who routes low value payments through CHAPS is wasting money and liquidity.

Missing the cut-off. CHAPS has a hard cut-off. A house purchase instructed at 5:30pm on a Friday will not settle until Monday. The customer, the solicitor, and the estate agent must all know the bank's customer cut-off, which is earlier than the Bank of England's system cut-off.

Forgetting the liquidity requirement. The sending bank must have the funds in its Bank of England settlement account. A bank that accepts a large CHAPS instruction without checking its intraday liquidity position will leave the payment stuck in a queue.

Confusing CHAPS with Faster Payments. CHAPS is real time gross settlement with no upper limit and same day finality. Faster Payments is near real time for the customer but settles between banks on a deferred net basis, with a 1 million pound scheme limit. They are different systems for different purposes.

Assuming CHAPS runs at weekends. It does not. CHAPS operates on business days only. A payment that must settle on a Saturday cannot use CHAPS.
