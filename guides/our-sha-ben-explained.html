OUR, SHA and BEN Explained

By Debarghya Mondal · Last reviewed 09 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • OUR, SHA, and BEN are the three charge options for a cross-border payment.
  • - OUR means the sender pays all the charges.
  • - SHA means the charges are shared.

Reading time: about 11 min

What it is

OUR, SHA, and BEN are the three charge options for a cross-border payment. They decide who pays the charges that the banks in the payment chain apply. The option is agreed between the sender and their bank before the payment is sent, and it is carried in the payment message so that every bank in the chain knows who should pay it.

The three options work like this:

  • OUR means the sender pays all the charges. Every bank in the chain charges the sender, and the beneficiary receives the full amount. In the MT103, this is field 71A with the code OUR. In the pacs.008, it is the charge bearer code DEBT, which means the charges are borne by the debtor.
  • SHA means the charges are shared. Each bank charges its own customer: the ordering bank charges the sender, and the beneficiary bank charges the beneficiary. No bank deducts its charge from the payment amount. In the MT103, this is field 71A with the code SHA. In the pacs.008, it is the charge bearer code SHAR.
  • BEN means the beneficiary pays all the charges. Every bank in the chain deducts its charge from the payment amount, and the beneficiary receives what is left. In the MT103, this is field 71A with the code BEN. In the pacs.008, it is the charge bearer code CRED, which means the charges are borne by the creditor.

A fourth code exists in the pacs.008: SLEV, which means the charges follow the service level agreement between the banks. It is rarely used for ordinary customer payments and usually appears in bank to bank arrangements.

The charge option is not a suggestion. It is an instruction to the banks in the chain, and it decides how much money the beneficiary actually receives. Getting it wrong is one of the most common causes of cross-border payment disputes.

Why it matters

A cross-border payment usually passes through two, three, or four banks, and each bank charges for its work. On a payment of USD 50,000, the total charges across the chain might be USD 25 to USD 75, depending on the banks and the corridor. That is a small share of the payment, but it decides whether the beneficiary receives the full amount or something less, and whether the supplier considers the invoice paid in full.

The charge option matters most when the payment settles a contract. A supplier who invoices USD 50,000 and receives USD 49,950 will usually treat the invoice as underpaid. The buyer then has to send a second payment for the shortfall, or argue with the supplier about who should bear the charges. Either way, the relationship suffers and the cost of the dispute is far higher than the charges themselves.

The charge option also matters for regulated payments. Some payments must arrive in full: a tax payment, a loan repayment, or a payment under a letter of credit. If the charge option allows deductions from the principal, the payment arrives short and the obligation is not met. The sender needs to choose the option that guarantees the full amount arrives, and the bank needs to check that the option in the message matches what the customer asked for.

For a business analyst, the charge option appears in almost every cross-border payments requirement. A new product, a new corridor, or a change to the payment hub will all need a decision on the default charge option, on whether customers can choose, and on how the option is shown on the payment confirmation. For an interview candidate, the charge options are a standard test of whether they understand what actually happens to the money in a cross-border payment.

How it works in a bank

The customer chooses the option. When a customer instructs a cross-border payment, the channel asks for the charge option, or the bank applies its default. Corporate customers usually have a default agreed in their service agreement. Retail customers usually accept the default the bank sets. The bank must record the option the customer chose, because that is what the bank is obliged to deliver.

The option is carried in the message. In the MT103, the option is field 71A, details of charges. It holds one code: OUR, SHA, or BEN. In the pacs.008, the option is the charge bearer element, with the values DEBT, SHAR, or CRED. The payment hub must map the customer's choice to the right code in the message, and must validate that the code is present before the message is sent. A payment message with no charge option will be repaired or rejected by the next bank.

Each bank applies the option. Under OUR, each bank in the chain charges the sender. In practice, the banks cannot charge the sender directly, because the sender is not their customer. Instead, they claim their charge from the bank that sent them the payment, and the claim travels back up the chain to the ordering bank, which debits the sender. Under SHA, each bank charges its own customer directly: the ordering bank charges the sender, the beneficiary bank charges the beneficiary, and any intermediary bank charges the bank that sent it the payment. Under BEN, each bank deducts its charge from the payment amount before forwarding it, so the amount shrinks at each step.

The CBPR+ default is SHA. Under the CBPR+ market practice for ISO 20022 cross-border payments, SHA is the default charge option. If the sender and the bank have not agreed otherwise, the payment is sent as SHAR. This default reflects the reality that most cross-border payments are now sent with each side paying its own bank, and it gives the beneficiary a predictable outcome: the beneficiary receives the full instructed amount, and pays their own bank separately.

gpi changes the practice. Under the SWIFT gpi rulebook, banks in the chain must not deduct their charges from the payment amount. Each bank charges its own customer instead. This means a gpi payment arrives in full even when the option is OUR, because the charges are settled separately rather than taken from the principal. The gpi rule is one of the reasons gpi payments are more predictable than ordinary SWIFT payments, and it is a reason banks promote gpi to corporate customers who need the full amount to arrive.

The beneficiary bank's own charge is separate. Even under OUR, the beneficiary bank may still charge the beneficiary for receiving the payment, under its own terms with the beneficiary. The charge option governs the charges of the banks in the chain for moving the payment. It does not govern what the beneficiary bank charges its own customer for its account services. A customer who needs the beneficiary to receive a precise net amount should check the beneficiary bank's receiving charges as well.

Worked example

A UK company, Harrow Engineering Ltd, pays a supplier in Germany, Rheinmetall Components GmbH, EUR 50,000 for invoice INV-2026-2044. The payment passes through three banks: Harrow's bank in London, a correspondent bank in Frankfurt, and the supplier's bank in Munich. Each bank charges EUR 15 for its part in the chain.

With SHA. Harrow's bank charges Harrow EUR 15. The correspondent bank charges Harrow's bank EUR 15, which Harrow's bank passes on to Harrow, so Harrow pays EUR 30 in total. The supplier's bank charges Rheinmetall EUR 15. Rheinmetall receives the full EUR 50,000, and pays its own bank EUR 15 separately. The invoice is settled in full.

With OUR. Harrow pays all the charges: EUR 15 to its own bank, EUR 15 to the correspondent bank, and EUR 15 to the supplier's bank, a total of EUR 45. Rheinmetall receives the full EUR 50,000 and pays nothing. The invoice is settled in full, and Harrow bears the whole cost.

With BEN. Each bank deducts EUR 15 from the payment amount. Harrow's bank forwards EUR 49,985. The correspondent bank deducts EUR 15 and forwards EUR 49,970. The supplier's bank deducts EUR 15 and credits EUR 49,955 to Rheinmetall. Rheinmetall receives EUR 49,955 against an invoice of EUR 50,000, a shortfall of EUR 45. The supplier treats the invoice as underpaid, and Harrow has to send a second payment of EUR 45 or negotiate the difference.

The example shows why the choice matters. The payment amount is the same in all three cases. The difference is who pays the EUR 45 of charges and whether the supplier receives the full invoice amount.

Technical detail

Field 71A in the MT103. Field 71A, details of charges, is a mandatory field in the MT103. It holds one of three codes: OUR, SHA, or BEN. The field is in the main text of the message, so every bank in the chain can read it. A payment hub that builds MT103 messages must always populate field 71A. A message with a missing or invalid field 71A will be stopped for repair by the next bank.

Charge bearer in the pacs.008. The pacs.008 carries the charge bearer in the charges information. The values are DEBT (the debtor pays, equivalent to OUR), SHAR (shared, equivalent to SHA), CRED (the creditor pays, equivalent to BEN), and SLEV (charges follow the service level agreement). The mapping from the MT103 codes to the pacs.008 codes is part of the CBPR+ translation rules, and the payment hub must apply it consistently when it converts between the two formats.

The CBPR+ default. Under the CBPR+ market practice, SHA is the default charge option for cross-border customer payments. If the customer has not chosen an option, the payment is sent as SHAR. The default matters because it decides the outcome for the large number of payments where no one actively chooses. A bank that sets its own default to OUR or BEN is going against the market practice and will create disputes with beneficiary banks that expect SHA.

Charges and foreign exchange. The charge option governs the transfer charges. It does not govern the foreign exchange rate. If the payment involves a currency conversion, the bank that converts the currency applies its own exchange rate and margin, and that cost is separate from the transfer charges. A customer comparing the cost of two charge options should also compare the exchange rate, because the exchange margin is often larger than the transfer charges.

Intermediary charges under OUR. Under OUR, the ordering bank guarantees that the beneficiary receives the full amount. In practice, the ordering bank cannot control every intermediary bank, and an intermediary outside the gpi network may still deduct its charge from the principal. This is why OUR does not always guarantee the full amount in an ordinary SWIFT payment, and why the gpi rule against deducting charges from the principal is valuable. A bank that promises OUR should check whether the corridor is fully gpi, and should warn the customer if it is not.

Common mistakes

Assuming OUR guarantees the beneficiary receives the full amount. OUR means the sender pays all the charges, but it does not guarantee that every bank in the chain will follow the instruction. An intermediary bank outside the gpi network can still deduct its charge from the payment amount, and the beneficiary bank can still charge the beneficiary for receiving the payment. If the full amount must arrive, use a gpi corridor and confirm the charges with the beneficiary bank. Do not promise what the chain cannot deliver.

Using BEN for a supplier payment. BEN means the beneficiary pays all the charges and receives less than the invoiced amount. Suppliers treat this as an underpayment. BEN is appropriate only when the beneficiary has agreed to bear the charges, for example when the sender is paying a refund or a personal transfer where the recipient has accepted the cost. For ordinary supplier payments, use SHA or OUR.

Forgetting the beneficiary bank's own charge. The charge option governs the chain's charges. The beneficiary bank's charge to its own customer for receiving the payment is separate and can still reduce what the beneficiary keeps. A customer who needs the beneficiary to net a precise amount should check the beneficiary bank's receiving charges as well as the charge option.

Leaving field 71A blank. Field 71A is mandatory in the MT103. A payment message without it will be stopped for repair, which delays the payment and costs more than the charges themselves. The payment hub should validate that the charge option is present before the message is sent, and should default to SHA under the CBPR+ market practice when the customer has not chosen.

Confusing the charge option with the exchange rate cost. The charge option decides who pays the transfer charges. It does not decide the exchange rate or the margin on a currency conversion. A customer who chooses OUR to protect the beneficiary from charges can still lose money on a poor exchange rate. Explain both costs to the customer, not just the charge option.
