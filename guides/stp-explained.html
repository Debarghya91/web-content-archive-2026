Straight-Through Processing (STP) Explained

By Debarghya Mondal · Last reviewed 09 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • Straight-Through Processing (STP) Explained
  • Straight-through processing, or STP, means a payment is processed from instruction to settlement without any manual intervention.
  • The STP rate is the share of payments that go straight through.

Reading time: about 10 min

What it is

Straight-through processing, or STP, means a payment is processed from instruction to settlement without any manual intervention. The payment is received, validated, screened, routed, and sent by systems, with no person touching it at any point. A payment that goes straight through is faster, cheaper, and less likely to go wrong than a payment that stops for manual work.

The STP rate is the share of payments that go straight through. It is calculated as the number of payments processed with no manual intervention, divided by the total number of payments, expressed as a percentage. A bank that processes 10,000 payments in a day and repairs 800 of them has an STP rate of 92 percent. The other 8 percent went to a repair queue, a compliance review, or an exception.

STP is not a single number for a whole bank. It is measured by channel, by currency, by corridor, and by payment type. A domestic faster payments flow might run at 99 percent STP, while a cross-border flow with new beneficiaries might run at 85 percent. The breakdown is what makes the measure useful, because it shows where the problems are.

A high STP rate is not the same as a good payments operation on its own. A bank can raise its STP rate by auto-releasing payments that should have been checked, which is dangerous. The goal is a high STP rate achieved by clean data and good validation, not by skipping controls.

Why it matters

STP matters because manual processing is slow and expensive. A payment that goes straight through costs the bank a small fraction of what a payment in a repair queue costs, because the repair needs a person to review it, correct it, and release it. A bank that processes millions of payments a year saves a large amount of money for every percentage point it adds to its STP rate, and it also reduces the risk that a manual touch introduces an error.

STP matters to the customer because it decides speed. A payment that goes straight through is sent within minutes of instruction. A payment that stops for repair can take hours, and if the repair queue is busy, it can take a day. A corporate treasurer who needs a supplier paid on the value date depends on STP. A retail customer sending money to family depends on it too.

STP matters to the bank's risk position because every manual touch is a chance to make a mistake. A payment that is processed automatically follows the same rules every time. A payment that is repaired by a person can be corrected wrongly, released without re-validation, or sent to the wrong account. Fewer manual touches means fewer opportunities for error, and a cleaner audit trail.

For a business analyst, the STP rate is one of the first measures requested in any payments project. A new channel, a new product, or a change to validation rules will all be judged partly on whether they raise or lower the STP rate. The analyst needs to know how the rate is measured, what breaks it, and what can be done to improve it without weakening controls.

How it works in a bank

The payment arrives clean. STP starts before the payment reaches the hub. The channel that receives the instruction, whether it is online banking, a corporate file, or an API, validates the data at the point of entry. It checks the account number format, the mandatory fields, and the amount. A payment that arrives with complete and valid data has the best chance of going straight through. A payment that arrives with missing or invalid data is already on its way to repair.

The hub validates automatically. The payment hub runs a series of automated checks: the account and beneficiary details, the currency and corridor, the value date, the cut-off time, the balance, and the sanctions and fraud screening. Each check either passes, in which case the payment continues, or fails, in which case the payment is routed to a repair queue or a compliance queue. The checks are the same for every payment, and they run in the same order.

The payment is routed and sent. A payment that passes all the checks is routed to the right clearing system or correspondent bank and sent. The hub records each step, sends the status updates, and files the payment for reconciliation. No person is involved. The whole journey from instruction to sending can take seconds for a domestic payment and minutes for a cross-border payment.

Breaks are measured. Every payment that does not go straight through is recorded with a break reason code: invalid account, missing data, sanctions hit, amount mismatch, limit breach, or cut-off missed. The break reasons are reported daily by channel, currency, and corridor. The report shows which reasons are most common and where they are happening, which is the starting point for fixing them.

The rate is reported and reviewed. The STP rate is reported daily to operations management and monthly to senior management. The review looks at the rate, the trend, and the break reasons. A falling STP rate triggers an investigation. A rising rate is checked to make sure it is genuine, and not the result of controls being weakened or repairs being hidden.

Worked example

A bank processes 20,000 cross-border payments in a week. Of those, 17,400 go straight through with no manual intervention. The other 2,600 stop for manual work. The STP rate is 87 percent.

The break reason report shows the 2,600 breaks in detail: 900 are invalid or incomplete beneficiary details, 700 are sanctions screening hits that need manual review, 500 are missing remittance references, 300 are payments that arrived after the cut-off time, and 200 are amount mismatches against the customer's instruction.

The bank's payments team works through the reasons. The 900 beneficiary detail breaks are mostly from one corporate channel, where customers are entering new suppliers without a full address. The team adds a validation rule to the channel that requires the beneficiary address before the instruction can be submitted. The 500 missing references are mostly from a file format that does not carry the reference field, and the team fixes the file specification with the customers who use it. The 300 cut-off misses are customers instructing late in the day, and the team adds a warning in online banking when a payment is instructed after the cut-off.

Three months later, the bank processes 21,000 cross-border payments in a week and 19,110 go straight through. The STP rate is 91 percent. The improvement came from fixing the data at the source, not from changing the hub's checks. The sanctions hits are unchanged at 700, because those are genuine screening results that need a person. The bank accepts that its STP rate will never reach 100 percent, because some payments will always need human judgement.

Technical detail

How the STP rate is calculated. The STP rate is the number of payments processed with no manual intervention, divided by the total number of payments, over a given period. The period is usually a day, a week, or a month. The measure should count each payment once. A payment that is repaired and then sent is not a straight-through payment, even though it was eventually sent. Counting it as STP would hide the repair work and give a false picture.

What breaks STP. The common breaks are invalid or missing beneficiary data, sanctions and fraud screening hits, amount mismatches, missing references, limit breaches, payments that arrive after the cut-off, and non-standard formats that the hub cannot parse. Each break has a reason code, and the codes should be specific enough to act on. A code that just says "repair" is useless. A code that says "invalid IBAN" tells the team exactly what to fix.

STP by segment. The STP rate should be measured by channel, by currency, by corridor, and by payment type, not just as a single bank-wide number. A bank-wide rate of 95 percent can hide a corridor running at 70 percent. The segmented view shows where the problems are and where the improvement work should focus. It also shows whether a new product or channel is performing as expected.

Improving STP. The most effective improvements are usually upstream, at the point where the payment is instructed. Validating data in the channel, requiring mandatory fields, using standing beneficiary instructions, and giving customers immediate feedback on errors all reduce the number of bad payments that reach the hub. Inside the hub, auto-repair rules for certain formatting errors, better reference data, and clearer routing rules also help. The least effective approach is to pressure the repair team to work faster, because that treats the symptom rather than the cause.

STP and controls. A high STP rate must not come at the cost of controls. Sanctions screening, fraud checks, and balance checks must run on every payment, including those that go straight through. A bank that raises its STP rate by skipping a check or by auto-releasing payments that should have been reviewed is taking a compliance risk. The STP rate should be reviewed alongside the control metrics, such as the number of sanctions hits and the number of fraud cases, to make sure the improvement is genuine.

Common mistakes

Counting repaired payments as STP. A payment that went to a repair queue and was then sent is not a straight-through payment. Counting it as STP inflates the rate and hides the repair work. The STP rate must count only payments that had no manual intervention at any point. A repaired payment is a break, even if the repair was quick.

Measuring STP only as a bank-wide number. A single bank-wide STP rate hides the problems. A domestic flow at 99 percent and a cross-border corridor at 70 percent average out to a respectable number, but the corridor is still broken. Measure STP by channel, currency, corridor, and payment type, and fix the segments that are underperforming.

Chasing the rate by weakening controls. The fastest way to raise the STP rate is to stop checking payments. It is also the most dangerous. A bank that auto-releases payments that should have been screened is taking a sanctions and fraud risk that can lead to regulatory action and financial loss. The STP rate should rise because the data is cleaner, not because the checks are weaker.

Fixing the repair queue instead of the source. A growing repair queue is a symptom. The cause is usually upstream: a channel that accepts bad data, a file format that drops a field, or standing instructions that are out of date. Hiring more repair analysts treats the symptom. Fixing the source data and the channel validation treats the cause, and it raises the STP rate permanently.

Ignoring the cost of breaks. A low STP rate is not just a number. Each break is a payment that needed a person, and each person costs money and time. A bank that does not connect its STP rate to its cost per payment will not understand what a falling rate is costing it. Report the STP rate alongside the repair volumes and the cost, so that the business case for fixing the breaks is clear.
