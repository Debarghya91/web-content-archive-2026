pain.001 Explained

By Debarghya Mondal · Last reviewed 07 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • pain.001 is the Customer Credit Transfer Initiation message.
  • A corporate customer sends a pain.001 to its bank, usually as a file containing many payments, or as a single payment from an online banking or ERP system.
  • pain.001 matters because it is the first message in the payment chain.

Reading time: about 5 min

What it is

pain.001 is the Customer Credit Transfer Initiation message. It is the message a customer uses to tell their bank to make a credit transfer. The name comes from the ISO 20022 message set: pain stands for Payment Initiation, and 001 is the credit transfer initiation variant.

A corporate customer sends a pain.001 to its bank, usually as a file containing many payments, or as a single payment from an online banking or ERP system. A retail customer does not normally see pain.001, because their bank builds it internally from the online banking instruction. But the message is the same: it is the instruction that starts a credit transfer.

Why it matters

pain.001 matters because it is the first message in the payment chain. Everything downstream depends on it being correct. If the debtor account is wrong, the payment debits the wrong account. If the creditor details are incomplete, the payment fails at the beneficiary bank. If the amount or currency is wrong, the customer is charged incorrectly.

It also matters because it is where validation happens. The bank checks the pain.001 against its rules before it accepts the instruction. A payment that fails validation here is rejected cheaply, before any money moves. A payment that passes validation here but is wrong will cost far more to fix later, through a return or a recall.

How it works in a bank

The customer creates the pain.001 in their own system, often an ERP or treasury management system, and sends it to the bank through a secure channel such as SWIFT FileAct, host to host, or an API. The bank receives the file and validates it in stages.

First comes technical validation: does the XML match the pain.001 schema, are all mandatory elements present, are the formats correct. Then comes business validation: does the debtor account exist and belong to this customer, is the account funded, is the creditor information complete, does the payment breach any limit or cut-off.

If validation passes, the bank accepts the instruction and returns a pain.002 status report. The pain.002 tells the customer whether each payment was accepted or rejected, and why. The bank then processes the accepted payments, builds the interbank messages such as pacs.008, and sends them onward.

Worked example

A corporate customer, a manufacturer, needs to pay 40 suppliers on Friday. Its treasury system generates a pain.001 file with 40 credit transfer instructions.

Each instruction has a Payment Information block (PmtInf) for the debit side, and a Credit Transfer Transaction Information block (CdtTrfTxInf) for each payment. The PmtInf carries the debtor, the debtor account, the requested execution date, and the payment method. Each CdtTrfTxInf carries the creditor, the creditor account, the instructed amount, and the remittance information with the invoice number.

The bank receives the file at 9 am. It validates all 40 payments. 39 pass. One fails because the creditor account number is two digits short. The bank returns a pain.002 showing 39 accepted and 1 rejected, with the reason code. The customer corrects the one payment and resubmits it. The 39 accepted payments are processed and the suppliers are paid on Friday.

Technical detail

The pain.001 has three main parts:

  • GrpHdr (Group Header): message identification (MsgId), creation date and time (CreDtTm), number of transactions (NbOfTxs), control sum (CtrlSum), and the initiating party (InitgPty).
  • PmtInf (Payment Information): payment information identification (PmtInfId), payment method (PmtMtd, usually TRF for credit transfer), batch booking indicator (BtchBookg), number of transactions, control sum, requested execution date (ReqdExctnDt), debtor (Dbtr), debtor account (DbtrAcct), and debtor agent (DbtrAgt).
  • CdtTrfTxInf (Credit Transfer Transaction Information): payment identification (PmtId, with InstrId and EndToEndId), instructed amount (InstdAmt), charge bearer (ChrgBr), creditor agent (CdtrAgt), creditor (Cdtr), creditor account (CdtrAcct), and remittance information (RmtInf).

Key fields to know:

  • InstrId: the instruction identification, unique per payment, assigned by the customer. It identifies the instruction within the file.
  • EndToEndId: the end to end identification, carried through the whole payment chain to the beneficiary bank. It is how the beneficiary reconciles the payment.
  • BtchBookg: whether the bank should book the debit as one batch entry or as individual entries. True means one batch debit, false means one debit per payment.
  • ReqdExctnDt: the date the customer wants the payment executed. The bank uses this for value dating and cut-off checks.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is reusing an InstrId across payments. The instruction identification must be unique within the file, and ideally unique across files, because the bank uses it to detect duplicates. A reused InstrId can cause a payment to be rejected as a duplicate, or worse, to be processed twice if the duplicate check is weak.

Another mistake is putting the wrong value in EndToEndId. Some customers put "NOTPROVIDED" when they do not have a reference. That is valid, but it means the beneficiary cannot reconcile automatically. Always put the real invoice or order reference in EndToEndId when one exists.

A third mistake is ignoring the pain.002 response. The customer must read the pain.002 and act on rejections. A payment that was rejected in pain.002 will never be processed, and the customer will only discover this when the supplier complains.
