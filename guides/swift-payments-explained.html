SWIFT Payments Explained

By Debarghya Mondal · Last reviewed 09 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • A SWIFT payment is a cross-border payment that travels over the SWIFT network.
  • Three facts about SWIFT surprise most newcomers.
  • The messages that carry SWIFT payments have changed.

Reading time: about 9 min

What it is

A SWIFT payment is a cross-border payment that travels over the SWIFT network. SWIFT is a member-owned cooperative that runs a secure messaging network for financial institutions. More than 11,000 institutions in over 200 countries use it. When a bank sends a payment to a bank in another country, it usually sends the instruction over SWIFT.

Three facts about SWIFT surprise most newcomers. First, SWIFT is a messaging network, not a bank. It does not hold money, it does not settle payments, and it does not move funds. It carries instructions between banks. Second, SWIFT does not move the money behind the instruction. The funds move separately, through nostro accounts that banks hold with each other, or through local clearing systems. Third, a SWIFT payment is a chain, not a single hop. The instruction usually passes through two, three or four banks before it reaches the beneficiary.

The messages that carry SWIFT payments have changed. For decades the standard was the MT103 for customer payments and the MT202 for bank to bank payments. Under CBPR+, the cross-border migration to ISO 20022, those messages are being replaced by pacs.008 and pacs.009. The CBPR+ coexistence period ended on 22 November 2025, and pacs.008 is now the standard format for cross-border customer credit transfers on SWIFT. The MT103 is retired for cross-border traffic between financial institutions, though it may still appear in some domestic or legacy corridors.

Why it matters

Almost every cross-border payment your bank sends or receives travels over SWIFT. If you work in payments operations, correspondent banking, trade finance, or treasury, you will read SWIFT messages daily. You need to know what each message does, because the message decides whether the payment goes straight through or stops for repair.

SWIFT payments matter commercially because they are slow and expensive compared with domestic payments. A payment can take hours or days, and each bank in the chain can deduct a charge. Customers ask why a payment is late, why the beneficiary received less than expected, and whether the money has arrived. Answering those questions means understanding the chain, the messages, and the tracking.

For a business analyst, SWIFT is the most common topic in cross-border payment requirements. A new corridor, a new correspondent bank, a change in cut-off times, or a compliance rule will all land as a change to how the bank builds, sends, and tracks SWIFT messages. For an interview candidate, SWIFT is the first topic interviewers test: how a payment moves, what the messages are, and what happens when it goes wrong.

How it works in a bank

A customer instructs a payment through a channel: online banking, a branch, a corporate file, or an API. The payment hub validates the instruction, checks the account balance, and runs sanctions screening and fraud checks. If the beneficiary bank is in another country and the payment goes through SWIFT, the hub builds the message.

The message. For a customer payment, the hub builds a pacs.008. It carries the debtor (the customer), the creditor (the beneficiary), the amount, the value date, the charge bearer, and the remittance information. It also carries a UETR, the Unique End-to-end Transaction Reference, a 36-character identifier that stays with the payment for its whole journey and lets every bank track it.

The chain. The ordering bank sends the pacs.008 to the next bank in the chain. That bank may be the beneficiary bank itself, an intermediary bank, or a correspondent bank. Each bank reads the message, screens it against its own sanctions and compliance rules, and forwards it. The last bank in the chain credits the beneficiary account.

The money. The instruction and the money travel separately. The instruction moves over SWIFT in seconds. The money moves by settlement: the ordering bank pays from its nostro account with the correspondent bank, and each bank in the chain settles with the next. The beneficiary bank credits the beneficiary only when it has received the funds or has a firm claim on them. This is why a payment can be "sent" on SWIFT but not yet received by the beneficiary.

The methods. Two methods move the funds behind the instruction. In the serial method, the pacs.008 travels bank by bank and the funds move with it. In the cover method, the pacs.008 goes directly to the beneficiary bank while a separate pacs.009 moves the funds through the correspondent chain. The cover method is faster, but the pacs.009 must carry the full customer details so every bank in the chain can screen the payment.

Tracking. SWIFT gpi, the Global Payments Innovation service, lets banks track a cross-border payment end to end. Each bank in the chain sends a pacs.002 status report as the payment progresses: accepted, accepted for settlement, credited, or rejected. The ordering bank can see where the payment is and what charges were deducted. A customer asking "where is my payment" is answered from the gpi tracker, not from a phone call to the correspondent bank.

Charges. The charge option decides who pays. DEBT (the old MT103 OUR) means the sender pays all charges. CRED (BEN) means the beneficiary pays. SHAR (SHA) means each side pays its own bank. The option is agreed with the customer before the payment is sent, and it decides how much the beneficiary actually receives.

Worked example

A UK manufacturer pays a supplier in Japan. The manufacturer, Harrow Engineering Ltd, owes JPY 8,000,000 for invoice INV-2026-2044. Harrow instructs its bank, a large UK bank, to pay the supplier, Kyoto Precision Co, which banks with a Japanese bank.

Harrow instructs the payment at 10:00 on a Tuesday through its corporate banking portal. The UK bank validates the instruction, runs sanctions screening, debits Harrow's GBP account, and converts the amount to JPY. It builds a pacs.008 with the debtor (Harrow Engineering Ltd), the creditor (Kyoto Precision Co), the instructed amount JPY 8,000,000, the charge option SHAR, and the invoice reference in the remittance information. The UETR is assigned and recorded.

The UK bank has no direct relationship with the Japanese bank, so it sends the pacs.008 through a correspondent bank in Tokyo. The correspondent bank receives the pacs.008, screens it, and forwards it to the Japanese bank. The funds settle through the nostro accounts: the UK bank's JPY nostro account with the correspondent bank is debited, and the correspondent bank settles with the Japanese bank.

The Japanese bank receives the pacs.008, checks the beneficiary account, and credits JPY 8,000,000 to Kyoto Precision Co. It sends a pacs.002 with status ACCC, accepted and credited. The UK bank sees the status on the gpi tracker and tells Harrow the payment has arrived. Total time: the payment was instructed on Tuesday morning and credited on Wednesday, because of the time zone difference, the correspondent chain, and the value date. The supplier received the full JPY 8,000,000 because the charge option was SHAR and each bank took its own charge from its own customer, not from the payment amount.

Technical detail

Message types. The main SWIFT payment messages are pacs.008 (customer credit transfer), pacs.009 (bank to bank credit transfer, including the cover payment), pacs.002 (status report), pacs.004 (return), camt.056 (cancellation request), and camt.029 (resolution of investigation). The older MT equivalents are MT103, MT202, MT202 COV, MT199, and MT192.

The UETR. The Unique End-to-end Transaction Reference is a 36-character UUID carried in the pacs.008. It is assigned by the ordering bank and never changes. Every bank in the chain includes it in its status reports, so the whole journey can be stitched together. A payment without a UETR cannot be tracked on gpi.

gpi status codes. The pacs.002 status report uses codes: ACSP (accepted, settlement in progress), ACSC (accepted, settlement completed), ACCC (accepted, settlement completed and credited to the beneficiary), PDNG (pending), and RJCT (rejected). A payment that shows ACSC has settled between banks but may not yet be credited to the beneficiary. Only ACCC confirms the beneficiary account was credited.

Cut-offs and value dates. Each bank has a cut-off time for each currency and corridor. A payment instructed after the cut-off is processed the next business day. The value date is the date the funds should be available to the beneficiary, and it depends on the cut-off, the time zone, and the business days in both countries. A payment instructed on a Friday in London for a beneficiary in Tokyo may not arrive until Monday or Tuesday.

Nostro funding. A bank can only send a SWIFT payment in a currency if it has enough funds in its nostro account for that currency. The treasury team manages nostro balances and liquidity. A payment can be delayed because the nostro account is short, not because of any problem with the message.

Compliance holds. Every bank in the chain screens the payment against sanctions lists. A name match, a high-risk country, or a missing field can hold the payment for manual review. The hold can last hours or days. The ordering bank is told the payment is pending, not why it is held, because banks do not share screening details with each other.

Common mistakes

Treating SWIFT as a settlement system. SWIFT carries instructions, not money. Saying "the money is in SWIFT" is wrong. The money is in nostro accounts and local clearing systems. A payment that is stuck is usually stuck in settlement, compliance, or a correspondent bank, not in SWIFT.

Treating an MT103 or pacs.008 copy as proof of payment. A copy of the message proves the instruction was sent. It does not prove the beneficiary received the money. Proof of receipt is the beneficiary bank's credit confirmation, a pacs.002 with status ACCC, or the beneficiary's own bank statement. Customers and suppliers often argue over this, and the distinction matters.

Ignoring cut-offs and time zones. A payment instructed at 16:00 in London for a beneficiary in Sydney will not arrive the same day. The value date and the cut-off decide the arrival, not the speed of the message.

Using the wrong charge option. If the customer agreed to DEBT and the message says SHAR, the beneficiary receives less than expected and complains. Confirm the charge option with the customer and check it in the message before sending.

Sending a payment without a UETR. Every pacs.008 must carry a UETR. Without it, the payment cannot be tracked, and a customer asking where their money is cannot be answered.

Forgetting the correspondent chain. The ordering bank does not control the intermediary banks. A payment can be delayed or charged by a bank the ordering bank never deals with directly. The chain, not just the two endpoint banks, decides the timing and the cost.
