UPI Explained

What it is

UPI (Unified Payments Interface) is India's real time payment system for person to person and person to merchant payments. It is operated by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI). It lets anyone with a bank account and a mobile phone send and receive money instantly, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, using a mobile app.

UPI works through a Virtual Payment Address (VPA), such as name@bankname, so people do not need to share their account number and IFSC code. It also works with QR codes, phone numbers, and account numbers. The standard transaction limit is 1 lakh rupees per transaction, although higher limits apply for certain categories such as capital markets, insurance, and tax payments.

UPI is the largest real time payment system in the world by volume, processing billions of transactions every month. It runs on top of the IMPS (Immediate Payment Service) rails for settlement between banks.

Why it matters

UPI changed how India pays. Before UPI, sending money to another person meant a bank transfer that took hours, or cash. Now a street vendor, a taxi driver, and a large retailer all accept the same instant payment. UPI is the model that other countries study when they build their own instant payment systems.

For a business analyst, UPI matters because it shows how a well designed instant payment system can scale to billions of transactions, and because its architecture is different from the card schemes and the bank transfer systems used in Europe and the US. Understanding UPI is essential for anyone working on payments in India or on instant payments globally.

How it works in a bank

The parties. A UPI transaction involves the payer, the payee, the payer's bank (remitter bank), the payee's bank (beneficiary bank), the payer's payment service provider app (PSP, such as Google Pay, PhonePe, or Paytm), and NPCI as the central switch.

The payment flow. A customer opens their UPI app and enters the payee's VPA, or scans a QR code. The app sends the instruction to the payer's bank through NPCI. NPCI routes the instruction to the payee's bank. The payee's bank validates the account and credits the payee. The payer's bank debits the payer. The customer sees a confirmation in seconds. The whole flow is real time.

Settlement. Settlement between banks happens through IMPS, with net settlement cycles run by NPCI and the Reserve Bank of India. The customer experience is instant, but the interbank settlement follows the IMPS settlement cycle. Banks manage their settlement positions and liquidity accordingly.

Two factor authentication. Every UPI transaction requires two factor authentication. The customer enters their UPI PIN, which is a 4 or 6 digit number set by the customer. This is the second factor, alongside the registered mobile device. Without the correct PIN, the transaction cannot proceed.

Collect requests. UPI also supports collect requests, where a merchant or a person can request money from another person. The payer receives a request on their app and approves or rejects it. This is used for bill payments and for merchant collections.

Worked example

A customer in Bangalore buys groceries worth 850 rupees from a local shop.

The shop has a QR code at the counter. The customer opens their UPI app, scans the code, and sees the shop's name and the amount. The customer enters their UPI PIN. The app sends the instruction to the customer's bank through NPCI. NPCI routes it to the shop's bank. The shop's bank credits the shop's account. The customer's bank debits the customer. Both the customer and the shopkeeper receive a confirmation within seconds.

A second example. A person in Mumbai sends 25,000 rupees to their parents in Delhi. They open their UPI app, select their parent's contact, enter the amount, and enter their UPI PIN. The money arrives in their parent's account within seconds. The standard limit of 1 lakh rupees per transaction is not a problem for this amount.

A failure example. A customer tries to send 50,000 rupees, but their bank's system is temporarily slow. The transaction times out. The customer does not know whether the money was sent. The bank must check the status with NPCI before telling the customer what happened. If the payment did not settle, the bank reverses the debit. If it did settle, the bank confirms success. The bank must never blindly retry, because a retry could create a duplicate payment.

Technical detail

The VPA. A Virtual Payment Address is an identifier like name@okhdfcbank or 9876543210@okicici. It maps to a bank account. The customer does not need to share their account number. The VPA is resolved by NPCI to the correct bank and account.

NPCI's role. NPCI operates the central switch that routes UPI transactions between banks. It also sets the rules, the limits, and the dispute resolution process. NPCI is a not for profit company promoted by the Reserve Bank of India and the Indian Banks' Association.

IMPS. UPI transactions are settled through IMPS, which is the Reserve Bank's immediate payment service. IMPS provides the interbank settlement. UPI provides the customer facing interface and the addressing (VPA, QR codes, phone numbers).

Limits. The standard UPI transaction limit is 1 lakh rupees per transaction. Higher limits apply for specific categories: for example, capital markets, insurance, and government tax payments have higher limits. Banks may also set their own lower limits for their customers. There is also a limit on the number of transactions per day, which varies by bank.

Dispute handling. If a transaction fails but the customer's account is debited, the bank must reverse the debit. The Reserve Bank of India has set timelines for this. If the bank does not reverse it in time, the customer is entitled to compensation. This is why banks invest heavily in their UPI reconciliation and exception handling.

Common mistakes

Calling UPI a wallet. It is not. UPI moves money directly between bank accounts. A wallet holds stored value. The two are different, and the regulatory treatment is different.

Confusing UPI with IMPS. UPI is the customer facing interface and addressing layer. IMPS is the settlement rail underneath. A business analyst should be able to explain both and how they connect.

Forgetting the PIN. Every UPI transaction needs the customer's UPI PIN. A design that skips this step is not compliant. The PIN is the second factor of authentication.

Ignoring the timeout problem. A UPI transaction can time out, leaving the status unknown. The bank must investigate before retrying or reversing. A blind retry creates a duplicate payment, which is one of the most common UPI complaints.

Assuming the limit is the same for everyone. The standard limit is 1 lakh rupees, but banks set their own limits and certain categories have higher limits. A business analyst must check the specific limit that applies to the customer and the transaction type.
