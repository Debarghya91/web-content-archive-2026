In this chapter

1 Trade Finance Basics: Why Exporters and Importers Need BanksBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain trade finance basics: why exporters and importers need banks. An exporter and an importer face opposite risks, and that tension is the heart of trade finance. A business analyst should ask which side bears the risk in each method, because that answer drives the whole design. 3:12Completed ›
2 Documentary Credit: Definition, Main Actors and How It WorksBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain documentary credit: definition, main actors and how it works. A documentary credit is an undertaking by a bank, the issuing bank, to pay a named beneficiary, usually the exporter, when the beneficiary presents documents that comply with the credit terms. If the documents comply, the bank must pay even if the importer later claims the goods are faulty, and if they do not comply, the bank must refuse even if the goods are perfect. 3:28Completed ›
3 Documentary Credit with a Confirming BankBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain documentary credit with a confirming bank. A confirming bank is a bank, usually in the exporter country, that adds its own independent undertaking to pay the beneficiary when complying documents are presented. If the documents do not comply, the confirming bank must refuse them, just as any nominated bank would, and it must tell the presenter why, listing each discrepancy. 3:25Completed ›
4 Documentary Credit with a Notifying Bank, and Documentary CollectionBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain documentary credit with a notifying bank. A notifying bank is a bank that tells the beneficiary that a documentary credit has been issued, without taking on the advising role in the usual sense. For a business analyst, the distinction matters for the message flow, because the credit may pass through a notifying bank before it reaches the advising bank, and each hop must be recorded with its date and its role. 3:19Completed ›
5 Simple Collection, Payment Against Invoice and Cash on DeliveryBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain simple collection, payment against invoice and cash on delivery. A simple collection, also called a clean collection, is a collection that involves only a financial document, such as a bill of exchange or a cheque, with no shipping documents attached. Choosing between these methods is a commercial decision, and a business analyst should frame it as a risk and cost trade off. 2:55Completed ›
6 Bills of Exchange and Promissory Notes: Parties and the Four-Corner ModelBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain bills of exchange and promissory notes: parties and the four-corner model. A bill of exchange is a written, unconditional order by one person, the drawer, telling another person, the drawee, to pay a fixed sum to a named person, the payee, on a set date or on demand. This matters because cross-border payments involve more banks, more charges, more time and more ways to fail than any domestic payment. Getting the chain right is the job. 3:20Completed ›
7 Worked Example: A EUR 180,000 Machinery Export and a EUR 12,000 Spare Parts CollectionBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain worked example: a EUR 180,000 machinery export and a EUR 12,000 spare parts collection. Harbour Bank in Hamburg acts for the exporter, a machinery maker called Rheinwerk GmbH, and Meridian Bank in Warsaw acts for the importer, a factory called PolTech Sp. z o.o. A business analyst should note that the trade system must show both deals in one customer view, with the credit status, the bill maturity date, and the expected cash date for each. 3:22Completed ›
