Learning objective. Learner explains clearing, bilateral netting, multilateral netting, and the role of a clearing house, and calculates net settlement positions from a set of gross payment obligations. Learner demonstrates this skill using realistic banking examples, explaining each step clearly and using correct terminology throughout.

Practical assessment. Learner receives gross obligations between four banks totalling 2,400,000 dollars and calculates the bilateral net positions, then the multilateral net position for each bank through a clearing house. Learner then explains the liquidity saved by netting, showing the gross versus net totals, and states what happens when one bank cannot meet its net debit. Learner submits the work with all calculations, sources, and assumptions stated clearly, and checks the result against the chapter checklist before final submission.

Expected deliverables.

  • Gross obligation matrix
  • Bilateral netting calculation sheet
  • Multilateral netting calculation sheet
  • Liquidity saving summary with figures

Completion criterion. Learner calculates both netting results correctly, quantifies the liquidity saved, and explains the default consequence for a bank missing its net debit. Completion is judged on accuracy, completeness, and clear reasoning throughout the submitted work.

In this chapter

1 What Clearing Really MeansBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain clearing really means. Clearing is the process that sits between a payment instruction and its final settlement. For a business analyst, understanding clearing means understanding where in the flow a payment can be stopped, corrected or returned, and what information is needed at each step to keep the process reliable and auditable. 4:21Completed ›
2 From Gross Payments to Net PositionsBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain from gross payments to net positions. Every payment has a gross value, which is the full amount instructed by the payer. By netting first, a bank needs a much smaller balance, and the cost of holding that balance falls. 4:20Completed ›
3 Bilateral Netting in PracticeBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain bilateral netting in practice. Bilateral netting is the simplest form of netting, and it involves just two parties. An analyst turns this into three artefacts. 3:45Completed ›
4 Multilateral Netting and the Clearing HouseBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain multilateral netting and the clearing house. Multilateral netting extends the same idea across many participants at once. A clearing house coordinates, while a central counterparty guarantees, and an analyst should not confuse the two. 4:25Completed ›
5 A Worked Example with Three BanksBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain worked example with three banks. Consider three banks, A, B and C, exchanging payments during one clearing cycle. Operations teams monitor this closely, because a sudden change in net position changes the liquidity a bank must have ready at settlement. 5:14Completed ›
6 Clearing Houses and Central CounterpartiesBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain clearing houses and central counterparties. A clearing house is more than a calculator. For a business analyst, the distinction matters when designing system requirements. 3:46Completed ›
7 Why Netting Matters for Risk and LiquidityBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain netting matters for risk and liquidity. Netting reduces the amount of money that must move at settlement, and that directly reduces both liquidity risk and settlement risk. There is a trade off that analysts must understand. 3:23Completed ›
8 System design deep dive: clearing house architectureBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain system design deep dive: clearing house architecture. A clearing house processes millions of instructions every day, often 10 to 50 million in a large domestic system, with peak rates of 5,000 to 10,000 instructions per second. This reduces the liquidity each bank needs by 90 percent or more in a typical cycle. 3:29Completed ›
9 Hybrid payment systemsBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain hybrid payment systems. A hybrid payment system settles some payments the gross way and others the net way inside a single system. An analyst should never assume that a payment in a hybrid system is final merely because it was accepted. 6:56Completed ›
10 RTFS and RTNS settlement modelsBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain rtfs and rtns settlement models. RTFS and RTNS are two labels for two different answers to the same question, namely when a payment that is processed in real time actually becomes final. For an analyst, the working question about any system is therefore not how fast it looks, but when finality happens, and the answer decides how credit, customer funds and failure handling must all be managed. 4:57Completed ›
11 Bilateral and multilateral limits in depthBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain bilateral and multilateral limits. Netting and clearing systems let participants exchange payments through the day and settle the net amounts later. This matters because clearing and settlement are where money actually moves between banks. A mistake here is a settlement failure, a liquidity shortfall, or a loss. 3:54Completed ›
