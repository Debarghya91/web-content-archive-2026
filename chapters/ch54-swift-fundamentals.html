In this chapter

1 What SWIFT Is and What It Is NotBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain SWIFT. SWIFT is a member owned cooperative based in Belgium that provides secure messaging services to more than 11,000 financial institutions in over 200 countries and territories. Analysts who understand this boundary can trace a delayed payment correctly, ask the right bank the right question, and avoid blaming the messaging network for a liquidity or compliance problem that belongs to a bank. 2:58Completed ›
2 SWIFTNet: How Banks ConnectBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain swiftnet: how banks connect. SWIFTNet is a private, closed network that connects member institutions to SWIFT services. This matters because the message is the payment instruction. A wrong field, a missing element or a misunderstood format is a failed payment, a return, or a compliance breach. 4:14Completed ›
3 SWIFT Message Types, Groups and CategoriesBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain SWIFT message types, groups and categories. SWIFT message types are three digit numbers, and the first digit is the category. Category 1 is the most familiar to payment operations teams. 4:00Completed ›
4 The BIC Code: ISO 9362 and How a BIC Identifies an EntityBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain BIC code: ISO 9362 and how a BIC identifies an entity. A BIC, or Business Identifier Code, is defined by the ISO 9362 standard and identifies a financial institution or a branch of that institution. A payment sent to DEUTDEFFXXX will be handled by the head office, while a payment sent to DEUTDEFF500 will be handled by that specific branch, which may have its own accounts and operations team. 3:27Completed ›
5 How a BIC Routes a Message Across SWIFTNetBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain BIC routes a message across swiftnet. Routing begins when the sender enters the receiver BIC in the payment system. That acknowledgement is the evidence operations teams use when a customer asks whether a payment was sent. 3:03Completed ›
6 FIN, InterAct and FileActBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain fin, interact and fileact. FIN is the original SWIFT service, and it carries MT messages such as MT103, MT202 and MT950 in a store and forward model. Operations teams must know which service carried each leg, because the investigation path is different for a FIN message, an InterAct exchange, and a FileAct transfer. 3:19Completed ›
7 Worked Example: Follow One Message from a Bank in Paris to a Bank in SingaporeBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain worked example: follow one message from a bank in paris to a bank in singapore. This worked example is set before the CBPR+ migration, so the payment travels as an MT103. Its operations team screens the ordering customer and the beneficiary against sanctions lists, checks the account number 003912345678, and confirms that the value date of 5 November 2024 has been met. 4:20Completed ›
8 RMA: The Authorisation That Lets Banks Exchange MessagesBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain rma: the authorisation that lets banks exchange messages. RMA, the Relationship Management Application, is the authorisation that decides whether one bank may send a SWIFT message to another bank. Harbour Bank might authorise Meridian Bank to send customer payment messages only, while Meridian Bank must grant its own separate authorisation for messages in return. 3:56Completed ›
9 ACK and NACK and What Network Validation ChecksBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain ack and nack and what network validation checks. ACK and NACK are the short answers SWIFT sends back after a bank transmits a message, and together they tell the sending bank whether the message entered the network. Operations teams monitor ACK and NACK traffic as a routine control over every message the bank sends, because an unmatched message is a payment whose fate is unknown. 4:12Completed ›
10 FINplus: the Channel That Carries ISO 20022 on SWIFTNetBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain finplus: the channel that carries ISO 20022 on swiftnet. The FINplus service is the SWIFTNet channel that carries ISO 20022 messages between banks, and it is the successor to FIN. The bank must send the right format to the right counterparty, because a counterparty that is ready for ISO 20022 receives FINplus traffic while a counterparty still on MT receives FIN traffic. 4:26Completed ›
11 Payment Controls: the SWIFT service that screens payments in flightBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain payment controls: the SWIFT service that screens payments in flight. A fraudulent payment that has left the bank is hard to recover, so the best place to stop it is while it is still in flight, between the back office and the network. The analyst must design the alert handling process with the same care as the screening rules, because a screening service that nobody watches is worse than no service: it creates delay without creating safety. 4:32Completed ›
12 UAK and UNK: how SWIFT knows a message was deliveredBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain uak and unk: how SWIFT knows a message was delivered. Sending a SWIFT message is not the same as delivering it. Operations teams watch these alongside the ACK, NAK, UAK, and UNK flow, because a message can be ACKed by the network and still never reach the receiver: it can sit undelivered, it can be aborted, or the receiver interface can fail to acknowledge it. 3:59Completed ›
13 SWIFT Browse: the interactive channel of SWIFTNetBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain SWIFT browse: the interactive channel of swiftnet. SWIFTNet, the network that carries SWIFT traffic, offers three service families, and only two of them move messages between applications. For a business analyst, the practical point is to ask, for every SWIFT service in scope, which channel it uses. 3:49Completed ›
14 Routing restrictions: controlling where SWIFT messages can goBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain routing restrictions: controlling where SWIFT messages can go. A SWIFT message does not go everywhere by default. A business analyst who understands routing restrictions can diagnose a rejected message in minutes; one who does not will chase the message format while the real problem sits in an authorisation table. 3:40Completed ›
15 Message user groups in depth: how communities agree on message useBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain message user groups in depth: how communities agree on message use. A message standard is a skeleton. For a business analyst, the working rule is simple: never implement a message, implement a guideline. 4:07Completed ›
16 SCORE, MA-CUG and TRCO: how corporates connect to SWIFTBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain score, ma-cug and trco: how corporates connect to SWIFT. Corporates connect to SWIFT through closed user groups, and three models dominate. For a business analyst, the CUG model matters at onboarding and at troubleshooting. 3:40Completed ›
17 SNL and the Alliance products: the software that connects a bank to SWIFTBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain snl and the alliance products: the software that connects a bank to SWIFT. A bank does not connect to SWIFTNet by plugging a cable into the wall. A business analyst specifying a SWIFT integration must specify the interface behaviour alongside the message behaviour: the queue, the routing, the validation, and the failure handling. 3:41Completed ›
18 SWIFT Y-copy: when a third party gets a copy of the messageBy the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain SWIFT y-copy: when a third party gets a copy of the message. Sometimes a payment message needs a third reader. A central bank needs to see the flows in its currency, for oversight and liquidity management. 3:41Completed ›
