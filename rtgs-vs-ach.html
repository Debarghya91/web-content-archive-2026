RTGS vs ACH: How These Payment Systems Differ

RTGS and ACH are the two main ways banks move money domestically. RTGS settles each payment individually, in real time, with immediate finality. ACH groups payments into batches and settles the net amounts later. Both are essential, and they exist side by side because they solve different problems: RTGS is for payments where timing and certainty matter most, ACH is for high-volume, low-value payments where cost matters most.

Last updated: 19 November 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • RTGS settles each payment individually, in real time, with immediate finality. ACH collects payments into batches and settles the net amounts later.
  • Use RTGS for high-value or time-critical payments. Use ACH for bulk, low-value, recurring payments where cost matters most.
  • In India, RTGS needs a minimum of Rs 2 lakh with no upper limit and runs 24x7. India's ACH equivalent is NACH, for bulk and recurring payments; NEFT is the batch system for routine transfers.
  • A payment that is cleared is not necessarily settled. Until settlement completes, the beneficiary has not truly been paid.

What Is RTGS?

RTGS stands for Real-Time Gross Settlement. It is the system central banks run for high-value, time-critical payments. Real-time means settlement happens continuously through the day, as each payment arrives. Gross means each payment settles individually, not netted against other payments. And settlement happens in central bank money, which makes it final and irrevocable the moment it completes.

Because every payment settles on its own, RTGS needs liquidity: the sending bank must hold enough central bank funds to cover each payment as it goes out. That is why RTGS is used for large payments and not for everyday retail transfers. Well-known RTGS systems include CHAPS in the UK, Fedwire in the United States, and T2 in the Eurosystem. Read more in the CHAPS guide.

What Is ACH?

ACH stands for Automated Clearing House. It is a batch-based system for routine, lower-value payments. Instead of settling each payment as it arrives, the ACH collects instructions through the day, sorts them into batches, and settles the net amounts between banks at set times. A bank that owes another bank money across thousands of individual payments only settles the net difference, which is far more efficient than settling each payment separately.

The trade-off is time. An ACH payment typically takes hours to days to reach the beneficiary, depending on the clearing cycles, and settlement is deferred rather than immediate. ACH is the workhorse of payroll, bill payments, direct debits and supplier payments. Examples include Nacha ACH in the United States, SEPA Credit Transfer in Europe, and BACS in the UK. Note that ACH is not one universal system: each country runs its own ACH with its own rules, cycles and limits.

Real-Time Gross Settlement Explained

In an RTGS system, a payment instruction goes from the sending bank to the central bank, which debits the sender's central bank account and credits the receiver's central bank account immediately. There is no waiting for a batch, no netting, and no settlement risk after the fact: once the central bank has moved the money, the payment is final. The receiving bank can then credit its customer with confidence.

The cost of that certainty is liquidity and operating hours. A bank must have the funds available at the moment the payment is sent, and RTGS systems run during defined hours, so a payment sent outside those hours waits until the system opens. RTGS systems also differ in operating hours and liquidity arrangements from one country to another.

ACH Batch Processing Explained

In an ACH, payment instructions are collected, validated and sorted into batches. At each clearing cycle, the batches are exchanged between banks, and the net amounts owed between each pair of banks are calculated. Only those net amounts are settled, usually through the central bank. A bank that sent 10,000 salary payments and received 9,500 payments in return settles only the difference.

Because settlement is deferred, ACH carries settlement risk: if a bank cannot meet its net obligation at settlement time, the system and other participants are exposed. That is why ACH systems set value limits and operating rules, and why high-value payments are kept out of the ACH and sent through RTGS instead. Same-day ACH exists in several countries, but it is still batch-based: it shortens the cycle, it does not make settlement real-time.

Clearing vs Settlement

Clearing and settlement are two different steps, and confusing them is the most common mistake in payments. Clearing is the exchange and sorting of payment instructions: working out who owes whom, and how much. Settlement is the actual movement of funds between banks' accounts, usually at the central bank. In RTGS, clearing and settlement happen together, payment by payment. In ACH, clearing happens through the day and settlement happens later, in batches. A payment can be cleared but not yet settled, and until it settles, the beneficiary has not truly been paid. Read the full explanation in Clearing vs Settlement.

Domestic and Cross-Border Payment Examples

Example 1: a corporate payroll batch. A company pays 4,000 employees on the last working day of the month. Each salary is a low-value, routine payment, and the exact second it arrives does not matter. The company's bank sends the batch through the ACH. The payments are cleared in batches and settle at the next cycle, and the employees see the money in their accounts the following morning. Sending 4,000 individual RTGS payments would cost far more and tie up liquidity for no benefit.

Example 2: a high-value treasury payment. The same company's treasury needs to move EUR 12 million to a supplier before a 2 PM cut-off, and the contract requires the funds to be final the same day. The payment goes through RTGS: the bank checks its central bank balance, sends the instruction, and the payment settles individually within minutes, final and irrevocable. The supplier's bank can release goods against it immediately.

RTGS and ACH in Different Countries

Every country runs its own versions, and the details differ. In the United States, Fedwire is the RTGS system for high-value same-day payments, and Nacha ACH handles batch payments including direct deposits and bill payments. In the UK, CHAPS is the RTGS system for high-value payments such as house purchases, BACS is the ACH for payroll and direct debits on a three-day cycle, and Faster Payments is a near-instant retail system that is neither RTGS nor ACH: it credits the beneficiary in seconds but settles between banks on a deferred net basis. In the Eurosystem, T2 is the RTGS system and SEPA Credit Transfer is the batch-based ACH equivalent, with SEPA Instant available for real-time retail payments. A BA must always check the specific system's rules rather than assuming one country's arrangement applies everywhere.

RTGS and ACH in India

India runs both models. RTGS, operated by the Reserve Bank of India, settles high-value payments individually in real time, with a minimum of Rs 2 lakh and no upper limit, and it has run 24 hours a day, 7 days a week since 14 December 2020. NEFT is India's batch-based system for routine transfers: it settles in hourly batches, has no minimum or maximum amount, and also runs 24x7. NACH is India's ACH equivalent: it handles bulk and recurring payments such as salaries, EMIs, dividends and utility bills, settling the net amounts in batches. UPI and IMPS sit alongside them for instant retail payments: IMPS settles instantly, 24x7, for transfers up to Rs 5 lakh, and UPI is the instant, mobile-first system for person-to-person and person-to-merchant payments, typically up to Rs 1 lakh per transaction.

The choice in India follows the same logic as everywhere else, with Indian numbers. A property payment of Rs 50 lakh goes through RTGS: it is above the Rs 2 lakh minimum, it must be final today, and the cost is worth the certainty. A monthly salary run for 4,000 employees goes through NACH: bulk, low-value, recurring, and cheap per payment. A vendor payment of Rs 50,000 goes through NEFT: routine, no minimum, settled within a couple of hours. A transfer to a friend goes through UPI or IMPS: instant, mobile, small value. When Do Banks Use RTGS or ACH?

The choice comes down to four questions. How large is the payment? High-value payments go through RTGS. How urgent is it? A payment that must be final today goes through RTGS. How much will it cost? Bulk low-value payments go through ACH. What does the customer expect? A salary must arrive reliably and cheaply through ACH; a house purchase must settle with certainty through RTGS. Banks encode these decisions as business rules in their payment engines: value thresholds, cut-off times, routing rules and charge structures that a BA documents and tests.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Faster Payments the same as RTGS? No. Faster Payments credits the beneficiary in seconds, but banks settle the net positions between themselves later, so it is not gross settlement in central bank money. It is a near-instant retail system, not an RTGS system.

Is SEPA Instant the same as ACH? No. SEPA Credit Transfer is batch-based clearing, while SEPA Instant settles in seconds. Both run in Europe, for different needs.

Can an ACH payment be reversed? Within limits, yes: ACH payments can be returned or recalled under the scheme rules during the clearing window. An RTGS payment, once settled, is final and cannot be reversed through the settlement system; recovery has to happen through a separate claim.

Why do RTGS payments cost more? Because each payment consumes central bank liquidity and is processed individually in real time. ACH spreads the cost of one batch settlement across thousands of payments.

Is RTGS available 24x7 in India? Yes. RTGS in India has run 24 hours a day, 7 days a week since 14 December 2020. NEFT also runs 24x7.

What is the minimum amount for RTGS in India? Rs 2 lakh, with no upper limit. A payment below Rs 2 lakh cannot go through RTGS; it goes through NEFT, IMPS or UPI instead.

What is India's ACH? NACH, the National Automated Clearing House, is India's batch system for bulk and recurring payments such as salaries, EMIs and dividends. NEFT is the batch system for routine one-off transfers.

RTGS vs NEFT: which should I use? RTGS for high-value, time-critical payments above Rs 2 lakh that must be final immediately. NEFT for routine transfers of any amount that can wait a couple of hours. The full comparison is in the RTGS vs NEFT vs IMPS vs UPI guide.
