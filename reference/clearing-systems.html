Domestic Clearing and Settlement Systems Reference

This page describes the domestic clearing and settlement systems a payments business analyst meets in the United States, the United Kingdom, and the euro area, plus the project that links domestic instant systems across borders. For each system it gives the operator, the settlement model, finality, cut-off behaviour, liquidity needs, and what a BA must document. Settlement model matters more than brand names: a system is either real-time gross settlement, deferred net settlement, or instant settlement in central bank money, and each model creates different liquidity, operational, and failure-mode obligations.

Three settlement models

Real-time gross settlement, or RTGS, settles each payment individually and immediately in central bank money. Finality is immediate, and the sending bank needs the full funds available at the moment of settlement. Deferred net settlement collects payments over a period, nets them, and settles only the net positions, so a bank needs far less liquidity but finality comes later. Instant settlement in central bank money settles each payment immediately, around the clock, from a prefunded account held at the central bank. The model decides the liquidity a bank must hold, the cut-off that applies, and what happens when a payment fails.

Fedwire Funds Service, United States

Fedwire is the US RTGS system for high-value payments, operated by the Federal Reserve Banks. Each payment settles individually in central bank money and is final and irrevocable once settled. There is no netting and no unwinding: once the Federal Reserve has debited the sender and credited the receiver, the payment cannot be reversed through the system.

Operating hours and cut-off behaviour: the Fedwire business day starts in the evening of the previous calendar day, Eastern Time, and runs into the early evening of the business day. The exact opening and closing times and the customer payment cut-off are set in Federal Reserve Operating Circular 6, so a specification should cite that circular rather than a fixed clock time, because the times can change. A payment submitted after the cut-off waits until the next business day, and a payment submitted without funds is queued or rejected depending on the sending bank's arrangement.

Liquidity needs: a bank settles from its Federal Reserve account balance, and may also use intraday credit in the form of a daylight overdraft, which the Federal Reserve prices and caps. A bank that sends large Fedwire volumes must manage its daylight overdraft position through the day, because an overdraft above its cap will block payments.

What a BA must document: the Fedwire message format and field requirements, the cut-off times that apply to the bank's own customers (which are earlier than the system cut-off), the daylight overdraft policy and caps, the queue and rejection handling, and the reconciliation between the Fedwire account statement and the core banking ledger.

CHIPS, United States

CHIPS, the Clearing House Interbank Payments System, is operated by The Clearing House and is the main US system for high-value US dollar payments that do not go through Fedwire, including many cross-border US dollar payments. CHIPS uses netting with prefunding: each participant prefunds a balance in a joint account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and payments are released against that prefunded balance, with multilateral netting applied continuously through the day and final settlement through the prefunded account.

Each participant and each payment is identified by a CHIPS UID, a universal identifier. The UID identifies the participant and, in the payment message, the parties to the payment, and it is the key a BA must get right in mapping customer data to CHIPS messages. A payment with a wrong or missing UID will not route correctly.

Cut-off behaviour: CHIPS operates on a business day that closes in the late afternoon Eastern Time. The exact closing time and the final settlement time are set in the CHIPS rules, so a specification should cite those rules rather than a fixed clock time. Payments that cannot be released because of insufficient prefunding wait in a queue, and the system applies optimisation to release queued payments as funds become available.

Liquidity needs: prefunding. A participant must place funds in the prefunded account before it can send payments, and the amount of prefunding decides how much it can send. This is different from Fedwire, where a bank can use a daylight overdraft. A BA must document the prefunding target, the process for adding prefunding during the day, and what happens when prefunding runs out.

What a BA must document: the CHIPS UID for each participant and counterparty, the prefunding policy and funding process, the queue handling, the cut-off times for the bank's own customers, and the reconciliation of CHIPS messages to the prefunded account.

CHAPS, United Kingdom

CHAPS is the UK RTGS system for high-value sterling payments, operated by the Bank of England. Each payment settles individually in central bank money and is final and irrevocable once settled. CHAPS is used for high-value and time-critical payments, including house purchases and wholesale settlement. The scheme itself sets no value limit on a CHAPS payment, but an individual bank may set its own limits for its customers.

Cut-off behaviour: CHAPS settles during the Bank of England operating day, and the system closes in the early evening UK time. Direct participants apply their own earlier cut-off times for customer payments, and the exact system times are set in the Bank of England CHAPS reference manual, so a specification should cite that manual rather than a fixed clock time.

Liquidity needs: a direct participant settles from its reserves account at the Bank of England and can use the Bank's intraday liquidity facilities against eligible collateral. A bank must manage its reserves through the day, because a CHAPS payment cannot settle without the funds in the account at that moment.

What a BA must document: the CHAPS message format, which is SWIFT MT and MX, the customer cut-off times, the liquidity and collateral arrangements, the handling of a payment that cannot settle, and the reconciliation of CHAPS settlement to the Bank of England statement.

Faster Payments, United Kingdom

Faster Payments, or FPS, is the UK near real-time retail payment system, operated by Pay.UK. A payment is normally credited to the beneficiary within seconds, and the service runs 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Settlement between member banks is deferred net settlement: member positions are netted and settled at the Bank of England in several settlement cycles during the day, rather than payment by payment.

Value limits: the scheme sets a maximum value per payment, which is currently one million pounds. Individual banks set lower limits for their own customers, and the limit that matters to a customer is the bank's limit, not the scheme maximum. A BA must record both figures and say which one applies to the customer in front of them.

Liquidity needs: because settlement is net and deferred, a member needs enough liquidity to cover its net settlement obligation at each settlement cycle, not the gross value of every payment. The member must also prefund or collateralise its net settlement exposure as the scheme requires. The exact number and timing of the net settlement cycles are set by Pay.UK and the Bank of England, so a specification should cite those sources rather than a fixed number of cycles.

What a BA must document: the FPS message format and sort code validation, the per-customer value limits, the handling of a payment that exceeds a limit, the recall and return process, which is different from a CHAPS recall because FPS payments settle in seconds, and the reconciliation of FPS transactions to the net settlement cycles.

TARGET2 / T2, euro area

TARGET2, now replaced by the T2 system, is the Eurosystem RTGS system for euro payments. It is operated by the national central banks of the euro area together with the European Central Bank. Each payment settles individually in central bank money and is final and irrevocable once settled. T2 is the system for high-value euro payments, monetary policy operations, and the settlement of other systems.

Cut-off behaviour: the published cut-off times in the TARGET guideline are 17:00 Central European Time for customer payments and 18:00 Central European Time for interbank payments. Direct participants normally impose earlier cut-off times on their own customers, so the time a customer sees is earlier than the system cut-off. A specification should state both the system cut-off and the bank's own customer cut-off, and name the guideline as the source.

Liquidity needs: a participant settles from its central bank account and can use intraday credit from its national central bank against eligible collateral. Liquidity management in T2 includes queues, priorities, and liquidity reservation, which a BA must understand because a payment can sit in a queue if the account has insufficient funds.

What a BA must document: the T2 message formats and the TARGET directory, the customer and interbank cut-off times, the liquidity reservation and queue handling, the collateral arrangements for intraday credit, and the reconciliation of T2 settlement to the central bank account statement.

TIPS, euro area

TIPS, TARGET Instant Payment Settlement, is the Eurosystem service for instant payments in euro. It settles each payment individually in central bank money, in seconds, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, 365 days a year. It is built for SEPA Instant Credit Transfer payments and settles from dedicated cash accounts, known as TIPS DCAs, which participants fund from their T2 accounts.

There is no cut-off in TIPS, because the service never closes. That is the point of the service, and it changes every operational assumption that a batch or business-day system relies on: liquidity must be available at all hours, screening must happen in real time, and operations and support must be staffed or automated around the clock.

Liquidity needs: prefunding of the TIPS DCA. A participant moves funds from its T2 account to its TIPS DCA, and payments settle only while the DCA holds enough funds. A BA must document the DCA funding process, the target balance, the alerts that fire when the balance runs low, and the process for moving funds back to T2.

What a BA must document: the reachable parties and instructing parties, the DCA funding and monitoring process, the real-time screening arrangement, the handling of a payment that cannot settle because the DCA is empty, and the reconciliation of TIPS transactions to the DCA statement.

Cross-border instant settlement linkage: Project Nexus

Project Nexus is a BIS Innovation Hub project that links domestic instant payment systems so a payment can travel from one country's instant system to another's. It is a design and a set of prototypes, not a live settlement system, and a BA should describe it as such.

The design works like this. Each domestic instant payment system connects once to Nexus through a Nexus Gateway, instead of building a separate connection to every other country's system. A payment is sent in ISO 20022 format through the gateway, foreign exchange and compliance checks are handled by the participating payment service providers, and settlement happens in the domestic instant systems at each end. The benefit is that a new country joins by connecting once, and the number of connections grows with the number of countries rather than with every pair of countries.

What this means for a BA: the questions are the same as for any cross-border payment, but the answers change. Who provides the foreign exchange quote and when is it locked in. Who screens the payment and against which lists. What happens when the receiving instant system rejects the payment. How a return or recall works across two instant systems that each settle in seconds. The answers depend on the participating systems and the service providers involved, so a specification should name the systems and providers it covers rather than describing Nexus as if it were a single finished product.

What changes when a payment moves from batch to instant

Moving a payment from a batch system to an instant one changes five things. First, liquidity: a batch system lets a bank fund one net settlement, while an instant system needs prefunded balances available at all hours, including nights and weekends. Second, cut-off: a batch system has a cut-off and a next business day, while an instant system has no cut-off and no next business day to fall back on. Third, screening: a batch payment can be screened while it waits for the batch, while an instant payment must be screened in real time, in seconds, before it is released. Fourth, exceptions: a batch payment can be stopped before the batch runs, while an instant payment that has settled can only be recovered by a recall or a return, which needs the beneficiary bank and often the beneficiary to cooperate. Fifth, customer expectation: once a customer has received a payment in seconds, a payment that takes hours feels like a failure, even if it is inside the old batch service level. A BA documenting a move to instant payments must cover all five, not just the message format.

What a BA must document for any clearing system

For every system, record the operator, the settlement model, the moment finality is reached, the cut-off times that apply to the bank's own customers, the liquidity the bank must hold and where it is held, the message formats, the failure modes, and the reconciliation source. If any of those is missing, the specification is incomplete.
