Regulatory Compliance Reference

This page covers three regulatory subjects a payments business analyst must be able to explain: the SWIFT CBPR+ programme that governs the move from MT to ISO 20022 for cross-border payments, sanctions screening and what happens when a payment is held, and the Basel intraday liquidity monitoring tools that govern a bank's participation in real-time gross settlement systems. Each section names its source so a reader can check the current position, because all three subjects change as usage guidelines, sanctions lists, and supervisory expectations are updated.

SWIFT CBPR+ baseline rules

CBPR+ is the SWIFT community programme that defines how cross-border payments use ISO 20022. It is not a regulation and it is not a single rulebook: it is a set of usage guidelines, market practice, and migration rules agreed by the SWIFT community, published by SWIFT, and adopted by banks that send cross-border payments over the SWIFT network. A bank that follows CBPR+ can send a pacs.008 that another CBPR+ bank can read, validate, and process straight through. A bank that does not follow it will have payments rejected or repaired by hand.

The migration from MT to MX

Cross-border payments migrated from MT to MX over a coexistence period that ran from November 2022 to November 2025. During coexistence, a bank could send either an MT103 or a pacs.008, and receiving banks had to accept both. At the end of the coexistence period, MT messages for cross-border payments were retired from the SWIFT network for the migrated message types, and ISO 20022 became the standard. Payments that still arrive as MT after the migration must be handled under the receiving bank's own arrangements, and a BA should confirm what those arrangements are rather than assuming an MT payment will still be accepted.

The migration also changed the data a payment must carry. ISO 20022 messages are richer than MT messages: they carry structured party information, structured remittance information, and a unique end-to-end transaction reference, the UETR, which identifies a payment across every bank in the chain and is used for tracking. A payment without a UETR, or with a UETR that does not match the payment, cannot be tracked reliably, so the UETR must be generated once, carried unchanged through the chain, and stored by every bank that handles the payment.

Usage guidelines

The CBPR+ usage guidelines are the binding detail. The ISO 20022 schema defines what is possible; the usage guidelines define what is permitted on the SWIFT network. They state which elements are mandatory, which code values are allowed, which character sets apply, and how MT fields map to MX elements. They also state the version of each message that must be used, for example pacs.008.001.10 for a customer credit transfer. A specification that names only the message and not the usage guideline version is incomplete, because the usage guidelines are updated and the rules change with them.

Structured addresses

CBPR+ requires structured addresses for the parties to a payment. The minimum is the town name and the country, with street name, building number, postcode, and other elements as separate fields where the data is available. During the migration, hybrid addresses were accepted: a structured town name and country combined with unstructured address lines. The CBPR+ roadmap moves from hybrid to fully structured addresses, and the usage guidelines state the date from which hybrid addresses stop being accepted. Check the current usage guidelines for that date before writing it into a specification, because it is set by the programme and can change.

Structured addresses matter for two reasons. First, screening: a sanctions screening engine matches names and addresses, and a structured address gives it clean fields to match against. Second, straight-through processing: a receiving bank can validate a structured address automatically, while an unstructured address line has to be read by a person or parsed by a machine that may get it wrong.

Coexistence period

The coexistence period is the part of the migration most often misunderstood. Coexistence did not mean that MT and MX were equivalent: it meant that both were accepted while banks migrated. During coexistence, a bank that sent MT and a bank that sent MX had to be able to work together, which meant translation between the two formats, with the data loss described on the mapping reference page. After coexistence ended, translation is no longer the normal path, and a bank that still sends MT for a migrated message type is outside the CBPR+ baseline. A BA should record the coexistence dates that applied to each message type, because different message types migrated on different timetables.

CBPR+ in one paragraph

CBPR+ is the SWIFT community programme that tells banks how to use ISO 20022 for cross-border payments: which message versions, which elements are mandatory, which code values are allowed, and how addresses and references are structured. It is published as usage guidelines, it replaced MT for cross-border payments after a coexistence period, and it is the first document to check whenever a cross-border payment fails validation.

Sanctions screening triggers

Sanctions screening is the check a bank runs on a payment against sanctions lists before it releases the payment. The lists that matter most in cross-border payments are the OFAC list published by the US Office of Foreign Assets Control, including the Specially Designated Nationals list; the EU consolidated list of persons, groups, and entities subject to EU financial sanctions; and the United Nations Security Council sanctions lists. A bank must also screen against the lists that apply in every jurisdiction it operates in, and against its own internal watchlists.

How name matching works

Screening is name matching, and name matching is approximate. An exact match on a name is a hit, but screening engines also use fuzzy matching to catch spelling variations, transliteration differences, and name order differences. A name written in a different script, a name with an extra middle name, or a name with a common spelling variation can all produce a match. Screening engines also match on other fields, such as date of birth, nationality, address, and identification numbers, where those are available, to separate a real match from a false positive.

That is why data quality matters. A payment with a misspelled beneficiary name, a missing date of birth, or an unstructured address is more likely to produce a false positive, and a false positive holds the payment while a person investigates it. A payment with a full structured name and address, and identification where the usage guideline requires it, is more likely to pass screening without intervention.

What a hit does

A screening hit does not reject the payment outright. It holds the payment. The payment is stopped in the queue, the screening team is notified, and an investigation starts. The payment stays held while the investigation runs, and it is released only if the investigation concludes that the hit was a false positive. If the investigation concludes that the hit is a real match, the payment is blocked, and the bank follows its sanctions policy and its legal obligations, which can include reporting to the relevant authority and freezing the funds. The difference matters: a held payment can still be released, a rejected payment cannot, and a blocked payment has legal consequences that a rejected payment does not.

The investigation loop

The investigation loop has four steps. First, the screening engine raises an alert with the matched list entry and the fields that matched. Second, an analyst reviews the alert, compares the payment data with the list entry, and decides whether it is a false positive, a possible match that needs more information, or a confirmed match. Third, if more information is needed, the analyst requests it from the customer, the sending bank, or the beneficiary bank. Fourth, the analyst records the decision and the evidence, releases the payment if the hit was a false positive, or escalates it if it was not. Every step must be recorded, because a regulator can ask for the evidence behind a decision to release a payment that produced a hit.

Where camt.029, camt.056, and pacs.002 fit

When a payment is held for screening, the banks in the chain need to talk to each other, and they do it with ISO 20022 messages. A bank that needs information about a payment sends an investigation request, and the bank that answers it sends a camt.029, Resolution of Investigation, with the outcome. A bank that wants a payment cancelled, for example because screening has confirmed a match, sends a camt.056, FI to FI Payment Cancellation Request, and the bank that receives it answers with a camt.029 stating whether the cancellation was accepted or rejected.

A payment that is rejected because of screening is reported with a pacs.002, Payment Status Report, with a rejected status. The pacs.002 carries a status code and a reason code, so the sender knows the payment was rejected and why. The reason codes that matter for screening outcomes are the standard ISO 20022 return and rejection reason codes, and the exact code depends on the usage guideline in force. A BA should record which reason code the bank uses for a sanctions rejection, because the code is what the sender's system reads, not the free-text explanation.

Basel III and IV: intraday liquidity management

Intraday liquidity is the liquidity a bank needs during the day to settle payments, as opposed to the liquidity it needs at the end of the day. A bank can be solvent and well capitalised and still fail to settle a payment at 11 in the morning if it does not have the funds in its central bank account at that moment. That is why the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision published monitoring tools for intraday liquidity management, and why supervisors expect banks to monitor intraday liquidity daily and report on it regularly.

Intraday liquidity matters for RTGS participation because an RTGS system settles each payment individually and immediately. A bank that cannot fund a payment when it is submitted will have the payment queued or rejected, and a queue in one bank can delay payments across the whole system. A bank that manages its intraday liquidity well can settle on time, use its collateral efficiently, and avoid expensive intraday credit. A bank that manages it badly will see payments queue, customers complain, and supervisors ask questions.

The BCBS intraday liquidity monitoring tools

The Basel Committee's monitoring tools for intraday liquidity management are seven measures, published by the Committee and used by supervisors. They are:

ToolWhat it measuresWhy it matters
Daily maximum liquidity needThe largest net cumulative outflow during the day, meaning the most liquidity the bank needed at any point in the dayShows the real size of the bank's intraday need, which is larger than its end-of-day position suggests
Available intraday liquidityThe liquidity available at the start of the business day, including the account balance and intraday credit linesShows whether the bank starts the day with enough to meet its expected need
Total value of paymentsThe total value of payments settled during the dayShows the scale of the bank's payment activity and how it changes over time
Time-specific obligationsThe value of payments that must settle at a specific time, such as CLS pay-ins or ancillary system settlementsShows the payments that cannot be delayed, so the bank must hold liquidity for them at a specific time
Value of payments made on behalf of customersThe value of customer payments the bank settles, as opposed to its own paymentsShows how much of the intraday need comes from customers, whose behaviour the bank cannot control directly
Intraday credit linesThe value of intraday credit lines available to the bank and the value actually usedShows how much the bank relies on intraday credit and whether its lines are large enough
ThroughputThe proportion of payments settled by a given time of day, measured against the total for the dayShows whether the bank is settling payments evenly through the day or leaving them until late, which creates risk for the whole system

What a bank must report

Banks monitor these tools daily as part of their liquidity management, and report them to their supervisor on a regular basis, normally monthly, in the format the supervisor requires. The report covers the bank's intraday liquidity position, its use of intraday credit, its throughput, and its largest intraday needs. A supervisor uses the report to see whether the bank understands its intraday liquidity, whether it holds enough, and whether its behaviour, such as leaving payments until late in the day, creates risk for other participants. The reporting frequency and the exact format are set by the supervisor, so a specification should name the supervisor and the reporting requirement that applies to the bank, rather than assuming one frequency fits all banks.

Why intraday liquidity matters for RTGS participation

RTGS participation is a promise: a bank that joins an RTGS system promises to settle its payments on time, in central bank money, when they are submitted. That promise can only be kept if the bank manages its intraday liquidity. The tools above exist so a bank can prove it is keeping the promise, and so a supervisor can see when it is not. A BA working on an RTGS project must understand this, because the liquidity arrangements, the queue handling, the collateral, and the throughput targets are all part of the specification, not just the message format.

What a BA must document for compliance

For CBPR+, record the usage guideline version and the message versions. For sanctions screening, record the lists screened against, the matching rules, the hold and release process, and the messages used to communicate with other banks. For intraday liquidity, record the monitoring tools, the reporting requirement, and the liquidity arrangements for each RTGS system the bank uses.
