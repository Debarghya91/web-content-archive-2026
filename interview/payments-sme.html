Payments SME Interview Track

Role expectations

A Payments Subject Matter Expert is the deep domain expert a bank turns to when a payment is complex, stuck, or wrong. The role covers payment operations, exceptions, reconciliation, and the regulatory detail behind domestic and cross border payments. The hiring manager expects the candidate to know how money actually moves, message by message, and to explain it clearly to operations, technology, product, and clients. That means knowing pacs.008, pacs.002, pacs.009, MT103, MT202, camt.053, and pain.001 in real use, knowing how clearing and nostro settlement work, and knowing what to do when a payment is returned, held, or lost in a chain of banks. The SME advises on new products, tests new flows, trains operations teams, and helps fix the hardest cases. Success is measured by faster and cleaner exception handling, fewer repeat errors, and advice that stops problems before they reach a client. The SME also checks that payment data is complete and correct, so reports, audits, and client answers rest on sound facts.

Competency framework

  1. Payment message expertise - Reads and explains ISO 20022 and SWIFT messages, field by field, and knows what each field means in practice.
  2. End to end payment flows - Traces a payment from instruction to settlement across banks, clearing systems, and nostro accounts.
  3. Exception investigation - Finds the cause of a failed, delayed, or returned payment using messages, statements, and tracking data.
  4. Reconciliation and nostro control - Matches payments to statements and nostro accounts, and clears breaks quickly and cleanly.
  5. Clearing and settlement knowledge - Explains how domestic and cross border clearing works, including value dates, cut off times, and settlement risk.
  6. Regulatory and control knowledge - Applies sanctions, anti money laundering, fraud, and reporting rules to real payment cases.
  7. Stakeholder advice - Gives clear, practical advice to operations, technology, product, and clients, in plain language.
  8. Training and knowledge sharing - Turns hard cases into guides, checks, and training that help the whole team work better.

Learning objectives

  • Read a pacs.008, MT103, pacs.002, and camt.053 and explain what each one shows.
  • Trace a cross border payment through each bank in the chain and find where it stopped.
  • Investigate a returned or rejected payment and explain the cause and the fix.
  • Reconcile a nostro account and clear a break using the statement and the payment record.
  • Explain sanctions, fraud, and value date rules in a real payment case.
  • Advise a product or technology team on a payment change, including the operational impact.
  • Handle a complex exception with a client, with clear updates and a clear timeline.
  • Turn a repeat exception into a process fix, a check, or a training note.

Beginner questions

1. A client asks what an MT103 is. How do you explain it in plain words?

Model answer: An MT103 is a SWIFT message that tells one bank to pay another bank for a customer. It is used for a single customer credit transfer across borders. The message holds the payer and the beneficiary, the amount and currency, the value date, and the details the beneficiary needs. It also holds the charges instruction, which says who pays the charges. When a bank sends an MT103, it is asking the next bank in the chain to pass the payment on, or to pay the beneficiary. The MT103 is the old SWIFT format. Under ISO 20022, the same payment travels as a pacs.008. A Payments SME should be able to read an MT103, find the key fields, and explain what the payment is for and where it is going.

2. What does a camt.053 statement show, and how do operations use it each day?

Model answer: A camt.053 is a bank to bank account statement in ISO 20022 format. It shows all the entries on an account for a period, such as a day, with the balance at the start and the balance at the end. Each entry shows the amount, the value date, the reference, and the counterparty where available. Operations use the camt.053 to reconcile the nostro account, which means they match each entry on the statement to a payment the bank expected to send or receive. If an entry has no match, it is a break, and operations must find the cause. If a payment the bank sent does not appear, operations must chase the other bank. The camt.053 is also used to check charges, to confirm settlement, and to spot an unexpected debit or credit on the account.

3. A payment has been returned to the payer. What are the most common reasons, and what should happen next?

Model answer: A payment is often returned because the beneficiary account is closed, the account number is wrong, or the name does not match the account. It can also be returned because the beneficiary bank cannot process the payment, the value date is wrong, or the payment details are missing. When a payment is returned, the bank should receive a pacs.004 return message or an MT199 free format message, with a return reason code. Operations should read the reason code, check the original payment, and tell the payer what went wrong in plain words. The payer should then fix the details and send the payment again, if it is still needed. The bank should also check whether other payments to the same beneficiary are affected, and whether the client needs to update their beneficiary record.

4. What is a value date, and why does it matter to a client?

Model answer: The value date is the date on which the funds are available to the beneficiary, and the date from which interest is counted. It is not always the same as the date the payment was sent. For example, a payment sent on a Friday may have a value date of Monday, if Monday is the next working day. The value date matters because a client may need the funds on a set date, for example to pay a supplier or to meet a contract. If the value date is late, the client may face a penalty or a cash flow problem. A Payments SME should check the value date on the message, compare it with the date the client asked for, and explain any difference. The SME should also know the cut off times, because a payment sent after the cut off will often have a later value date.

5. What is the difference between a serial payment and a cover payment in cross border processing?

Model answer: In a serial payment, the payment instruction and the funds travel together through each bank in the chain. Each bank passes the pacs.008 or MT103 to the next bank, until it reaches the beneficiary bank. In a cover payment, the two parts travel separately. The customer payment instruction goes direct to the beneficiary bank, while the funds move separately through a pacs.009 or MT202 cover payment between banks. The cover method is faster for the instruction, because the beneficiary bank knows the payment is coming before the funds arrive. It also needs careful reconciliation, because the instruction and the cover must be matched. A Payments SME should know which method a payment used, because it affects how the payment is tracked and how a problem is investigated.

Intermediate questions

1. A cross border payment shows as rejected with a pacs.002 status report. How do you investigate it?

Model answer: I would start by reading the pacs.002 in full, not only the headline status. The report holds a status code, a reason code, and often extra text that says why the payment was rejected. I would then read the original pacs.008 and check the fields the reason code points to, such as the beneficiary name, the account number, the currency, or the structured address. I would check whether the payment failed validation, sanctions screening, or a limit at the next bank. I would also check the UETR and the payment reference, so I can trace the same payment in the hub and in core banking. Once I know the cause, I would tell the payer what is wrong in plain words, and what to fix before the payment is sent again. I would also check whether other payments with the same issue are in the queue.

2. A nostro account shows a break of 50,000 US dollars. How do you clear it?

Model answer: I would first confirm the break by comparing the camt.053 statement from the correspondent bank with our own payment record. I would look for a payment of 50,000 US dollars that we expected but cannot find, or an entry on the statement that has no match in our books. The break may be a payment that was sent but not yet posted, a charge we did not expect, a duplicate posting, or a payment that was returned without a clear reference. I would check the pacs.008, pacs.009, and MT202 messages for that day, and speak to the correspondent bank if the entry is unclear. Once I find the cause, I would post the correct entry, raise a query if the other bank made an error, and record the fix so the break does not happen again.

3. A corporate client sends a pain.001 file and 40 payments are rejected. How do you help the client fix the file?

Model answer: I would first read the pain.002 status report, which lists each rejected payment and the reason for the rejection. I would group the rejections by reason, such as an invalid account number, a missing field, a value above a limit, or a wrong currency. I would then check a sample of the rejected payments against the original file, to confirm the cause and to make sure the report is correct. I would speak to the client in plain words, explain the groups of errors, and give a clear example of each one. I would tell the client which payments can be fixed and resent, and which need new beneficiary details. I would also check whether the file format or the client guide needs a clearer note on the field that caused the most errors.

4. A payment is held by sanctions screening. What do you do, and what do you tell the client?

Model answer: I would first check the screening result to see what caused the hold, such as a name match, a country, or a missing detail. I would not release the payment until the check is complete, because the bank must meet its legal duty. I would gather the facts the screening team needs, such as the full payer and beneficiary details, the purpose of the payment, and any supporting documents. I would work with the compliance team to clear the hold or to confirm that the payment must be blocked. I would tell the client that the payment is held for a compliance check, that we are working on it, and when I will update them. I would not share the screening detail with the client, because that could help a person avoid a future check.

5. How do you use SWIFT gpi to track a cross border payment and answer a client query?

Model answer: SWIFT gpi gives each cross border payment a unique UETR reference, which stays with the payment through every bank in the chain. Each bank in the chain sends a tracking update, called a gpi tracker update, when it receives and forwards the payment. To answer a client query, I would take the UETR from the original pacs.008 or MT103, and check the tracker to see which banks have handled the payment and when. I would also check the pacs.002 status reports from each bank, to see if any bank rejected or held the payment. If the tracker shows the payment is with a named bank, I would tell the client the bank name, the time it arrived there, and the next step. If the tracker shows no update, I would raise a query with the next bank in the chain, using the UETR and the payment reference.

Advanced questions

1. A high value cross border payment is missing. The payer bank says it sent the funds, the beneficiary bank says it has not received them. How do you investigate?

Model answer: I would start by confirming the facts on both sides. I would ask the payer bank for the pacs.008 or MT103, the UETR, the value date, and the pacs.009 or MT202 cover payment, if one was used. I would ask the beneficiary bank what it has received, by UETR and by reference, and whether it has a held or unapplied payment. I would check the SWIFT gpi tracker to see each bank in the chain and the time each bank handled the payment. I would also check the nostro accounts and the camt.053 statements, to see whether the funds left one account and arrived in the next. The payment may be held by an intermediary bank for a compliance check, it may have been sent to the wrong account, or the cover and the instruction may not have been matched.

2. A client is charged twice for the same cross border payment, once by the sending bank and once by an intermediary bank. How do you explain and resolve this?

Model answer: I would first check the charges instruction on the original payment. An MT103 or pacs.008 can carry OUR, BEN, or SHA, which says who pays the charges. OUR means the payer pays all charges. BEN means the beneficiary pays all charges. SHA means the payer and the beneficiary share the charges. If the instruction was OUR, the intermediary bank should not have deducted a charge, so I would raise a claim with that bank, using the payment reference and the UETR. I would also check the camt.053 statement to confirm the exact charge and the date. I would explain to the client in plain words what the instruction was, which bank took the extra charge, and what we are doing to recover it. I would also check whether other payments on the same route have the same issue, and whether the route or the instruction needs to change.

3. A payment fails because the beneficiary name does not match the account. How do you handle this across different countries?

Model answer: The rule differs by country, so I would first check the rule for the beneficiary country and bank. In the UK, confirmation of payee checks the name against the account before the payment is sent. In the euro area, the SEPA rule has moved to a similar check, called verification of payee. In other countries, the bank may rely on the account number alone, and a name mismatch may only be found after the payment arrives. I would check the pacs.002 or return message for the exact reason, and speak to the beneficiary bank to confirm the correct name and account. I would tell the payer what to fix, and ask the payer to confirm the details with the beneficiary before resending. I would also check whether the client needs to update their beneficiary record, and whether the payment channel should warn the client when a name does not match.

4. Your bank is moving from MT to ISO 20022 for cross border payments. What operational risks must the SME watch for?

Model answer: The main risk is data loss or data error when an old message is turned into a new one. An MT103 has less space for remittance and address data than a pacs.008, so a mapping may drop detail the beneficiary needs. I would watch the structured address fields, because a missing town or country can cause a rejection or a sanctions hold. I would also watch the remittance information, because a client may need the full invoice detail to reconcile the payment. I would check the return reason codes, because the new codes differ from the old ones and operations must know what each one means. I would test with real client files, check the pacs.002 status reports, and make sure the camt.053 statement still reconciles. I would also train operations on the new fields and the new error messages before the go live date.

5. A corporate client needs to pay suppliers in five currencies every week. What advice would you give on routing, value dates, and reconciliation?

Model answer: I would advise the client to use one file and one value date per currency where possible, so each currency can be tracked and reconciled on its own. I would explain the best route for each currency, such as a local clearing system where the bank has a direct link, and SWIFT with a pacs.008 where it does not. I would explain the cut off time for each currency, because a payment sent after the cut off will have a later value date. I would advise the client to use a unique reference for each payment, and to include the invoice number in the remittance information, so the supplier can match the payment. I would also advise the client to check the pacs.002 status report each day, and to reconcile each payment against the camt.053 statement or the account statement.

Expert questions

1. A payment chain has four banks. The beneficiary receives less than the amount sent, and no bank can explain the difference. How do you solve it?

Model answer: I would rebuild the full chain from the messages and the statements. I would start with the original pacs.008 or MT103, and note the amount, the charges instruction, and the UETR. I would then ask each bank in the chain for its incoming and outgoing message, and for the amount it received and the amount it sent on. I would compare the amounts at each step, and check the camt.053 statements for each nostro account in the chain. The difference may be a charge taken by an intermediary bank, a currency conversion at a poor rate, or a fee that was not shown on the original instruction. I would also check whether the payment used a cover payment, because the cover and the instruction can carry different amounts if they are not matched.

2. A bank faces a regulatory review of its cross border payments. What would you check first, and what evidence would you prepare?

Model answer: I would first check the areas the regulator cares about most, which are sanctions screening, anti money laundering checks, and the quality of payment data. I would sample payments by corridor, client type, and value, and check whether each payment has a full audit trail, from the pain.001 or channel instruction to the pacs.008, the pacs.002 status, and the camt.053 settlement record. I would check whether held payments were reviewed on time, whether blocked payments were handled correctly, and whether the screening rules are up to date. I would also check the nostro reconciliation, to show that breaks are cleared quickly and that no payment is left unaccounted for. I would prepare a clear summary for each sample, with the payment reference, the checks done, and the result, so the regulator can see the control working in practice.

3. A new instant payment system launches in a country where your bank operates. What operational advice would you give before go live?

Model answer: I would advise the team to treat instant payments as a new operational model, not only as a new message type. The bank must be able to process payments 24 hours a day, including weekends, with real time fraud and sanctions checks. Operations must have a clear process for a payment that fails, because there is no batch window to fix it later. The team must agree the value limit, the cut off rules, and what happens when the system is down. The payment hub must be able to send a status update at once, and the client must be able to see whether the payment worked. I would also advise a full test of the exception paths, such as a timeout, a duplicate, a return, and a fraud hold.

4. Two nostro accounts in the same currency show opposite breaks on the same day. What does this tell you, and how do you fix it?

Model answer: Opposite breaks in the same currency often mean a payment was posted to the wrong nostro account, or a payment was sent through one account but expected in another. I would first list the breaks by amount, value date, and reference, and look for a pair that matches. I would check the pacs.009 and MT202 messages for that day, to see which account was used for funding and which account was expected. I would also check the camt.053 statements for both accounts, to confirm the entries. If a payment was posted to the wrong account, I would correct the posting, move the funds to the right account, and record the fix. I would also check whether the routing rule in the payment hub sent the payment to the wrong account, and fix the rule.

5. A client wants to send a payment to a country under new sanctions. How do you advise the client and the bank?

Model answer: I would first check the current sanctions list with the compliance team, to confirm whether the country, the person, or the type of payment is restricted. I would not guess the rule, because sanctions change often and the penalty for a breach is severe. If the payment is not allowed, I would tell the client clearly that the bank cannot send it, and explain the reason in plain words without sharing screening detail. If the payment is allowed with extra checks, I would tell the client what documents are needed, such as the purpose of the payment and the full beneficiary details, and how long the check may take. I would also advise the bank to hold the payment until compliance clears it, and to record the decision with the full audit trail.

Scenario

Business problem

A mid sized corporate client in the UK pays suppliers in the United States, Germany, and Singapore every week. On a Tuesday, the client sends three pain.001 files, one for each currency, with 180 payments in total worth 2.4 million pounds. By Thursday, 22 suppliers have not been paid. The suppliers are chasing the client, the client is chasing the bank, and the bank operations team has a queue of 22 exceptions with no clear pattern. Some payments show as sent, some show as rejected, and some show no status at all. The bank must find each payment, pay the suppliers who are still unpaid, and explain to the client what went wrong.

Information to investigate

The SME should gather the three file references, the value dates, and the list of the 22 unpaid suppliers. The team should check the pain.002 status report for each file, the pacs.008 for each cross border payment, and the pacs.002 status report from each bank in the chain. The team should check the SWIFT gpi tracker by UETR for each payment, to see where each payment stopped. The team should check the nostro accounts and the camt.053 statements for US dollars, euros, and Singapore dollars, to see whether the funds left the bank. The team should also check the sanctions screening queue, the fraud queue, and the clearing records for each currency, to see whether any payments were held or returned.

Questions to ask stakeholders

Ask the client which suppliers are unpaid, what they were told, and whether any supplier details changed recently. Ask operations which of the 22 payments were rejected, which were held, and which have no status. Ask the sanctions screening team whether any of the 22 payments were held, and what is needed to clear them. Ask the correspondent banks in the United States, Germany, and Singapore whether they received the payments, by UETR and reference. Ask the treasury team whether the nostro accounts were funded on time for each currency. Ask the technology team whether the payment hub had a delay or a failure on Tuesday, and whether any status updates were lost.

Possible root causes

The cause may differ by currency, so the team should not look for one single cause. Some payments may have been rejected for invalid beneficiary details after the client changed supplier records. Some may have been held by sanctions screening because a supplier name matched a screening rule. Some may have been delayed because a nostro account was short of funds in one currency. Some may have been sent by the wrong route, such as a cover payment where the instruction and the cover were not matched. Some may have missed a cut off time, so the value date moved to the next working day. Some may have been lost in the chain, with an intermediary bank holding the payment without a clear status update.

Recommended solution

First, sort the 22 payments by status, so the team knows which were rejected, which were held, and which are truly missing. For rejected payments, tell the client the exact reason and the fix needed, then resend once the details are corrected. For held payments, work with compliance to clear them or to confirm they must be blocked, and tell the client the timeline. For missing payments, raise a trace with the next bank in the chain using the UETR, and chase it daily until the payment is found. Pay the suppliers who are still unpaid by the fastest safe route, and confirm each payment with the supplier. Then fix the root causes, such as the supplier record change, the screening rule, or the nostro funding check. Finally, give the client a clear summary by currency, with the cause for each payment and the fix.

Data and technical considerations

The team needs payment level data for all 180 payments, not only the 22 exceptions. Each payment needs a UETR, a reference, a status, and a timestamp at each step, from file receipt to payment hub processing, clearing, and settlement. The pain.002 status report must show the reason for each rejection in plain words. The SWIFT gpi tracker must be checked for each cross border payment, so the team can see the last bank that handled it. The camt.053 statements must be reconciled for each nostro account, so the team can prove whether the funds left the bank. The payment hub must have duplicate detection, so a resend cannot pay a supplier twice. The data must also show the route for each payment, so the team can tell whether it went by serial payment or cover payment.

Failure and recovery paths

If a resend fails, the team should have a manual payment route for the remaining suppliers, with clear approval and control. If a payment is found with an intermediary bank, the team should agree with that bank whether it will be forwarded or returned, and tell the client the new timing. If a payment was sent twice, the team must start a recovery process at once, contact the beneficiary bank, and keep the client informed. If a nostro account runs short, the treasury team should fund it at once and tell operations the new timing for the affected payments. If the client file cannot be fixed in time, the team should agree a priority list of suppliers and pay the most urgent ones first. Every recovery step must be logged, with who approved it and when.

Expected response

A strong candidate will sort the 22 payments by status before acting, and will not resend the whole file. The candidate will use the UETR, the pacs.002 reports, the SWIFT gpi tracker, and the camt.053 statements to find each payment. The candidate will explain the cause for each group in plain words, agree a fix with the client, and set out a plan to stop the same failure next week. The candidate will also show awareness of sanctions holds, nostro funding, cut off times, and the difference between a serial payment and a cover payment. A weak candidate will guess one cause for all 22 payments, resend the file without checking, or focus only on one currency.

Interviewer evaluation criteria

The interviewer should check whether the candidate sorts the exceptions by status and by currency. The interviewer should check whether the candidate can use the UETR, SWIFT gpi, pacs.002, and camt.053 to trace a payment. The interviewer should check whether the candidate can name the likely causes, such as invalid details, screening holds, nostro funding, and missed cut off times. The interviewer should check whether the candidate can set out a clear recovery plan and a fix that prevents a repeat. The interviewer should also check whether the candidate communicates clearly with the client, takes ownership, and works across operations, compliance, treasury, and technology.

Weak vs strong answers

Example 1: How do you investigate a missing payment?

Question: A client says a cross border payment has not arrived. What do you do first?

Weak answer: I would tell the client the payment is probably still on its way and they should wait a few more days. Cross border payments can be slow, and the beneficiary bank may take time to process it. I would check again next week if it still has not arrived.

Strong answer: I would ask for the payment reference, amount, value date, and the UETR if available. Then I would check the pacs.002 status report, the SWIFT gpi tracker, and the camt.053 statement to see where the payment stopped. I would check whether it was rejected, held by sanctions screening, or is with a named intermediary bank. I would tell the client what I found, what I am checking next, and when I will update them.

Why the strong answer is better: The strong answer uses the UETR, the status report, the tracker, and the statement to find the payment, instead of guessing it is slow. It gives the client facts and a clear next update. The weak answer gives no check, no timeline, and no plan to find the money.

Example 2: How do you clear a nostro break?

Question: A nostro account shows a break. How do you clear it?

Weak answer: I would ask the other bank to check their records and wait for them to reply. If they do not reply, I would leave the break open and check it again next month. Breaks happen often, so it is not urgent unless the amount is very large.

Strong answer: I would compare the camt.053 statement with our own payment record, by amount, value date, and reference, to find the unmatched entry. I would check the pacs.008, pacs.009, and MT202 messages for that day, to see whether the break is a missing payment, a duplicate posting, or an unexpected charge. I would raise a query with the correspondent bank if needed, post the correct entry, and record the cause so the same break does not happen again.

Why the strong answer is better: The strong answer finds the cause with the statement and the messages, then fixes it and records the lesson. The weak answer waits for the other bank and leaves the break open, which hides a real risk and delays the fix.

Follow-up questions

  1. You mentioned the UETR. What do you do if the payment was sent before gpi tracking was available? Hint: Use the payment reference, value date, and a SWIFT query to the next bank.
  2. How do you tell the difference between a payment held by sanctions and a payment held by fraud? Hint: Check which queue it is in and what the hold reason code says.
  3. What would you check first if a camt.053 shows a debit you did not expect? Hint: Match the reference and value date to the payment record and the charges instruction.
  4. How do you explain a cover payment to a client in plain words? Hint: The instruction goes direct, the money follows on a separate bank to bank leg.
  5. A supplier changes bank details. What checks would you ask the client to do before the next payment run? Hint: Confirm the new account with the supplier, test with a small payment, and update the beneficiary record.

Practical exercises

  1. Trace a payment. Take a sample pacs.008 and a pacs.002 status report. Write out each field that matters, what it means, and what you would check next if the payment was rejected. Then write the message you would send to the payer in plain words.
  2. Reconcile a nostro account. Take a one day camt.053 statement and a list of expected payments. Match each entry, list the breaks, and write the cause and the fix for each break. Include the query you would send to the correspondent bank for any unclear entry.
  3. Run an exception drill. Take the three currency scenario in this track. Sort 22 exceptions into rejected, held, and missing, and write the action, the owner, and the client update for each group. Then write a one page guide to stop the same failure next week.
