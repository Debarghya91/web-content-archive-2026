Preview: investigation frameworks

The three-check method. For any missing payment, run three checks in order. First, the pacs.002 status report: ACSC means settled, RJCT means it never left, ACCP or ACSP means it is still in process. Second, the value date against the cut-off: a payment sent after cut-off settles the next business day. Third, the cover leg: the debit proves only that the first leg moved. Most missing-payment cases are solved by these three checks, not by resending the payment. In an interview, walk through the three checks in order and say what each one proves. The interviewer is testing whether you investigate from evidence or from guesses.

The status-first method. For any payment problem, start from the status, not from the symptom. The symptom is what the customer reports. The status is what the system recorded. Read the status and the reason code together, then read the original instruction, then read the references. Only then decide what happened. An answer that starts with the status shows the interviewer you work from evidence. An answer that starts with a guess shows the opposite.

The evidence rule. Every claim in an investigation must point to a message, a log, or a posting. "The payment failed" is not evidence. "The pacs.002 for this UETR reports RJCT with reason code AC01" is evidence. In an interview, name the message and the field. That is what separates a BA who has investigated real payments from one who has only read about them.

Preview: interview questions

1. A customer payment is accepted (ACCP) but never reaches ACSC. How do you investigate? ACCP means accepted for processing, not settled. Check the UETR in the gpi tracker for the last recorded status and which bank holds the payment, then check whether a further pacs.002 was received and missed by looking at the hub logs. If no further status arrived, send an investigation to the next bank in the chain, and check whether the payment is stuck in a queue, held for sanctions review, or waiting for liquidity. Do not resend the payment, because that risks a duplicate.

2. What is the difference between pacs.008 and pacs.009? pacs.008 is a customer credit transfer: it moves money on behalf of a customer and carries customer data, the debtor, the creditor, the instructed amount, the charge option, and the remittance information. pacs.009 is a bank to bank credit transfer: it moves a bank's own money, with no customer behind it and no customer charge option. The test is simple: if there is a customer, it is pacs.008; if it is the bank's own money, it is pacs.009.

3. What is idempotency, and why does it matter in payment APIs? Idempotency means that processing the same request twice has the same effect as processing it once. In a payment API, the caller sends an idempotency key with the instruction, and the receiving system stores the key with the result. If the same key arrives again, the system returns the original result instead of creating a second payment. Without it, any retry after a timeout risks a duplicate debit.

4. How do you write a good requirement for an exception handling flow? Start from the failure, not from the happy path. Name the failure, name who sees it, name how long it may sit before it is escalated, and name what the customer is told at each stage. Then add the acceptance criteria: the payment must appear in the exception queue within a defined time, the queue must show the UETR and the reason, and no payment may sit in the queue without an owner.

Full question bank

5. Explain ISO 20022 to a non-technical stakeholder. ISO 20022 is a common language for financial messages. Before it, banks used MT messages, which are short text blocks with limited data, so a beneficiary address was four lines of free text and every bank read it differently. ISO 20022 replaces that with structured data, so the address, the remittance information and the purpose are separate fields a system can read and reconcile automatically. The industry moved because regulators want better data for sanctions screening, corporates want automatic reconciliation, and banks want fewer errors.

6. A pacs.008 is rejected with reason code AC01. What happened and what do you do? AC01 means incorrect account number. The beneficiary bank could not find the account stated in the pacs.008, and the payment was rejected before settlement, so no money moved. Read the pacs.002 to confirm the reason code and the original references, check the creditor account in the pacs.008 against the customer instruction, then correct it and resubmit as a new pacs.008 with a new message identification but the same UETR so tracking stays continuous.

7. What is the UETR and why is it critical? The UETR is the Unique End to End Transaction Reference, a UUID generated by the first bank in the payment chain and carried unchanged by every bank that handles the payment. It is the single key that links the pacs.008, every pacs.002 status report, any pacs.004 return, and any camt.056 cancellation. Without it, each bank sees only its own leg and the payment becomes untrackable, which is why SWIFT gpi relies on it.

8. How does a pacs.002 status flow work? When a bank sends a pacs.008, the receiving bank replies with a pacs.002. ACCP means the payment passed validation and was accepted. ACSP means it was accepted and settlement is in process. ACSC means settlement is complete. RJCT means the payment was rejected, with a reason code explaining why. Read the status and the reason code together, because the same status with a different reason code means a different fix.

9. What is the difference between a serial payment and a cover payment? In a serial payment, the instruction travels from bank to bank along the chain, and each bank passes the funds to the next. In a cover payment, the customer instruction goes directly to the beneficiary bank while the funds travel separately through a cover payment between correspondent banks. The cover method is faster for the instruction but creates two legs that must be tracked as one payment, and the cover leg is the one most often missed in an investigation.

10. What is a nostro account, and why does it matter for settlement? A nostro account is an account a bank holds with another bank, usually in a foreign currency. Settlement of a cross-border payment happens by debiting and crediting these accounts, not by moving physical money. If the sending bank has insufficient balance in its nostro account at settlement time, the payment waits, even though the message was sent hours earlier.

11. What happens when a payment misses its cut-off? The payment is not lost. It is held for the next clearing or settlement cycle, usually the next business day, and the value date moves with it. The customer must be told at entry time if the requested value date cannot be met, because the gap between the instruction date and the value date is the most common source of missing-payment complaints.

12. How do you handle a duplicate payment? First, prove it is a duplicate: same UETR, same amount, same value date, same beneficiary, two postings. Then stop any further processing of the second payment, notify the beneficiary bank, and arrange the return of the duplicate funds through a pacs.004. Then fix the cause, which is usually a retry after a timeout without an idempotency key, so the same instruction was processed twice.

13. Explain the difference between clearing and settlement. Clearing is the exchange and sorting of payment instructions: working out who owes whom, and netting payments where the scheme allows it. Settlement is the actual movement of money between accounts at the central bank or correspondent banks. A payment can be cleared and still unsettled, and the gap between the two is where most delays sit.

14. What is a pacs.004, and when is it used? A pacs.004 is a payment return. It is used when a payment was accepted and then has to come back, for example because the beneficiary account was closed, the account details were wrong but discovered after acceptance, or the beneficiary bank cannot apply the funds. The return references the original payment through the UETR, and the BA checks the return reason code before telling the customer what happened.

15. How would you test an ISO 20022 migration? Test three layers. Schema validation: does the message match the published definition. Business validation: does the receiving system accept the business data, including value dates, currencies and character sets. And end to end: does a payment travel from pain.001 through pacs.008 to pacs.002 with the right status at each step, including every rejection reason, truncation cases, and duplicate handling. A migration test plan that only covers the happy path will miss the failures that happen in production.

16. What is truncation, and why does it matter in MT to MX migration? Truncation is the loss of data when a rich ISO 20022 message has to be forwarded as an MT message to a bank that has not migrated. Structured addresses collapse into free text, remittance information is cut, and fields that exist in MX have no MT equivalent. The mapping workbook must record, field by field, what survives, what is truncated, and what is lost, and the business must accept the loss in writing.

17. What is the role of the Business Application Header, head.001? The Business Application Header travels with an ISO 20022 message and carries routing and processing information: who sent it, who should receive it, what type it is, and when it was created. It is separate from the business content of the message, which means a system can route and log a message from the header alone, without reading the payment details.

18. How do sanctions screening delays differ from rejections? A rejection ends the payment: the instruction is refused and a status is sent. A screening hold pauses it: the payment sits in a queue for manual review, no status is sent, and the customer hears nothing. A held payment looks identical to a lost payment from the outside, which is why operations must be able to see the screening queue and why the customer must be told the payment is under review.

19. What is a camt.056, and does it guarantee a payment stops? A camt.056 is a payment cancellation request. It asks the next bank to stop a payment that is already in flight. It does not guarantee the payment stops: if settlement already completed, the cancellation is rejected and the funds must be recovered through a return instead. The BA tracks whether the cancellation was accepted or rejected, and whether the funds were already settled.

20. A customer reports a payment as missing. What are your first three checks? First, the pacs.002 status: ACSC means settled, RJCT means it never left, ACCP means it is still in process. Second, the value date against the cut-off: a payment sent after cut-off settles the next business day. Third, the cover leg: in a serial or cover payment, the debit proves only that the first leg moved. Only after those three checks do you raise an investigation.

Gherkin and acceptance criteria examples

Example 1: Cut-off warning at entry. Feature: value date warning. Scenario: a customer enters a payment after the cut-off for the chosen corridor. Given the customer enters a payment instruction after the cut-off time for the selected corridor, when the customer reaches the confirmation screen, then the channel shows the expected value date and a message that the payment will settle on the next business day, and the customer must confirm before the instruction is submitted. Acceptance criteria: the warning appears for every corridor and every cut-off, the expected value date is the next business day for that corridor, and no payment can be submitted after cut-off without the warning being shown and confirmed.

Example 2: Idempotent retry. Feature: safe retry after a timeout. Scenario: a channel times out waiting for an acknowledgement. Given the channel sends a payment instruction with an idempotency key and receives no acknowledgement within the timeout, when the channel resends the instruction with the same idempotency key, then the receiving system returns the original result and does not create a second payment. Acceptance criteria: a retry with the same key returns the original result, a retry with a different key is treated as a new payment, and the duplicate check uses the UETR as well as the idempotency key.

Example 3: Exception queue ownership. Feature: exception queue. Scenario: a payment fails validation. Given a payment fails validation, when the failure is recorded, then the payment appears in the exception queue within one minute, the queue shows the UETR, the failure reason, and the time of failure, and the payment has an owner assigned. Acceptance criteria: no failed payment is invisible, no payment sits in the queue without an owner, and a payment past its expected settlement time is escalated automatically.

Example 4: Sanctions hold visibility. Feature: sanctions hold status. Scenario: a payment is held for sanctions review. Given a payment is held for manual review, when the hold is placed, then operations can see the payment in the screening queue with the reason for the hold, and the customer is told the payment is under review with a realistic time for the review to complete. Acceptance criteria: a held payment is visible to operations within an agreed time, the customer is told the payment is under review rather than left to assume it failed, and a hold without a deadline is escalated.

How to write acceptance criteria for payments. Name the failure first, then the happy path. Every criterion must be testable: a time, a count, a status, or a visible message. A criterion that says "the system handles errors gracefully" cannot be tested and will not be built. A criterion that says "a payment that fails validation appears in the exception queue within one minute with the UETR and the reason" can be tested, and will be built.

End-to-end BA case study: the missing EUR 50,000 payment

The situation. On Monday, a corporate customer in Mumbai instructs a EUR 50,000 payment to a supplier in Frankfurt. The payment is debited from the customer's account on Monday. On Wednesday the supplier says nothing arrived. The customer calls the bank and asks where the money is. The relationship manager assumes the payment failed and asks operations to send it again.

Your task as the BA. Investigate the payment, explain what happened in plain language, and write the requirements that prevent the next one. The case study below is the full answer: the investigation, the explanation, the requirements, and the test scenarios.

The investigation. Read the pacs.002 for the payment's UETR. The latest status is ACCP, accepted, with no ACSC and no RJCT. The payment is not failed and not settled. It is in process. Next, check the value date in the pain.001 against the cut-off. The instruction was entered at 16:40 on Monday, after the 15:30 cut-off for the Frankfurt corridor, so the value date is Tuesday, not Monday. The payment entered the Tuesday clearing cycle. Then check the cover leg. The payment travels as a cover payment: the instruction went directly to the beneficiary bank, and the funds travel separately through a correspondent. The cover payment is waiting for liquidity at the correspondent, which is why the beneficiary bank has the instruction but no funds. The beneficiary is right to say nothing arrived, and the customer is right to say the money left the account. Both are true.

The resolution. The payment settles on Wednesday once the correspondent funds the cover leg. The beneficiary is credited on Wednesday. No resend is needed, and a resend would have paid the supplier twice.

The requirements. Requirement 1: the channel must warn the customer at entry time when the requested value date cannot be met, showing the expected value date before the customer confirms. Requirement 2: operations must be able to see both legs of a cover payment under one UETR, so a missing cover leg is visible without a manual investigation. Requirement 3: no payment may be resent while its latest pacs.002 status is ACCP or ACSP; the resend function must be disabled in that state, and an attempt to resend must show the current status. Requirement 4: a payment that is past its expected settlement time must appear in an exception queue with an owner, and the customer must be told the realistic arrival time.

The test scenarios. Test 1: enter a payment after the cut-off and confirm the warning shows the correct expected value date for that corridor. Test 2: enter a payment before the cut-off and confirm no warning appears. Test 3: attempt to resend a payment with status ACCP and confirm the resend is blocked and the current status is shown. Test 4: attempt to resend a payment with status RJCT and confirm the resend is allowed after the reason code is corrected. Test 5: hold a payment in the sanctions queue and confirm it appears in the exception queue with an owner within one minute. Test 6: settle a cover payment late and confirm both legs are visible under one UETR in the operations view.

How to present this in an interview. Start with the three checks in order: status, value date, cover leg. State what each check proved. Then give the resolution in one sentence. Then give the requirements, not as a wish list but as testable statements with acceptance criteria. The interviewer is testing three things: whether you investigate from evidence, whether you can explain a complex failure in plain language, and whether you can turn an incident into requirements that prevent the next one.
